# Module 19: Building an NVIDIA GPU: From RTL to Rack

An H100 die is 814 mm² of TSMC 4N silicon carrying 80 billion transistors. A B200 package holds two of those-class dies plus eight HBM3E stacks on a CoWoS-L interposer, and a GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 such packages, 36 Arm CPUs, 18 NVLink switch chips, roughly 5,000 copper cables, and pulls 120–140 kW through cold plates. The problem this module solves is the one every previous module has been building toward: how does a fabless company with no fab, no packaging house, and no factories turn a few million lines of SystemVerilog into a 1.4-tonne liquid-cooled rack that sells for $3–4 million, and do it on a twelve-month product cadence?

It is hard for three reasons that compound. First, the design is at the edge of what can be verified: 80–200 billion transistors cannot be simulated at speed, so NVIDIA is the world's largest buyer of hardware emulators. Second, the die is at the edge of what a lithography scanner can print: every NVIDIA data-center GPU since A100 has been within a few percent of the 26 × 33 mm reticle limit, and Blackwell had to go to two reticle-limited dies stitched together at 10 TB/s. Third, the supply chain is at the edge of capacity: from 2023 through 2026 the binding constraints on how many GPUs shipped were CoWoS packaging and HBM, not wafers, and the whole industry's allocation politics ran through TSMC's advanced-packaging fabs. We take the four parts in order: design, manufacturing timeline, module and rack, economics.

## Part A: Design, from Architecture to Tape-Out

### What the die has to be

A data-center GPU is a hierarchy of replicated compute blocks wrapped in a memory system. On the full GH100 die (the silicon behind H100) the hierarchy is 8 **GPCs** (Graphics Processing Clusters), each holding 9 **TPCs** (Texture Processing Clusters) of 2 **SMs** (Streaming Multiprocessors), for 144 SMs on the die. Each SM contains 128 FP32 CUDA cores, 4 fourth-generation Tensor Cores (matrix-multiply units executing 8 × 4 × 16 FP16 multiply-accumulate operations per clock per Tensor Core, plus FP8 with the Transformer Engine's per-tensor scaling), a 256 KB register file, and 256 KB of combined L1 cache / shared memory. Around the GPCs sits a 50 MB **L2 cache** split into two partitions with a crossbar between them, then 12 512-bit HBM3 memory controllers (six stacks, each with two 512-bit pseudo-channel groups), 18 fourth-generation **NVLink** ports at 50 GB/s each (900 GB/s bidirectional total), a PCIe Gen5 x16 host interface, and the copy engines, video decoders, and the Multi-Instance GPU partitioning logic. Roughly speaking: the SMs are ~60% of the die area, L2 and memory PHYs ~25%, and the rest is I/O and glue.

The architectural decisions are made two to four years before silicon. The team decides the FP8/FP4 formats, the size of the L2 (which trades die area against HBM traffic), the number of NVLink ports (which sets the rack-scale topology three years later), and the **harvesting** plan: the die is built with 144 SMs and 6 HBM sites, but the shipping H100 SXM5 enables 132 SMs and 5 HBM stacks. That redundancy is what makes an 814 mm² die economically yieldable, and it is designed in from day one, with each SM and each memory partition individually fusable.

Blackwell's architectural leap was structural rather than per-SM: two dies of ~104 billion transistors each, on TSMC 4NP, joined by **NV-HBI** (NVIDIA High-Bandwidth Interface), a die-to-die link running at 10 TB/s across the CoWoS-L local silicon bridges so that the pair behaves as one 208-billion-transistor GPU with a single coherent L2 and one CUDA device. That link is the reason Blackwell needed CoWoS-L instead of CoWoS-S: a passive silicon interposer the size of two reticle dies plus eight HBM stacks (over 3× the reticle area) is beyond what a single-reticle interposer can do, so TSMC embeds small silicon bridges (LSI, Local Silicon Interconnect) in an organic RDL interposer, and the 10 TB/s of NV-HBI traffic crosses those bridges on thousands of fine-pitch wires.

### RTL: writing the chip

The design is captured in **RTL** (register-transfer level) code, almost entirely SystemVerilog, describing what every flip-flop holds at every clock edge and the combinational logic between them. NVIDIA's codebase is not 200 billion transistors of hand-written logic; it is a few million lines of parameterized RTL describing one SM, one L2 slice, one memory controller, one NVLink port, and so on, which the synthesis tools instantiate 144 times, 96 times, 12 times, 18 times. A modern GPU generation is a mix of carried-forward blocks (the video engines, PCIe controller, and display logic change little), evolved blocks (the SM datapath is revised every generation), and new blocks (the Transformer Engine's FP8 scaling in Hopper, NV-HBI and the decompression engine in Blackwell).

Alongside the RTL, the architecture team maintains a **C++ performance model** (a cycle-approximate simulator of the whole GPU) and a **functional model** that runs CUDA programs at a higher abstraction to generate reference results. The RTL is checked against both. NVIDIA has also published on using machine learning inside this loop: PrefixRL (reinforcement learning for adder circuit design, used in Hopper), AutoDMP (GPU-accelerated macro placement), and ChipNeMo, a domain-adapted LLM for engineering assistance. These are real but incremental; the flow is still overwhelmingly human engineers plus commercial EDA.

### Verification: why NVIDIA owns more emulators than anyone

Verification consumes more engineer-hours than design. The first line is **RTL simulation** on Synopsys VCS or Cadence Xcelium: constrained-random test benches written in SystemVerilog/UVM drive each block, with coverage metrics tracking which states have been exercised. Simulation of a block runs at perhaps 10–1,000 cycles per second on a CPU; simulation of a full GPU runs at a few cycles per second, which means booting a driver (billions of cycles) is impossible. **Formal verification** (Cadence JasperGold, Synopsys VC Formal) proves properties of small critical blocks (arbiters, cache coherence state machines, floating-point rounding) exhaustively.

For the full chip, the industry uses **hardware emulation**: the RTL is compiled onto a machine built from either custom processor arrays (Cadence Palladium Z2/Z3, whose processing elements execute the netlist's Boolean operations in parallel) or large FPGA arrays (Synopsys ZeBu EP1/EP2, built on AMD/Xilinx FPGAs). Emulation runs at ~1–10 MHz, a million times faster than simulation, fast enough to boot Linux and the CUDA driver, run real kernels, and run real games on a GeForce derivative, months before tape-out. A single Palladium Z3 rack holds on the order of tens of billions of gates of capacity; emulating a full Blackwell die needs many racks ganged together, and NVIDIA has for over a decade been widely reported (and cited by Cadence) as the largest Palladium customer, with data-center-scale emulation farms. The compile of a full GPU onto the emulator takes many hours to days on large CPU clusters, and every RTL change re-triggers it. The economic logic is simple: an emulator farm costs hundreds of millions of dollars; a base-layer respin of a reticle-limited N4 die costs a $20–30M mask set plus a three-month delay in a market where NVIDIA books tens of billions of dollars per quarter.

Beyond functional correctness, **power** is verified pre-silicon by replaying emulation activity traces through power-estimation tools (Synopsys PrimePower, Ansys PowerArtist), because a 700–1,400 W part with a peak-current transient that exceeds the VRM's slew capability will brown out in the lab and no amount of functional verification will have caught it.

### Physical design: from netlist to polygons

Once the RTL is frozen, the **physical design** flow turns it into the polygons on ~80 mask layers. The die is too big to handle flat, so it is partitioned hierarchically: a GPC is one physical block, designed once and instanced 8 times; an SM is a sub-block inside it; the L2 slices, memory controllers, and I/O rings are separate blocks; and a top level stitches them together. Each block goes through the following sequence, and the top level goes through it again with abstracted timing models of the blocks.

**Logic synthesis** (Synopsys Fusion Compiler or Design Compiler; Cadence Genus) maps the RTL to a gate-level netlist using the foundry's **standard-cell library**: a few thousand pre-designed, pre-characterized cells (NAND2, NOR3, flip-flops, multiplexers, adders, in multiple drive strengths and threshold voltages). The tool chooses cells to meet timing at the target clock (~1.7–2.0 GHz boost for H100 SMs) while minimizing area and power, using the library's **Liberty (.lib)** timing tables characterized at multiple process-voltage-temperature corners. Synthesis also inserts the clock-gating cells that let idle SMs stop toggling, which is where most dynamic-power savings come from.

**Floorplanning** places the big objects first: the SRAM macros (the 50 MB L2 is hundreds of individual SRAM instances from TSMC's memory compiler; the register files and L1s inside each SM are more), the HBM PHYs along the two long edges of the die facing the HBM stacks, NVLink and PCIe PHYs along the other edges, and the power grid. The power delivery network on a 700 W die is itself a major design object: the chip draws ~700–1,000 A at ~0.7–0.9 V, so the top two or three thick metal layers are dense meshes of Vdd and Vss stripes tied to thousands of C4 bumps, and the placement of every block is constrained by how much current can be brought down through the stack without exceeding the **IR-drop** budget (typically ~5–10% of Vdd from bump to transistor).

**Placement** (Cadence Innovus, Synopsys IC Compiler II / Fusion Compiler) assigns each of the tens of millions of cells in a block to a legal site in the standard-cell rows, driven by timing (critical paths short), congestion (routing demand below track supply), and power. **Clock tree synthesis** then builds the network that distributes the clock to every flip-flop with controlled skew; GPUs typically use a clock mesh or a hybrid H-tree plus mesh for the high-fanout core clock, accepting several watts of clock-distribution power to keep skew under ~10–20 ps across a block. **Routing** connects the ~100 million nets in the die across ~15–18 metal layers: global routing assigns nets to regions, track assignment to metal tracks, detailed routing to exact geometries obeying the design rules, and on the lower layers (M0–M3 at 4N, with ~28–30 nm minimum pitch on M0) the router must also respect the multi-patterning or EUV single-exposure constraints of each layer.

**Static timing analysis** (Synopsys PrimeTime is the signoff standard; Cadence Tempus) then checks every path against setup and hold constraints across all corners and modes, with **on-chip variation** margins (AOCV/POCV statistical derates) for the fact that two nominally identical gates a millimeter apart differ by a few percent in delay. A full-chip GPU signoff involves hundreds of corner-mode combinations and billions of paths. Parasitic extraction (Synopsys StarRC, Cadence Quantus) supplies the wire RC values from the routed geometry. **Timing closure** is the loop of finding violating paths, fixing them with cell upsizing, buffering, re-placement, or, if necessary, RTL changes, and re-running; it consumes weeks per block and is the single largest schedule risk in physical design.

**Power integrity signoff** (Ansys RedHawk-SC is the standard; Cadence Voltus) simulates the current draw of the placed-and-routed design under realistic activity vectors (again from emulation) and computes IR drop and electromigration across the full grid; hot spots force grid re-work or decoupling-capacitor insertion. **Signal integrity** (crosstalk-induced delay and glitches) is analyzed inside STA. **Thermal** analysis is done at the package level with the die's power map.

**Physical verification** is the last gate. **DRC** (design rule checking) verifies every polygon against the thousands of geometric rules in the PDK: minimum widths, spacings, enclosures, densities, antenna ratios, and the pattern-matching "forbidden pitch" rules that came with 193i multi-patterning and EUV. **LVS** (layout versus schematic) extracts the transistors and wires from the polygons and proves the layout matches the netlist. The near-universal signoff tool is Siemens Calibre (nmDRC, nmLVS, and the DFM fill and litho-hotspot checks); Synopsys IC Validator and Cadence Pegasus compete but TSMC's reference flows are Calibre-first. A full-chip DRC run on an 800 mm² N4 die takes many hours on hundreds of CPU cores and typically returns tens of thousands of violations in the first pass, most of them at block boundaries.

**DFT insertion** happens in parallel with synthesis and placement. Every flip-flop is made part of a **scan chain** so that test patterns can be shifted in and captured out through a few hundred pins; the patterns come from ATPG (Synopsys TestMAX, Siemens Tessent) targeting stuck-at and transition faults with compression ratios of ~100:1 so that test time at sort stays in seconds. Every SRAM instance gets **MBIST** (memory built-in self-test) logic that exercises it with March algorithms at speed. The HBM PHYs, NVLink and PCIe SerDes get loopback and eye-monitoring test modes. A JTAG (IEEE 1149.1) and IJTAG (IEEE 1687) network ties all of this together, and it is the same network that bring-up engineers will use to debug first silicon. The e-fuse block that records which SMs and HBM controllers are disabled at sort is also part of this infrastructure (see Module 14).

### The PDK, the libraries, and "4N"

None of this works without the **PDK** (process design kit) from TSMC: the DRC/LVS rule decks, the SPICE transistor models, the parasitic extraction technology files, the layer map, and the DFM rules. On top of the PDK sit the **standard-cell libraries** and **memory compilers**; TSMC supplies its own, and Arm, Synopsys, and Cadence sell alternatives, but NVIDIA is known to build custom cells for its most critical datapaths (its published NVCell work uses reinforcement learning and simulated annealing to lay out standard cells automatically for new nodes).

"TSMC 4N" is not a public TSMC node; it is a customized variant of TSMC's N5 family (N4-class) tuned for NVIDIA, with its own rule deck and library characterization. **4NP** (Blackwell) is a further performance-tuned derivative, and **3N** (Rubin) is the equivalent on the N3 family (NVIDIA says only "TSMC 3 nm"; trade reports put Rubin on N3P). What "custom" actually means is not disclosed; publicly, TSMC's foundry model allows large customers to negotiate design rules, metal-stack options (thicker top metals for power delivery, more layers), and library cells characterized to the customer's target voltage and temperature. It does not mean a different transistor. The name also has a commercial function: it signals that NVIDIA's wafers are on a dedicated flow that TSMC has committed capacity to.

### IP: the blocks NVIDIA does not design from scratch

A GPU of this class contains a large amount of licensed or foundry-provided **IP**: the HBM3/HBM3E/HBM4 PHY and controller (Rambus, Synopsys, and Cadence sell these; NVIDIA is widely believed to use in-house PHYs on its data-center parts, but has not said so), the PCIe Gen5/Gen6 controller and PHY (Synopsys is the dominant supplier industry-wide), the NVLink SerDes (in-house, since it is NVIDIA's proprietary protocol; the 200 Gb/s-per-lane PAM4 SerDes in NVLink 5 is at the edge of what has been done on N4), the Arm Neoverse cores in the Grace and Vera CPUs (Arm-licensed, with NVIDIA's own custom "Olympus" cores in Vera), and the video codecs, thermal sensors, PLLs, and e-fuse macros. Each IP block ships as a hardened GDS layout plus timing and physical abstracts and is verified on a **test chip** run on a TSMC **MPW shuttle** (multi-project wafer, in TSMC's CyberShuttle program) a year or more before the GPU tapes out, because a SerDes that does not meet its eye margin on real silicon is not something you discover on the production die.

### Tape-out: from polygons to masks

**Tape-out** is the moment the final layout database is sent to the foundry. The native format is GDSII (a 1970s binary stream format) or, for anything this size, **OASIS**, which is roughly 5–10× more compact. A hierarchical N4 full-chip layout for an 800 mm² die runs from tens to a few hundred gigabytes in OASIS, and it grows enormously through the next step.

**Mask data preparation** (MDP) at TSMC turns the design layers into mask layers. First the Boolean layer operations and sizing that convert the drawn layers into the physical shapes to be printed. Then **OPC** (optical proximity correction), which pre-distorts every edge so that after the diffraction, resist blur, and etch bias of the real process the printed pattern matches the intent; for the most critical layers this is **ILT** (inverse lithography technology), which solves for the mask pattern as a free-form optimization rather than by moving edge segments, producing curvilinear masks with sub-resolution assist features. ILT on a full chip is one of the largest compute jobs in the industry: TSMC's computational lithography ran on tens of thousands of CPU cores, and this is why NVIDIA built **cuLitho**, a GPU-accelerated library for OPC/ILT that TSMC and Synopsys brought into production in 2024, with NVIDIA claiming that 350 H100 systems replace 40,000 CPU servers and deliver ~40× speedups, plus a further ~2× from a generative-AI initialization step. After OPC the data is fractured into the mask writer's format (trapezoids or, for multi-beam writers, pixel maps), checked by mask rule check, and sent to the mask shop. Post-OPC data for a full mask set runs into terabytes.

**Mask making** (TSMC has its own mask shops; Photronics, Toppan, and DNP serve the merchant market) writes each layer onto a 6-inch quartz blank. For DUV layers the blank is chrome-on-quartz (or MoSi attenuated phase-shift), written on a multi-beam e-beam writer (IMS Nanofabrication MBMW; IMS is majority-owned by Intel, with Bain Capital and TSMC holding minority stakes since 2023) or a laser writer. For **EUV** layers the blank is a low-thermal-expansion glass substrate carrying a 40-bilayer Mo/Si multilayer mirror with a tantalum-based absorber (blanks from Hoya and AGC), written on a multi-beam writer over ~10–20 hours, inspected with actinic (13.5 nm) tools (Lasertec ACTIS) and e-beam, repaired at the atom scale by Zeiss MeRiT electron-beam tools, and fitted with a pellicle. At N4 a full set is roughly 80 or more masks, of which ~12–15 are EUV. An EUV mask costs on the order of $300–500k, a critical DUV mask $50–100k, so the set is ~$20–30 million, and it is only usable for that design revision. This is the sense in which a "respin" costs tens of millions before a single wafer moves.

A test chip goes on a shuttle; a GPU gets a **full run**: its own dedicated lot (typically 25 wafers), with the full mask set, on the production flow. First silicon lots are run as "hot lots" or "super-hot lots" that jump every queue, cutting the ~100–120 day cycle time to ~60–75 days. Add a few weeks for CoWoS (first silicon for a CoWoS part must be packaged with real HBM before it can even be probed properly) and it is roughly three months from tape-out to the first packaged part on a lab bench.

### First silicon, bring-up, and respins

Bring-up begins with the JTAG network: can the chip be seen, do the PLLs lock, can the on-die sensors be read. Then power: the die is stepped through voltage and frequency ("shmoo" plots) on each rail to find the operating envelope. Then memory: the HBM PHYs are trained, and a failed HBM channel here is one of the more common first-silicon problems. Then the firmware boots, the driver loads, and CUDA kernels run; then months of characterization across thousands of parts to set the production voltage/frequency tables, binning rules, and the harvesting configuration. Bugs found at this stage go into an **errata** list and are triaged: fixable in firmware or the driver (most), fixable with a **metal-only respin** (change only the interconnect layers, re-using front-end wafers held at the contact layer, costing a few million dollars in masks and ~6–8 weeks), or requiring a **base-layer respin** (full new mask set, full cycle time, ~3–4 months). Steppings are named by letter and number: A0 is first silicon, A1 a metal respin, B0 a base-layer respin. NVIDIA does not publish its stepping history, but the Blackwell program made one public in 2024: a yield-limiting design flaw found in mid-2024 forced a mask change (reported to involve the top metal layers and bumps, i.e. a metal-level respin) that pushed B200 volume by roughly a quarter, and in October 2024 Jensen Huang confirmed it was a "design flaw", "100% NVIDIA's fault", fixed with TSMC's help. That single event cost on the order of one production quarter of the most valuable product line in the industry, which is the cleanest possible illustration of why the verification budget is what it is.

So the **cycle time** (time a given unit of material spends moving through the chain) from wafer start to a GPU shipping on a board is roughly six months, and to a burned-in rack, seven or more. The **lead time** a customer experiences is longer, because the chain has to be primed: HBM DRAM wafers start two to three months before the logic wafer arrives at CoWoS, substrates are ordered 8–12 weeks out, and NVIDIA must commit to TSMC wafer and CoWoS capacity a year or more ahead. In 2023 the widely quoted H100 lead time reached ~52 weeks; NVIDIA's executives on the mid-2023 earnings calls described "significantly long" supply cycle times and said they had already placed purchase commitments for the following year, which is what a ~12-month planning horizon looks like from the inside. The distinction matters for reading the industry: a 52-week lead time was mostly queueing for CoWoS and HBM allocation, not 52 weeks of processing.

> **Worked example: dies per wafer and cost per good die for H100.**
> GH100 is 814 mm²; treat it as ~26 × 31.3 mm to fit the 26 × 33 mm reticle. The standard gross-die estimate for a 300 mm wafer (usable diameter ~297 mm after edge exclusion, d = 297) is
> N = π(d/2)² / A − π d / √(2A) = (69,300 / 814) − (933 / 40.3) = 85.1 − 23.1 ≈ 62 gross die per wafer.
> Real placements with scribe lanes give ~60–65, matching what has been reported. Now yield. At a mature N4 defect density of D0 ≈ 0.1 per cm², the Poisson model gives Y = exp(−A·D0) = exp(−8.14 × 0.1) = 0.44, and Murphy's model ((1 − e^(−AD))/AD)² gives 0.47. That would be ~28 perfect die per wafer, which is why nobody ships a perfect GH100. With 12 of 144 SMs and 1 of 6 HBM controllers held in reserve, a die with a defect in an SM or in one memory partition is still sellable, and the effective yield of *sellable* die rises to roughly 65–80%, or ~40–50 die per wafer. At a 4N wafer price on the order of $16,000–17,000, that is $16,500 / 45 ≈ $370 per sellable die, consistent with the ~$300–400 range that appears in most public BOM estimates. The lesson is that harvesting, not defect density, is what makes reticle-limited dies economic.

## Part C: From Package to Rack

### The SXM module

The packaged GPU does not plug into anything directly. It is reflowed onto a small, extremely dense PCB, the **SXM** module (SXM4 for A100, SXM5 for H100/H200, and a Blackwell-generation module for the HGX B200/B300), which is what actually sits in a server. The module carries three things besides the package.

The **VRM** (voltage regulator module) converts the 12 V (or 48 V on newer boards) that the baseboard supplies into the ~0.7–0.9 V core rail plus separate rails for HBM, PHYs, and I/O, delivering 700 W on H100 and ~1,000–1,400 W on Blackwell parts. At 0.8 V, 1,000 W is 1,250 A, so the VRM is a multiphase buck converter with on the order of 16–30 phases, each a **power stage** (an integrated pair of MOSFETs with driver, from Monolithic Power Systems, which was NVIDIA's primary supplier through the Hopper generation, plus Infineon, Renesas, and onsemi on later boards), an inductor per phase (Vishay, Cyntec, Delta), and a digital controller. The power stages sit as close to the package as possible because every millimeter of copper between the VRM and the C4 bumps is resistance and inductance the core-voltage transient response has to fight. Around the package are hundreds of **MLCCs** (multilayer ceramic capacitors, from Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, TDK, and Taiyo Yuden; the highest-value ones are in the substrate cavity under the die) that supply the current for the nanoseconds between a load step and the VRM's response. Getting this right is a significant part of why an SXM board is a 1,000-plus-component object.

The **mezzanine connector** connects the module to the baseboard: on SXM5 this is a pair of high-density connectors carrying the 18 NVLink ports, PCIe x16, power, and management signals (Amphenol and TE Connectivity supply this class of connector). NVLink at 50–100 Gb/s per lane through a connector is a signal-integrity problem in its own right; the channel budget is why the switch chips are on the same baseboard rather than on a separate card.

The **thermal solution** for an air-cooled HGX H100 is a large vapor-chamber heatsink (a sealed copper chamber with a wick and a working fluid, spreading the die's ~700 W over a fin stack that the server's fans drive air through at high static pressure; Auras, AVC, Cooler Master, and Delta supply these), mated to the lid with a thermal interface material and a spring-loaded retention frame that applies a controlled load. For liquid-cooled Blackwell and the NVL72, the heatsink is replaced by a **cold plate**: a copper block with microchannels that water at ~25–45 °C inlet flows through, removing ~1,000–1,400 W from a surface a few square centimeters across; CoolIT, Boyd, Asetek, Auras, and Delta are the main suppliers, and the plate's flatness and the TIM under it determine whether the GPU throttles.

### The HGX baseboard

Eight SXM modules mount on one **HGX** baseboard, along with the NVSwitch chips that connect them. On HGX H100, four third-generation NVSwitch chips, each with 64 NVLink ports at 50 GB/s, give every GPU its full 900 GB/s to every other GPU without any host involvement. On HGX B200/B300, two fifth-generation NVLink switch chips (72 ports of 100 GB/s each, 7.2 TB/s per chip) supply each of the eight GPUs with 1.8 TB/s. The baseboard is a large (~ 60 × 40 cm), heavy PCB with on the order of 20–30+ layers because it carries 8 × 18 NVLink ports, 8 × PCIe Gen5 x16, and a power plane that delivers 6–10 kW. Every high-speed trace runs on low-loss laminate: Panasonic Megtron 6 or Megtron 7 (a polyphenylene-ether resin with a dielectric loss tangent of ~0.002–0.005 at 10 GHz, versus ~0.02 for FR-4), with EMC and Isola equivalents, on copper foil chosen for low surface roughness to limit skin-effect loss. The fabrication is done by Unimicron, Ibiden, WUS Printed Circuit, Victory Giant Technology, Gold Circuit Electronics, and TTM, and the assembly (placing ~10,000-plus components, including the GPUs themselves via mezzanine connectors after board-level test) is done by the ODMs. HGX baseboards are sold by NVIDIA as a unit, to be integrated into a chassis by the OEM; the reported list price of an HGX H100 baseboard was ~$200–300k in 2023.

### The server: DGX and its OEM cousins

Around the baseboard, a complete 8-GPU server adds: the **CPU tray** (two x86 CPUs; Intel Xeon Platinum 8480C on DGX H100, Xeon 8570 on DGX B200, or AMD EPYC on many OEM designs) with 2 TB of DDR5 and NVMe storage; **PCIe switches** (Broadcom PEX-series Gen5 switches) that fan the CPUs' PCIe lanes out to the GPUs and NICs; eight **ConnectX-7** (400 Gb/s) or **ConnectX-8** (800 Gb/s) NICs, one per GPU, so that each GPU has a dedicated path to the scale-out network; two **BlueField-3** DPUs for storage and management traffic; and a power supply set of 6 × 3.3 kW or so. DGX H100 is rated at 10.2 kW maximum; DGX B200 at 14.3 kW. NVIDIA sells DGX as its own branded server at roughly $300–400k for H100 (list) and higher for B200; Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro sell functionally similar HGX-based servers at their own margins; and hyperscalers buy HGX baseboards for their own chassis designs.

### GB200: the superchip and the compute tray

Blackwell changed the server design. Instead of x86 CPUs on a separate tray, **GB200** puts one **Grace** CPU (72 Arm Neoverse V2 cores, 480 GB of LPDDR5X soldered beside it at ~500 GB/s) and two B200 GPUs on one board, the "Bianca" superchip board, connected by **NVLink-C2C** at 900 GB/s so the GPUs can use the CPU's memory as a slow extra tier. A superchip draws on the order of 2,700 W. Two superchips (2 Grace, 4 B200) share one 1U liquid-cooled **compute tray**, together with four ConnectX-7/-8 NICs, a BlueField-3, and the cold plates; the tray is built to NVIDIA's MGX reference design and is physically a slab of copper cold plates and PCB with quick-disconnect fittings on the back.

### The NVL72 rack

The **GB200 NVL72** is the unit NVIDIA now sells as "one GPU". In one rack (an OCP ORv3-derived design ~2.2 m tall, ~1.36 tonnes fully loaded): 18 compute trays (72 B200 GPUs, 36 Grace CPUs, ~13.4 TB of HBM3E and ~17 TB of LPDDR5X); 9 **NVLink switch trays**, each with two NVLink 5 switch chips (18 chips, 129.6 TB/s of switching); 6 to 8 power shelves at ~33 kW each feeding a DC busbar; and the **NVLink spine**, a backplane of roughly 5,000 passive copper cables totaling about 2 miles that connects every GPU's 18 NVLink ports to the switch trays. All 72 GPUs form one NVLink domain with 130 TB/s of aggregate bandwidth, which is the point of the design: a 72-GPU all-to-all fabric with no optics.

Copper is why the rack is the shape it is. At 200 Gb/s per lane, passive copper reaches about 1–1.5 m; that is enough to reach from any compute tray to the switch trays in the middle of a single rack, but not to a second rack. NVIDIA has said the copper spine saves ~20 kW per rack versus an optical equivalent (each optical transceiver burns ~15–20 W and the 72 GPUs would need thousands of them) and a large fraction of the cost. The consequence is that NVL72 is power-dense: ~120–132 kW per rack for GB200, ~135–142 kW nominal for **GB300 NVL72** (Blackwell Ultra, 288 GB per GPU, ~20 TB HBM per rack, ~1,400 W per GPU), against a conventional data-center rack of 10–20 kW. There is no way to move that with air.

**Direct liquid cooling** therefore is not optional. Each GPU, CPU, and switch chip sits under a cold plate. Inside the tray, rubber-free hoses run to a rear manifold; the tray connects to the rack manifold through **quick disconnects** (Stäubli, CPC, Parker) that seal drip-free when a tray is pulled. The rack manifold runs to a **CDU** (coolant distribution unit, from Vertiv, Motivair (now Schneider Electric), CoolIT, Boyd, and Delta), which is a heat exchanger and pump set that isolates the clean secondary loop (treated water with a corrosion inhibitor, ~25–45 °C supply) from the facility water loop. In-row CDUs handle ~1–2 MW; a "sidecar" CDU per rack is also used. About 80–90% of the rack's heat leaves through the liquid; the rest (NICs, DIMMs, power shelves) still goes to air. A 100 MW building of NVL72s moves ~80–90 MW through water, which is a plumbing project as much as an electrical one.

Beyond GB300, NVIDIA's roadmap goes to the **Vera Rubin NVL72** (announced at GTC 2025 as "NVL144", counting the 144 Rubin dies, and renamed at CES 2026 to count the 72 packages, each with two Rubin dies, plus 36 Vera CPUs; second half of 2026; NVLink 6 at 3.6 TB/s per GPU, ~20.7 TB of HBM4 per rack; power reported at ~190–230 kW per rack) and then the **Kyber** rack for Rubin Ultra ("NVL576" at GTC 2025 counting dies, "Kyber NVL144" in 2026 materials counting packages) in the second half of 2027: 144 packages of four reticle dies each, compute blades rotated vertical in four 18-blade pods, ~600 kW per rack, on an 800 V DC power architecture that NVIDIA announced in 2025 to make 1 MW-class racks feasible. All of this is roadmap as of the 2025–2026 announcements, and in July 2026 SemiAnalysis reported the Kyber rack slipping to 2028 over midplane-PCB manufacturability, with a stopgap back-to-back NVL72x2 configuration dropped after customer pushback.

### Networking beyond the rack

Inside the rack, NVLink. Between racks, the **scale-out network**: either **InfiniBand** (NVIDIA Quantum-2 at 400 Gb/s or Quantum-X800 at 800 Gb/s, inherited from Mellanox) or Ethernet with NVIDIA's **Spectrum-X** (Spectrum-4 51.2 Tb/s switches with congestion control and adaptive routing tuned for AI traffic). Each GPU's ConnectX NIC connects to a leaf switch; a 100,000-GPU cluster uses a three-tier fat-tree with tens of thousands of switch ports, and every link over a few meters is optical.

The **optical transceivers** are their own supply chain. An 800G OSFP module holds 8 lanes of 100 Gb/s (or 4 × 200 Gb/s for 1.6T), each driven by an **EML** (electro-absorption modulated laser, an indium-phosphide chip from Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Mitsubishi Electric, or Sumitomo) or, increasingly, by a silicon-photonics modulator with an external continuous-wave InP laser, plus a DSP (Marvell, Broadcom) that recovers the PAM4 signal. The modules are assembled by Innolight, Eoptolink, and Coherent (the three leaders), plus Accelink, Hisense, and others, largely in China and Thailand. A single NVL72-scale cluster of 100,000 GPUs consumes on the order of hundreds of thousands of 800G transceivers, and the 1.6T generation ramped alongside ConnectX-8 and Quantum-X800 in 2025–26. NVIDIA's answer to the transceiver power and cost is **co-packaged optics**: the Quantum-X and Spectrum-X Photonics switches announced at GTC 2025 put silicon-photonics engines (built with TSMC's COUPE process) on the switch package, replacing pluggable modules on the switch side, with Quantum-X Photonics slated for late 2025 and Spectrum-X Photonics for 2026 (as announced at GTC 2025). GPUs themselves still use copper to the NIC.

### Who builds it

NVIDIA designs the GPU, the switch chips, the NICs, the SXM module, the HGX baseboard, the Bianca board, the compute and switch trays, and the rack, and publishes all of them as reference designs (it contributed the NVL72 designs to the Open Compute Project in 2024). It manufactures none of them. The board-level and system-level builders are the Taiwanese **ODMs**: **Foxconn** (Hon Hai, including its Ingrasys server unit, the largest builder of NVL72 racks), **Quanta** (QCT), **Wistron** and its spinoff **Wiwynn**, **Inventec**, and **Pegatron**. **Supermicro** is a hybrid, designing and building its own chassis around HGX and MGX. **Dell**, **HPE**, and **Lenovo** are OEMs that sell to enterprises under their own brands, sourcing boards from NVIDIA and manufacturing from the ODMs. ZT Systems, a major hyperscaler ODM, was bought by AMD in 2024, with its manufacturing arm sold to Sanmina in 2025.

Geography shifted fast. Through 2023, most of this was assembled in Taiwan. By 2024–25, Foxconn's Guadalajara plant in Mexico became the largest GB200 assembly site, and in April 2025 NVIDIA announced that Foxconn (Houston) and Wistron (Dallas) would build Blackwell and Rubin systems in Texas, with TSMC Arizona wafers, Amkor and SPIL packaging in Arizona, as part of a stated plan for up to $500 billion of US-built AI infrastructure over four years. As of 2026, the split is roughly Taiwan for boards and early production, Mexico and the US for high-volume rack integration for US hyperscalers, with tariff policy as the variable.

## Part D: The Economics

### The bill of materials of an H100

Public estimates of the H100 SXM's manufacturing cost (SemiAnalysis and others, 2023–24) cluster around $3,000–3,500. The approximate decomposition:

| Item | Cost (2023–24 estimate) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| GPU die | ~$300–400 | ~$16–17k N4 wafer, ~45 sellable die |
| HBM3, 5 stacks, 80 GB | ~$1,300–2,000 | ~$15–25/GB in 2023–24; HBM was the scarcest input |
| CoWoS-S interposer, assembly, ABF substrate | ~$500–1,000 | interposer ~ 2.5 reticles; substrate ~$100–200 |
| Wafer sort, final test, burn-in, SLT | ~$100–200 | test time on V93000 class ATE |
| SXM board, VRM, passives, heatsink | ~$200–400 | |
| Total COGS | ~$3,000–3,500 | vs. ~$25,000–30,000 selling price |

Note what this says: the 814 mm² die that consumes the entire front half of this course is ~10% of the cost of the product; memory is ~40–50%; packaging and test ~20–25%. For B200 the same structure at roughly double: two dies (~$800–1,000), eight HBM3E stacks (192 GB at ~$15–20/GB is ~$3,000–3,500), CoWoS-L (~$1,500–2,000, since a three-reticle-plus interposer with embedded bridges yields worse and TSMC charges more), and a larger substrate, for a COGS on the order of $6,000–8,000 against a ~$30,000–40,000 price. A full GB200 superchip has been estimated at ~$14,000 COGS. The rack: an NVL72 at ~$3–4 million contains ~$500–600k of GPU COGS at these figures, and the rest of the price is NVIDIA's margin on the GPUs plus the CPUs, switches, NICs, boards, cold plates, and ODM assembly at their own margins.

### NVIDIA's margin, TSMC's revenue, and the allocation game

NVIDIA's company-level gross margin ran ~75% in fiscal 2025 and ~70–75% through the Blackwell ramp (CoWoS-L and HBM3E are more expensive per package than the Hopper generation, and early Blackwell yields were lower). On a per-H100 basis the estimates above imply ~85–90% gross margin on the chip, diluted by DGX systems, networking, and software. TSMC's revenue from NVIDIA rose from a few percent in 2022 to 12% in 2024 and 19% in 2025 (per TSMC's annual-report customer disclosures as reported in January 2026), overtaking Apple (17%) as TSMC's largest customer. Even so, TSMC captures on the order of $2,000–3,000 of an $8,000 B200 COGS (wafers plus CoWoS), while NVIDIA captures ~$25,000 of margin. This asymmetry is why the foundry model's rents flow to the design company when the product is scarce and to the foundry when it is not.

The **allocation politics** of 2023–26 were fought over two inputs. **CoWoS**: TSMC's capacity was ~10–15k wafers per month in 2023, ~35k at end-2024, ~75–80k at end-2025, with a target of ~120–130k for end-2026, and NVIDIA held roughly half or more of it throughout, with AMD, Broadcom, and the hyperscalers' custom ASICs (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium) competing for the rest. A B200-class package needs ~3.3 reticles of interposer, so a CoWoS wafer yields only ~16 packages; 1 million B200s is ~60,000 CoWoS wafers, roughly two months of end-2024 capacity or ~15–20% of a full 2024 year. NVIDIA prepaid, took capacity commitments, and pushed TSMC to qualify ASE/SPIL and Amkor for the wafer-on-substrate step to free CoW capacity at TSMC. **HBM**: SK hynix supplied essentially all H100 HBM3 in 2023 and the majority of HBM3E for Blackwell; Micron qualified HBM3E in 2024 and reached ~20%+ share by 2025–26; Samsung's HBM3E qualification at NVIDIA lagged until September 2025, when its 12-high HBM3E finally passed. HBM is ~5% of DRAM bits but ~30–40% of DRAM revenue, and every HBM wafer displaces ~3 wafers of conventional DRAM bits, which is why DRAM prices for everyone else rose in 2025–26.

### The supplier tree

Reading upward from the rack, the dependencies are:

- **Logic wafers:** TSMC only (Fab 18 Tainan Phases 1–4 for 4N/4NP; Fab 18 Phases 5–8 for N3; Fab 20 Hsinchu and Fab 22 Kaohsiung are N2, not N3; TSMC Arizona Fab 21 for N4 from 2025). No second source exists for the GPU die; Samsung built earlier NVIDIA consumer parts (Ampere GeForce on 8LPP) but not the data-center line.
- **HBM:** SK hynix (leader), Micron, Samsung.
- **Advanced packaging:** TSMC CoWoS (nearly all); ASE/SPIL and Amkor for wafer-on-substrate and overflow; Amkor Arizona (Peoria) from 2028.
- **Substrates:** Ibiden, Unimicron, with Shinko, AT&S, Kinsus; ABF film from Ajinomoto; glass cloth from Nittobo; copper foil from Mitsui.
- **Test:** Advantest (V93000 EXA) testers, FormFactor probe cards, KYEC and ASE test services.
- **EDA and IP:** Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens (Calibre, Tessent); Ansys; Arm.
- **Masks and lithography compute:** TSMC in-house; IMS writers; Hoya/AGC blanks; NVIDIA's own cuLitho.
- **Module and board:** MPS, Infineon, Renesas, onsemi (power stages); Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, TDK (MLCCs); Amphenol, TE (connectors); Panasonic, EMC, Isola (laminates); Unimicron, Ibiden, WUS, Victory Giant, GCE, TTM (PCBs); Auras, AVC, Delta, CoolIT, Boyd (thermal).
- **Rack:** Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron/Wiwynn, Inventec, Supermicro (build); Dell, HPE, Lenovo (OEM); Vertiv, Schneider (Motivair), CoolIT, Delta (CDUs and power); Stäubli, CPC, Parker (quick disconnects); Amphenol, Luxshare, Molex (NVLink spine cables).
- **Network:** NVIDIA (switches, NICs); Innolight, Eoptolink, Coherent (transceivers); Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Sumitomo (InP lasers); Marvell, Broadcom (DSPs); Broadcom (PCIe switches); Corning (fiber).
- **CPU:** Arm (IP), Intel and AMD (x86 for HGX); Grace and Vera built at TSMC.

Every one of these is one to three companies deep. The ones with genuinely no substitute on a two-year horizon are TSMC (wafers and CoWoS), the three HBM makers as a group, Ajinomoto (ABF), and ASML upstream of all of them.

> **Worked example: wafers and HBM for one million B200 GPUs.**
> Logic: 1 million packages × 2 dies = 2 million dies at ~800 mm² each. From the die-per-wafer formula, ~63 gross die per 300 mm wafer; with harvesting, ~70% sellable, so ~44 good die per wafer, and 2,000,000 / 44 ≈ 45,000 N4P wafers. TSMC's N5-family capacity is on the order of 150,000 wafers per month, so a year's worth of the most valuable product on earth is about ten days of fab output. The wafer fab is not the constraint.
> HBM: 8 stacks per GPU = 8 million HBM3E stacks. Each 24 GB 8-high stack is eight 24 Gb (3 GB) DRAM dies plus a base die, so 64 million DRAM dies. A 24 Gb HBM3E die on a 1β-class node is reportedly ~110–120 mm²; at ~500 gross die per wafer and ~70% net yield (HBM DRAM dies are also tested and thinned, with TSV yield loss on top), call it ~350 good die per wafer, so ~185,000 DRAM wafers, plus base-die wafers and stacking loss. That is roughly 15,000 wafers per month for this one SKU, about 1% of the ~1.5 million-plus DRAM wafers per month the three vendors start (~2 million industry-wide). Add H200, B300, AMD and the custom-ASIC customers, and at ~3× the wafer area per bit of commodity DRAM, HBM as a whole reportedly took ~30% of SK hynix's DRAM wafer starts by 2025, which is why HBM crowded out commodity DRAM in 2025–26.
> CoWoS-L: at ~16 packages per interposer wafer and ~90% assembly yield, 1,000,000 / 14.4 ≈ 70,000 CoWoS wafers, or about 6,000 per month. Against ~35,000 wafers per month of total CoWoS capacity at end-2024, one million B200s would have been ~17% of a year's global advanced-packaging capacity for a single SKU. That, and not the fab, is the bottleneck.

> **Worked example: NVL72 racks per 100 MW.**
> A GB200 NVL72 rack draws ~120–132 kW at full load; take 130 kW. A data center with 100 MW of IT power (excluding cooling and distribution losses, i.e., before PUE) holds 100,000 / 130 ≈ 770 racks, or ~55,000 GPUs and ~28,000 Grace CPUs. If the 100 MW is the utility feed rather than IT power, at a PUE of ~1.2 for a liquid-cooled facility, the IT budget is ~83 MW and the count falls to ~640 racks, ~46,000 GPUs. At ~$3.5 million per rack that is ~$2.2–2.7 billion of racks alone before the network, storage, building, and power infrastructure, which at ~$30–50 million per MW all-in adds another $3–5 billion. Move to GB300 NVL72 at ~140 kW and the GPU count per 100 MW falls ~7% while the FP4 throughput per rack rises ~50%; move to the roadmap Kyber rack at 600 kW and the same 100 MW holds ~140–165 racks of 576 Rubin Ultra dies each. The power per rack is rising far faster than the power per site, which is why 2026-era data-center design is a story of 800 V DC distribution and liquid cooling rather than of more floor space.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"NVIDIA makes chips."** → NVIDIA designs chips and systems and owns the architecture, RTL, software, and reference designs; every physical object, from the wafer to the rack, is made by TSMC, the HBM vendors, the OSATs, the substrate makers, and the ODMs. NVIDIA's only "factory" is its emulation and compute farm.
- **"The 80-billion-transistor design is hand-built."** → It is a few million lines of parameterized RTL describing one SM, one L2 slice, one PHY, instantiated by the tools hundreds of times and then placed and routed hierarchically by Synopsys and Cadence software. What is hand-tuned is the datapath, the custom cells, and the clock and power grids.
- **"Yield on an 814 mm² die must be terrible, so the die is the expensive part."** → Perfect-die yield is indeed under 50%, but 12 spare SMs and a spare HBM controller make ~70–80% of dies sellable; the die ends up at ~$350, about 10% of a ~$3,300 BOM, with HBM at ~40–50%.
- **"A 52-week lead time means 52 weeks of manufacturing."** → Physical cycle time from wafer start to a GPU on a board is ~6 months. The rest of a 2023 lead time was queueing for CoWoS and HBM allocation and the ~12-month capacity commitments the chain requires.
- **"NVL72 uses optics because it is so fast."** → It is the opposite: the 130 TB/s NVLink domain is all passive copper (~5,000 cables) precisely to avoid the ~20 kW and cost of thousands of transceivers, and the ~1.5 m reach of 200 Gb/s copper is what confines the domain to one rack. Optics begin at the scale-out NIC.
- **"Liquid cooling is a choice for efficiency."** → At 120–140 kW per rack (and 600 kW on the Kyber roadmap) there is no air-cooling design that works; direct-to-chip cold plates and CDUs are a requirement, not an optimization.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module consumes everything the previous eighteen produced: the polished wafers of Module 03 patterned through Modules 05–12 with the masks of Module 04, sorted and harvested in Modules 13–14, joined to the HBM stacks of Module 15 on the CoWoS interposers and ABF substrates of Modules 16–17, and tested in Module 18. It adds the two things no fab supplies: the design (RTL through GDS, verified on emulators and closed with EDA tools against TSMC's PDK) and the system (module, baseboard, tray, rack, cooling, and network), built by ODMs from a supplier tree of several hundred companies. Its output is the finished product: an HGX server or an NVL72 rack shipped to a hyperscaler, a neocloud, or an enterprise, at $300k to $4M per unit. Module 20 steps back to ask what this chain costs, where it is concentrated, who can and cannot replicate it, and what a single disruption at TSMC, at the HBM makers, or in the Taiwan Strait would do to the numbers in this module.
