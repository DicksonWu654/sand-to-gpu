# Module 08: Photolithography II: EUV in Full Detail

Module 07 ended with a problem. An ArF immersion scanner (193 nm, NA 1.35) bottoms out at roughly 38–40 nm half-pitch (a 76–80 nm pitch) in a single exposure, and every metal, fin, or gate layer tighter than that has to be built by multi-patterning: two, three, or four lithography passes plus the spacer and cut steps in between. At 7 nm-class nodes the tightest layers needed LELELE or SAQP plus multiple cut masks, and each extra pass costs a mask, a track pass, an etch, a clean, a metrology step, a day of cycle time, and an overlay error budget that was being spent faster than it was being earned. **Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography** exists to collapse those three or four passes back into one by cutting the wavelength by a factor of 14, from 193 nm to 13.5 nm.

The price of that factor of 14 is that almost nothing about a DUV scanner survives. There is no lens, because no material transmits 13.5 nm light. There is no laser at 13.5 nm; the light comes from a tin plasma at half a million kelvin, created 50,000 times per second by a 10.6 µm CO₂ laser hitting falling droplets of molten metal. There is no air, because air absorbs the light within centimeters; the whole machine is a vacuum vessel filled with a few pascals of hydrogen. There is no transmissive photomask, so the reticle is a mirror, and the light has to come in at an angle, which distorts the image in ways a transmissive mask never did. And a 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV, so a normal exposure dose consists of so few photons that their counting statistics show up as defects on the wafer. This module goes through each of those problems and how ASML, Zeiss, Trumpf, Cymer, and the resist and mask suppliers solved them, in enough detail to explain why one of these machines costs $200 million, or $380 million for the High-NA version.

## Why 13.5 nm

### Everything absorbs EUV

Photon energy is $E = hc/\lambda$, which for convenience is $1240\ \text{eV·nm}/\lambda$. At 193 nm that is 6.4 eV, deep in the ultraviolet but still below the ionization energies of most atoms; fused silica and calcium fluoride transmit it. At 13.5 nm the photon carries 91.8 eV, which is above the ionization threshold of every element's valence shell and many of their inner shells. A 92 eV photon absorbed by any solid ejects a photoelectron; the material absorbs, it does not refract. The absorption length in silicon at 13.5 nm is under 1 µm; in most metals and oxides it is tens to a few hundred nanometers. Even gases absorb strongly: at atmospheric pressure the 1/e length in air is a few hundred micrometers. There is therefore no lens material, no window material, and no possibility of running in air. EUV optics must be all-reflective, and the beam path must be in vacuum.

### The Mo/Si Bragg mirror sets the wavelength

But ordinary mirrors do not work either. A metal surface at normal incidence reflects a few percent at best at 13.5 nm because the refractive index of every material is close to 1 (the real part is $n = 1 - \delta$ with $\delta$ of order 0.01 to 0.1). The trick, developed at Bell Labs and elsewhere in the 1980s, is the **multilayer mirror**: a stack of alternating thin films of two materials chosen so that the small reflections at each interface add in phase. This is a Bragg reflector, the same physics as X-ray diffraction from a crystal, and the condition for constructive interference at near-normal incidence is that the bilayer period $d$ satisfies $2d\cos\theta = \lambda$, so $d \approx \lambda/2 \approx 6.9$ nm.

The material pair is chosen to maximize the reflectance difference at each interface while minimizing absorption inside the stack. Molybdenum and silicon turn out to be close to ideal in a narrow window: silicon becomes unusually transparent just above its L-absorption edge at 12.4 nm (99.8 eV), while molybdenum has a high electron density and therefore a large $\delta$ contrast. A **Mo/Si multilayer** with ~ 40 to 50 bilayers, each ~ 6.9 to 7.0 nm thick (roughly 2.8 nm Mo and 4.1 nm Si, the Mo fraction near 0.4), has a theoretical peak reflectance of ~ 74–75% and a practically achieved ~ 67–70% at 13.5 nm. The reflectance curve of a single mirror is a peak roughly 0.5 nm (~ 4%) wide at half maximum; after ten or more reflections in series the system passband narrows to about 2% (13.5 nm ± 1%, ~ 0.27 nm), which is the "in-band" definition used for source power, and it falls off steeply above ~ 14 nm and below ~ 12.5 nm. The wavelength of EUV lithography is set by where that mirror works, not by anything about the light source.

The stacks are deposited by magnetron sputtering (or ion beam sputtering for the highest quality) with thickness control at the 0.01 nm level across a 300 mm or larger substrate, because a 1% error in period shifts the reflectance peak by 1% of the wavelength and detunes the mirror from every other mirror in the system. Mo and Si interdiffuse into molybdenum silicide at the interfaces, which blurs the interface and costs reflectance; commercial stacks insert sub-nanometer diffusion barriers (boron carbide or carbon) and cap the top with ~ 2 to 2.5 nm of ruthenium to resist oxidation and hydrogen-induced blistering.

Each reflection loses at least 30% of the light. That single fact drives much of the EUV story: with ten or eleven reflections between the source and the wafer, only about 1 to 2% of the in-band light created at the source's focus ever reaches the resist. Every watt of source power is precious, and every mirror in the design is argued over.

### Why tin

Given that the mirror wants 13.5 nm ± 1%, the source has to emit as much energy as possible into that band. Several plasmas were tried. Xenon emits around 10.8 to 11 nm and was the source in the early 2000s prototypes; its conversion efficiency into the Mo/Si band was under 1%. Lithium has a strong single line at 13.5 nm (the Li²⁺ Lyman-α transition) but is chemically vicious and hard to handle. Tin won because highly charged tin ions, from roughly Sn⁸⁺ to Sn¹⁴⁺, have hundreds of thousands of overlapping 4d–4f and 4p–4d transitions that pile up into an **unresolved transition array (UTA)** peaking at ~ 13.5 nm. A tin plasma at an electron temperature of ~ 30 to 40 eV (350,000 to 500,000 K) has its ionization balance right in that charge-state range and emits a broad hump centered near 13.5 nm that overlaps the mirror band well. Conversion efficiency of laser energy into in-band (2% bandwidth) EUV radiated into the forward hemisphere is ~ 5 to 6% in production sources, the highest of any candidate.

## The Laser-Produced Plasma Source

The EUV source is a machine in its own right, built by **Cymer** in San Diego (bought by ASML for about $2.5 billion, announced in 2012 and closed in 2013, because the source was the pacing item of the whole program) with the drive laser from **Trumpf** in Ditzingen, Germany. It occupies a vessel below and beside the scanner body, with the laser and its power supplies and cooling taking up more floor space in the sub-fab than the scanner does in the cleanroom. Its job is to produce, at an **intermediate focus (IF)** roughly the size of a pinhead, a steady stream of 13.5 nm light at hundreds of watts, with a spectral purity and pointing stability that the illuminator downstream can live with, for months without opening the vessel.

### The droplet generator

The fuel is tin, delivered as a stream of identical microscopic droplets. Inside the source vessel, a reservoir holds a few hundred grams of tin heated to ~ 250 °C, just above tin's 232 °C melting point, and pressurized with argon to tens of bar. The liquid is forced through a nozzle only a few micrometers in diameter, producing a filament of tin roughly 20 µm thick moving at 70 to 80 m/s. A liquid jet of that diameter is unstable: surface tension amplifies any small perturbation at wavelengths longer than the jet circumference (the **Rayleigh-Plateau instability**), and the jet spontaneously breaks up into droplets. Left alone, it breaks up irregularly. A piezoelectric actuator on the nozzle imposes a controlled pressure modulation at the desired frequency so the breakup is deterministic: one droplet per cycle, every cycle. Satellite droplets that form between the main ones are made to merge with them within a short distance downstream by choosing the modulation waveform carefully.

The result is a train of tin spheres ~ 25 to 30 µm in diameter, spaced uniformly, falling through the vacuum at 70 to 80 m/s, at a repetition rate of 50 kHz in the NXE:3400/3600 generation and higher (60 kHz and, in the 2026 1 kW demonstration, 100 kHz) in the newest sources. Fifty thousand droplets per second, each one a target that must be hit by a laser pulse focused to a spot not much larger than the droplet itself, on a path of a few hundred millimeters, after which the surviving material must be removed from the chamber. Positional jitter of the droplet train is a few micrometers; if it drifts more than that, the laser misses and EUV output collapses. The droplet generator is a consumable: the nozzle erodes and clogs, the tin runs out, and the whole unit is swapped every few weeks to a few months of operation.

> **Worked example: tin consumption and droplets per wafer.** Take a 27 µm droplet. Its volume is $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ with $r = 13.5\ \mu m$, which is $1.03 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}^3$. Liquid tin has a density of ~ 7,000 kg/m³, so each droplet has a mass of ~ 72 nanograms. At 50,000 droplets per second the generator consumes ~ 3.6 mg/s, i.e. ~ 13 g per hour or ~ 0.3 kg per 24 hours of continuous operation (the droplet train runs continuously; droplets that are not hit by the laser are caught in a tin catcher). A month of full-time operation therefore uses on the order of 10 kg, which is why the reservoir holds kilograms of tin and is refilled (in newer sources in line, without opening the vessel) or swapped every few weeks. Tin cost is irrelevant at ~ $30 per kilogram; the problem is where the fraction that is vaporized goes, because every atom that does not leave through the pumps ends up on something. Per wafer: an NXE:3800E at ~ 220 wafers per hour spends ~ 16 seconds per wafer, so on the order of 800,000 droplets are fired per wafer (the source runs continuously, whether or not the wafer stage is actively scanning). A single 26 × 33 mm field is exposed in roughly 0.1 to 0.15 seconds, so each field receives ~ 5,000 to 7,500 pulses, which is why the dose delivered to any point on the wafer is the integral over thousands of pulses and the ~ few percent pulse-to-pulse energy jitter of the plasma averages away.

### The Trumpf CO₂ drive laser

The laser that turns tin droplets into plasma is a **CO₂ laser** operating at 10.6 µm, an infrared wavelength 785 times longer than the EUV it produces. The choice is deliberate. When a laser hits a plasma, it can only propagate up to the **critical density** where the plasma frequency equals the laser frequency; that density scales as $1/\lambda^2$. For 10.6 µm the critical density is ~ 10¹⁹ electrons per cm³, while for a 1.06 µm solid-state laser it is 100 times higher, ~ 10²¹ cm⁻³. A plasma heated at lower density is less opaque to its own EUV emission (less re-absorption of 13.5 nm photons on their way out), and its ionization balance sits in the Sn⁸⁺–Sn¹⁴⁺ range over a larger volume. The practical result is that CO₂-driven tin plasmas reach 5 to 6% conversion efficiency, while 1 µm-driven plasmas plateau at 2 to 3%. Solid-state lasers are more compact and efficient, so this remains an active research topic (Gigaphoton and several academic groups have pursued 1 µm and even 2 µm thulium drive lasers), but production sources are CO₂.

The laser system is a **master oscillator, power amplifier (MOPA)** chain. A low-power seed laser (a radio-frequency-excited CO₂ oscillator, itself producing pulses of tens of nanoseconds) is shaped and gated, sent through pre-amplifiers, and then through a chain of five large RF-excited CO₂ amplifier stages, each several meters long, built by Trumpf on the platform of its industrial cutting and welding lasers, which amplify the few-watt seed by more than 10,000×. The output is tens of kilowatts of average power in production sources (~ 25 to 40 kW; ASML has discussed higher for the 1 kW roadmap), pulsed at the droplet frequency, so each main pulse carries a few hundred millijoules in a few tens of nanoseconds, i.e. a peak power in the tens of megawatts range. The beam is transported ~ 20 meters through an evacuated **beam transport system** from the laser bay in the sub-fab up through the floor into the source vessel, steered by water-cooled mirrors, and finally focused through a hole in the center of the collector mirror onto the droplet with a spot of a few hundred micrometers.

A subtlety that took years to control: a molten tin droplet is a nearly perfect spherical mirror at 10.6 µm. Some of the laser light that hits it reflects straight back up the beam path into the amplifier chain, where it is amplified in reverse and can destroy the seed optics. The laser therefore carries optical isolation (acousto-optic and polarization-based) and the amplifiers are timed and saturated so that the back-reflection sees little gain. Thermal lensing in kilowatt-class CO₂ amplifiers, wall-plug efficiency (a few percent, so the laser alone dissipates on the order of a megawatt of electrical power as heat), and the gas mixture lifetime are the other everyday engineering problems of the drive laser.

### Pre-pulse and main pulse

Hitting a 27 µm sphere with a 10.6 µm beam is inefficient: the sphere is smaller than the focused spot and denser than optimal, and most of the laser energy would either miss or be reflected. Every production source therefore uses two pulses per droplet.

The **pre-pulse** arrives first. In early sources it was a separate solid-state (1.06 µm Nd:YAG) laser; newer sources derive it from the CO₂ chain. It is a low-energy pulse (millijoules, and in recent designs picoseconds long) that does not aim to make EUV; its purpose is to deform the droplet. The absorbed energy launches a shock through the liquid tin and the droplet expands laterally into a thin disk or pancake, a few hundred micrometers across, over the next 1 to 3 microseconds, during which the target has moved a few hundred micrometers downstream. With a short (ps) pre-pulse the droplet instead fragments into a cloud of fine mist. Either way the target that the main pulse sees is now a low-density, laser-spot-sized object with far more surface area.

The **main pulse** then arrives, timed to the microsecond and pointed to the tens-of-micrometers where the expanded target now is. Its few hundred millijoules are absorbed efficiently by the disk, heating the tin to ~ 30–40 eV. The ions strip to Sn⁸⁺ through Sn¹⁴⁺ and radiate their UTA; the plasma expands and cools within a few hundred nanoseconds, and the EUV flash is over. The output from a single pulse is a few millijoules of in-band light into the hemisphere facing the collector. Fifty thousand of those per second is the source's several hundred watts.

The two-pulse scheme, the timing loops that keep the main pulse landing on the pancake, and the target shaping are the heart of Cymer's conversion-efficiency gains from ~ 2% in 2010 to ~ 5.5% around 2017 and up to 6% or a little more since. The 2026 demonstration of 1,000 W in-band relied on a further refinement: pre-shaping with two pulses and doubling the droplet rate to 100 kHz, so the per-pulse energy did not have to double.

### Collector

Directly behind the plasma sits the **collector**, an ellipsoidal mirror ~ 650 mm in diameter with the plasma at its first focus and the intermediate focus at its second, a little over a meter away. The collector is a normal-incidence Mo/Si multilayer, graded in period across its surface because the angle of incidence varies from the center to the rim, so the Bragg condition has to be met everywhere. It subtends ~ 5 steradians, a bit under half of the full sphere, and reflects ~ 60 to 65% when new. It has a central aperture for the laser beam, and its front surface faces a plasma that emits ~ 2 kW of unwanted out-of-band radiation, tin ions at keV energies, tin neutrals, and tin microdroplets, all of which want to land on it.

The power delivered to the IF is what ASML quotes as source power: 250 W for the NXE:3400B/C generation (2017–2020), ~ 350 to 400 W for the NXE:3600D (2021), 500 to 600 W for the NXE:3800E (2024 onward) and the High-NA EXE systems, and 1,000 W demonstrated in a proof-of-concept source in early 2026 with a stated path to 1.5 or even 2 kW. Every step has come from some combination of laser power, droplet rate, conversion efficiency, and collector efficiency.

### Tin debris mitigation

A tin plasma that expands at tens of kilometers per second, 50,000 times a second, next to a $1 million mirror is the central reliability problem of EUV. Three things arrive at the collector: fast ions (which sputter the multilayer), slow neutral atoms and clusters (which deposit as a tin film), and occasionally larger fragments. One monolayer of tin on the collector costs about 1% reflectance; a few nanometers is a serious throughput loss.

The mitigation is built around hydrogen. The source vessel is not a hard vacuum but is back-filled with H₂ at a pressure on the order of 1 mbar (roughly 100 Pa), flowing continuously. The gas serves three purposes. First, it decelerates the ions and atoms by collisions before they reach the collector, so the fast ions are thermalized and far fewer sputter the mirror. Second, the EUV light and the plasma dissociate H₂ into hydrogen radicals, and atomic hydrogen reacts with tin to form **stannane (SnH₄)**, a gas at room temperature, which the vacuum pumps remove. Tin that has already deposited on the collector is slowly etched away by the same reaction, so the collector partly cleans itself in operation. Third, hydrogen is nearly transparent to 13.5 nm (it is the least absorbing gas at that wavelength; at 100 Pa it costs only ~ 10% per meter of path, and the vessel's actual pressure profile and path lengths are engineered to keep the total loss to a few percent), so the buffer pressure can be high enough to be useful without killing the light. Gas flows are arranged as curtains and jets (ASML calls one piece of this the **dynamic gas lock**) that push debris away from the collector and away from the IF, where the source vessel connects to the scanner. Some designs add a **plasma flush**, an actively generated hydrogen plasma to accelerate tin removal. Gigaphoton's competing source design (never adopted by ASML) uses a superconducting magnet of about 1 tesla to steer the ions along field lines away from the collector; ASML's production approach is gas-based.

None of this is perfect. Collector reflectance decays with pulse count, historically a few percent per billion pulses; at 50,000 pulses per second a billion pulses is under six hours of firing, so early collectors were being swapped every few weeks. Progress on mitigation has stretched collector life to many months and ASML has targeted more than a year. The collector is designed to be removed and replaced in a swap that takes a shift, and the old collector is sent back to Cymer for stripping and recoating; the swap itself, plus the pumpdown and re-alignment, is part of the machine's scheduled downtime budget.

### Targeting, metrology, and availability

Keeping the laser on the droplets is a closed-loop control problem at 50 kHz. A **droplet detection module** illuminates the droplet stream with continuous lasers and watches the scattered light with fast photodiodes and cameras to measure each droplet's position and timing. Fast steering mirrors in the final focus assembly correct the pointing of the pre-pulse and main pulse, and the trigger timing is adjusted pulse by pulse. Energy sensors at the IF measure the EUV energy of every pulse; the scanner's dose controller uses this to adjust the drive laser and, if the source falls short, slows the wafer stage scan so that dose per area is preserved at the cost of throughput. Spectral purity is monitored because the plasma emits from the infrared to the EUV, and out-of-band DUV in particular exposes resist without contributing to the image; a spectral purity filter or the multilayer's own selectivity, plus the illuminator's grazing mirrors, keep the DUV fraction at the wafer low.

Source availability was the reason EUV was a decade late. In 2012 sources at customer sites delivered ~ 10 to 30 W with availability (the fraction of time the tool could actually expose wafers) well under 70%; every droplet-generator clog, collector swap, and laser fault took the scanner down. ASML's goal through the 2010s was 250 W at greater than 90% four-week availability; 250 W was demonstrated in 2016 and reached at customer sites around 2017–2018 with the NXE:3400B, which is when Samsung and TSMC committed to EUV in production. By the early 2020s availability of 90% and above became typical, and the source no longer limits wafer output, though it still limits how far throughput can grow.

## The Optics

### The illuminator

Light leaving the intermediate focus enters the **illuminator**, whose job is to make the ring-shaped slit on the reticle uniformly bright and to set the angular distribution of the light (the **illumination pupil**), one of the strongest levers on resolution and process window, exactly as in DUV. With no lenses available, the illuminator is built from two **facet mirrors** (a field facet mirror and a pupil facet mirror), each a segmented surface of hundreds of small individually shaped and tilted tiles acting as a reflective **fly's eye** integrator, plus one or two grazing-incidence fold mirrors. Each field facet images the IF onto the reticle slit, so hundreds of overlapping images average the source's brightness into a uniform arc; the pupil facets set the angle each contribution arrives from. By choosing which field facets are routed to which pupil facets (with tiltable facets in ASML's **FlexPupil** illuminators), the tool synthesizes annular, dipole, quasar, and freeform pupils without absorbing apertures that would waste light. Grazing-incidence mirrors reflect over 80% at 13.5 nm thanks to total external reflection, which is why the illuminator uses them wherever geometry permits.

The field at the reticle is not a rectangle but a curved **ring-field** arc, ~ 104 mm wide and a few millimeters tall at the reticle (26 mm × 2 to 3 mm at the wafer). Reflective projection optics are only well corrected over an annular region around the optical axis, so the exposure is done by scanning the reticle and wafer through this arc, as in a DUV scanner but with the extra constraint that the ring field must be off-axis.

### The projection optics

The **projection optics box (POB)** in every NXE model is a **six-mirror, 4× reduction, 0.33 NA** all-reflective system designed and built by **Carl Zeiss SMT** in Oberkochen. Six mirrors is the practical compromise: four mirrors cannot reach 0.33 NA with the required aberration correction and field size, and every added mirror costs 30% of the light. The mirrors are aspheric and, in the newest designs, freeform (no rotational symmetry) so that the six surfaces can do the work that a DUV lens does with 30 to 40 elements. Mirror diameters range from ~ 100 mm to ~ 500 mm in the NXE optics and larger in High-NA. The full imaging field at the wafer is 26 mm × 33 mm, the same as DUV, so a reticle patterned over 104 mm × 132 mm prints a 26 × 33 mm field.

Each mirror is a substrate of ultra-low-expansion glass (Zerodur from Schott, or Corning ULE) polished and then coated with the same Mo/Si multilayer as the collector. The requirements on the substrates are where EUV optics become extraordinary:

- **Figure** (the low-spatial-frequency shape error) must be ~ 50 picometers RMS or better per mirror, because a shape error of height $h$ produces a wavefront error of $2h$ on reflection, and the total system wavefront error must be a small fraction of 13.5 nm. Fifty picometers is a quarter of a silicon atom's diameter. Zeiss's own comparison: if an NXE mirror were scaled up to the size of Germany, the largest bump would be about a millimeter high; for the High-NA mirrors it would be a tenth of a millimeter.
- **Mid-spatial-frequency roughness** (periods of micrometers to millimeters) scatters light at small angles into the image, producing **flare**, a uniform background haze that reduces contrast. Flare in NXE tools is at the few-percent level and has to be modeled in OPC.
- **High-spatial-frequency roughness** (sub-micrometer) scatters light out of the pupil entirely, reducing reflectance. It has to be below ~ 0.1 nm RMS.

The mirrors are shaped by successive polishing, measured by interferometry at visible wavelengths and at-wavelength, and finished by **ion beam figuring (IBF)**: a focused argon ion beam rasters over the surface, sputtering away material with a dwell time computed from the measured error map, removing picometers to nanometers locally without touching the surrounding area. The measurement is the harder half of the problem. Zeiss builds its own interferometers and reference optics, and the final test is exposing wafers in a fully assembled system. The projection optics box is delivered as one aligned unit, kept in vacuum, and its mirrors are actuated in situ (each mirror on hexapod-like manipulators with nanometer resolution) so the scanner can correct aberrations that drift with temperature and multilayer aging over the tool's life. Mirror heating by the absorbed 30% of the light is itself a real effect at 500 W sources and is corrected by wavefront sensing plus mirror actuation.

> **Worked example: transmission from source to wafer.** Count the reflections in an NXE system after the intermediate focus: about four in the illuminator (two facet mirrors and two fold mirrors; the grazing ones are more efficient but call all four ~ 0.67 to be conservative), one at the reticle (~ 0.65 for a patterned mask, less than a bare mirror because of the absorber and the cap), and six in the projection optics at ~ 0.67. That is $0.67^{4} \times 0.65 \times 0.67^{6} = 0.20 \times 0.65 \times 0.091 \approx 0.012$, so ~ 1.2% of the light at the IF reaches the wafer plane. Add a pellicle at ~ 90% single-pass transmission, which the light crosses twice, giving another factor of 0.81, and hydrogen absorption over the beam path of a few percent, and the net is about 1%. A 500 W source therefore delivers on the order of 5 W to the wafer. A full 300 mm wafer has an area of 707 cm²; at 30 mJ/cm² it needs 21 J. At 5 W that is a bit over 4 seconds of pure exposure, and the tool's 16 seconds per wafer at 220 wph is consumed largely by scanning overhead, stage turnaround between fields, and wafer exchange. That is why ASML's throughput gains come from both the source (dose time) and the stages (everything else).

## The Reflective Reticle

### Structure

An EUV **reticle** is a 152 mm × 152 mm × 6.35 mm plate of low-thermal-expansion glass (ULE) polished to a flatness of tens of nanometers, coated with the same ~ 40-bilayer Mo/Si multilayer and a ruthenium cap as the mirrors, and then with an **absorber** layer, ~ 55 to 70 nm of tantalum-based material (TaN or TaBN, with a thin TaBO or TaON anti-reflective top for inspection contrast). The pattern is written into the absorber by e-beam (Module 04): reflective areas are bright, absorber-covered areas dark. The backside carries a conductive chromium-nitride film because the reticle is held by an electrostatic chuck; a vacuum chuck cannot work in vacuum. **Hoya** and **AGC** supply the blanks; a buried bump or pit in the multilayer larger than ~ 1 nm high and a few tens of nanometers wide prints as a phase defect, and the yield of blanks free of such defects has been a problem for 15 years. Captive mask shops at TSMC, Samsung, and Intel, plus merchants Toppan/Photronics and DNP, deliver finished reticles at hundreds of thousands of dollars per plate.

### The 6° angle and mask 3D effects

Because the reticle is a mirror, the illumination cannot arrive along the optical axis, or the reflected light would go straight back into the illuminator. The **chief ray angle (CRA)** at the reticle is 6° from normal in the NXE optics, and the illumination cone around it spans roughly ± 5° (the reticle-side NA is 0.33/4 = 0.0825, i.e. ± 4.7°). That 6° tilt, combined with an absorber 60 to 70 nm tall, i.e. roughly the same height as the width of the features being printed at 4× (a 13 nm half-pitch line on the wafer is 52 nm on the reticle), produces a family of problems collectively called **mask 3D effects**:

- **Shadowing.** The absorber casts a shadow on the multilayer on one side of every feature. Lines running perpendicular to the plane of incidence are shadowed differently from lines parallel to it, producing a **horizontal-vertical (HV) bias** of a few nanometers between identical features drawn in the two orientations, which OPC must correct.
- **Pattern shift through focus (non-telecentricity).** The reflected light from a feature leaves at an angle, so when the wafer moves through focus the image moves sideways. The shift depends on feature size and pitch, so it cannot be fixed by a global overlay correction; it becomes part of the edge placement error budget.
- **Best-focus shifts and contrast loss** between different pitches, because the phase of the light diffracted off the absorber edge varies with the absorber's height and refractive index.

Mitigations are all in progress: thinner absorbers, absorbers with higher extinction (ruthenium-, platinum-, or nickel-based alloys) so they can be thinner, **low-n absorbers** that act as attenuated phase-shifters at EUV, and mask-3D-aware OPC (Module 07's OPC problem, made harder by a full 3D electromagnetic simulation of every edge). The most important mitigation, though, is why High-NA is anamorphic, covered below.

### Pellicles

A DUV reticle is protected by a **pellicle**, a thin transparent membrane held a few millimeters above the mask surface so that any particle landing on it sits out of the focal plane and does not print. For EUV no membrane material is transparent enough; a polymer would absorb everything. The first EUV tools ran without pellicles, which meant every particle that landed on the reticle printed on every die of every wafer until the reticle was inspected and cleaned. Fabs managed this with very frequent reticle inspection, in-vacuum reticle handling with the mask kept in a dual-pod (the outer pod is a standard SMIF-like box, the inner pod a clean metal enclosure that is only opened inside the scanner's reticle load lock), and by accepting some yield loss on layers with large dies.

The EUV pellicle developed by ASML with imec was a ~ 50 nm freestanding polysilicon membrane, later a metal-silicide stack, with ~ 83 to 88% single-pass transmission. Light crosses it twice, so the dose penalty is 20 to 30%, and the membrane, heated by absorbed light in vacuum with no convection to cool it, sags and fails at higher source powers. **Mitsui Chemicals** licensed ASML's pellicle in 2021 and mass-produces it. The next generation is the **carbon nanotube (CNT) pellicle** developed by imec and commercialized by Mitsui (partnership signed December 2023; a 5,000-sheet-per-year plant at Iwakuni-Ohtake completed at the end of 2025 for introduction in 2025–2026): a sparse mat of nanotubes that transmits 92% or more single-pass (imec has shown > 94%, i.e. ~ 85–89% double-pass) and is designed to survive 600 W and, eventually, 1 kW sources because the tubes radiate heat efficiently; hydrogen-radical etching of the carbon is the remaining lifetime question. Pellicle use is a fab decision: TSMC uses pellicles selectively, where the yield gain outweighs the throughput loss, and some memory makers ran pellicle-free longer.

### Inspection and heating

Because a buried multilayer defect and a 60 nm absorber edge are invisible to visible-light and DUV inspection at the required sensitivity, EUV reticle inspection has moved to **actinic** (at-wavelength) tools. **Lasertec** of Japan is the sole supplier: its ABICS blank inspection and ACTIS pattern inspection systems use a small EUV source and EUV microscope optics, and each costs on the order of tens of millions of dollars. KLA competes with e-beam and DUV approaches. Because reticle defects print on every die, EUV reticle inspection frequency is orders of magnitude higher than for a pelliclized DUV mask.

Finally, the reticle absorbs roughly 40% of the light hitting it (the absorber areas absorb almost all, the multilayer areas ~ 30%). At 500 W at the IF and 26 mm × 104 mm of illuminated arc, the mask's local temperature rises during exposure, and even with ULE glass the expansion produces overlay error at the nanometer level; the scanner models and pre-compensates reticle heating field by field.

## The Wafer Side

### A scanner that is a vacuum chamber

Everything downstream of the source, the illuminator, reticle stage, projection optics, and wafer stage, sits inside a single vacuum vessel about the size of a small bus, pumped continuously and back-filled with hydrogen at a few pascals. Wafers enter through load locks from an atmospheric wafer handler that receives FOUPs from the track (Module 07's TEL or SCREEN track is docked to the scanner exactly as with DUV, but the interface includes a vacuum pump-down cycle per wafer). Hydrogen serves the same anti-contamination role here as in the source: EUV light cracks any hydrocarbon that outgasses from the resist, the cables, or the stage components into carbon that deposits on the mirrors, and the hydrogen radicals etch it off again. Every material inside the vessel has to be qualified for outgassing, and every resist has to pass an outgassing test before it is allowed in the tool, because a resist that deposits carbon on the $100 million optics box is unacceptable.

Particles are a larger problem than in DUV because there is no pellicle by default and because vacuum has no air flow to carry particles away; moving stages, cable slabs, and door mechanisms are all engineered to shed nothing. Wafer clamping is electrostatic (the wafer table, made by ASML's Berliner Glas subsidiary, is a ceramic plate with embedded electrodes and thousands of burls that touch the wafer only on a fraction of a percent of its area) and cooling of the wafer during exposure is done through the small amount of hydrogen gas trapped under the wafer, since there is no air.

### Stages in vacuum

The stage architecture is inherited from DUV TWINSCAN: two wafer stages, one measuring the wafer (height map, alignment) while the other exposes, and one reticle stage scanning in synchrony at 4× the wafer speed. In vacuum the long-stroke stages are **magnetically levitated planar motors** (no air bearings, no lubricant) with accelerations of several g, and the position is tracked by interferometers and, on newer tools, by encoder gratings, at sub-nanometer resolution. Reaction forces are absorbed by balance masses so the vessel does not shake. The NXE:3800E's ~ 35–40% throughput gain over the NXE:3600D (220 versus 160 wph at 30 mJ/cm²) came as much from a new wafer stage, faster reticle stage, and faster wafer handling as from source power. The stages are ASML's own design; nobody else makes anything like them.

### Throughput and the dose trade-off

ASML specifies throughput at a reference dose. The NXE:3400C was rated at 170 wafers per hour (wph) at 20 mJ/cm² (135 wph at 30 mJ/cm²); the NXE:3600D at 160 wph at 30 mJ/cm²; the NXE:3800E at > 195 wph initially and 220 wph with upgrades, both at 30 mJ/cm², with the 500–600 W source. A fab that runs a layer at 60 mJ/cm² (which many contact and via layers do, to suppress stochastic defects) cuts the source-limited part of throughput in half. The relationship is not linear because of the fixed overhead per wafer, but at high doses the tool is source-limited and throughput scales as source power divided by dose. That relation is the whole economic argument for the source power roadmap, and for the 1 kW source's projected ~ 330 wph at 30 mJ/cm² by 2030.

The economics follow directly. A $200 million tool depreciated over five to seven years, plus service, consumables (collectors, droplet generators), and ~ 1 to 1.5 MW of electricity (mostly the laser), costs on the order of $30 to 40 million per year to own and run, or roughly $30 to 40 per wafer-pass at realistic availability and doses. A leading-edge logic wafer sees 15 to 25 EUV passes, so EUV contributes on the order of $500 to $1,000 to a wafer that sells for $20,000 to $30,000. Expensive, and still cheaper than the multi-patterned DUV it replaced.

## Photon Physics and Stochastics

### Very few photons

Here is the number that shapes all EUV process development. A 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV, versus 6.4 eV for an ArF photon. For the same energy dose in millijoules per square centimeter, EUV delivers 14 times fewer photons.

> **Worked example: photons per square nanometer at 30 mJ/cm².** One photon at 13.5 nm has energy $E = 1240 / 13.5 = 91.8$ eV $= 91.8 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} = 1.47 \times 10^{-17}$ J. A dose of 30 mJ/cm² is 0.030 J spread over $10^{14}$ nm², i.e. $3.0 \times 10^{-16}$ J/nm². Dividing, the dose corresponds to ~ 20 photons per nm² incident on the resist; a 20 mJ/cm² dose, which is where resist suppliers would love to operate for throughput, is ~ 14 photons/nm². Now account for what is actually absorbed: a chemically amplified resist absorbs ~ 5 µm⁻¹ at 13.5 nm, so a 30 nm film captures only $1 - e^{-0.15} \approx 14\%$ of the incident photons, roughly 3 absorbed photons per nm². A 16 nm × 16 nm pixel at the edge of a contact hole, the region that decides whether the hole is open or closed, is defined by ~ 750 absorbed photons at 30 mJ/cm². The Poisson standard deviation of that count is $\sqrt{750} \approx 27$, a 3.6% fluctuation in exposure from a source that is otherwise perfectly stable. On a 1 nm-wide strip along a line edge the count is a few dozen photons and the fluctuation is 15 to 20%. Compare ArF immersion: at the same 30 mJ/cm², 290 photons/nm² incident, and several tens absorbed in a typical 100 nm film; photon shot noise is not on the list of ArF problems.

### Consequences: stochastics

Because the photon count per feature is small and random, the chemical result of exposure varies from feature to feature even when the optical image is identical. The industry term is **stochastics**, and it manifests as a defect population with no deterministic cause: a contact hole that fails to open (a **missing contact**), two adjacent holes that merge (a **kissing contact**), a line that breaks, or a bridge between lines. The probability of any single feature failing may be $10^{-9}$ or $10^{-12}$; a chip with $10^{10}$ contacts on a critical layer then has a defect somewhere with a probability that decides the yield. Stochastic failure rates rise exponentially as the feature is pushed smaller or the dose is lowered, which is why EUV process windows are described not just in terms of CD and focus but with a **stochastic failure cliff** on each side.

Line-edge roughness (LER) and line-width roughness (LWR) are the continuous version of the same statistics. EUV lines at 13 nm half-pitch have LWR of ~ 2 to 4 nm (3σ) in the best resists, a large fraction of the linewidth, and roughness at line edges transfers into transistor variability and interconnect resistance variation.

The photon shot noise is compounded by the resist's own chemistry. Each absorbed 92 eV photon does not expose one molecule; it ejects a photoelectron that scatters in the film, generating a cascade of ~ 4 to 8 **secondary electrons** with energies of a few to tens of eV over a radius of a few nanometers, and it is those electrons that decompose the photoacid generator (in a CAR) or crosslink the metal cluster (in a metal-oxide resist). That **secondary electron blur** of ~ 2 to 4 nm sets a floor on the resolution and adds its own randomness (how many secondaries, where they went, which molecules they hit). Then in a CAR the acid diffuses during post-exposure bake, each acid catalyzing a random number of deprotection events, and the quencher base neutralizes acids at random. Every stage is a counting process and every stage adds variance.

The design response is captured in the **RLS triangle**: Resolution, Line-edge roughness, and Sensitivity (dose) cannot all be improved at once. A resist can be made faster (lower dose) by increasing acid yield or diffusion, but then fewer photons decide each edge and diffusion blurs it; it can be made smoother by adding quencher and shortening diffusion, but then it needs more dose. The empirical Z-factor, $Z = R^{3} \times L^{2} \times S$, has stayed roughly constant across a resist generation and each new platform tries to move it. Doses that were expected to be 10 to 15 mJ/cm² when EUV was planned in the 2000s are in practice 30 to 60 mJ/cm² for critical layers because that is what the stochastics demand, and the source power roadmap has had to make up the difference.

## EUV Resists

### Chemically amplified resists at EUV

Module 07's chemically amplified resist (CAR) platform, a polymer with acid-labile protecting groups, a photoacid generator (PAG), and a quencher, was extended to EUV by **JSR**, **TOK**, **Shin-Etsu**, **Fujifilm**, and **DuPont**, with JSR and TOK holding the largest EUV share. The adaptations: thinner films (~ 20 to 35 nm, versus 80 to 120 nm for ArF immersion) because tall, narrow resist lines collapse under capillary forces during development; polymer-bound PAGs to limit acid diffusion; higher PAG loading; and, in the newest formulations, metal-containing sensitizers (tin, iodine, or fluorine, elements with a high 13.5 nm cross-section) to absorb more of the sparse photons, moving absorption from ~ 4 to ~ 6 µm⁻¹. CARs remain dominant for lines and spaces at N5/N3 because of their maturity, their compatibility with the existing track and TMAH aqueous develop, and their low defectivity.

Underneath the resist is an **underlayer** (spin-on carbon or a thin organic adhesion and secondary-electron-emitting layer, a few nanometers thick) that improves adhesion, reduces pattern collapse, and in some designs adds secondary electrons back into the resist from below. Thin EUV resists are typically transferred first into a hardmask stack (spin-on carbon plus a SiON or metal-oxide hardmask, a **tri-layer** stack) before the real etch, because 25 nm of resist cannot survive a 100 nm dielectric etch.

### Metal-oxide resists

The alternative platform is the **metal-oxide resist (MOR)**, pioneered by **Inpria** of Corvallis, Oregon (acquired by JSR in 2021 for ~ $514 million). Inpria's resist is a solution of tin-oxo clusters, roughly 1 to 2 nm particles of butyl-tin oxide hydroxide, spin-coated to ~ 15 to 25 nm. Tin's 13.5 nm absorption is among the highest of any element, so the film absorbs ~ 15 to 20 µm⁻¹, three to four times a CAR, and a 20 nm film captures ~ 30% of incident photons rather than ~ 10%. Exposure cleaves the tin-carbon bonds and condenses the clusters into an insoluble tin-oxide network (a negative-tone process, developed in organic solvent), with no acid diffusion step; the "pixel" is the cluster size. The result is lower LER at a given dose and a higher etch resistance (it is essentially SnO₂ after exposure) that lets the resist be very thin. The cost is a different track chemistry, sensitivity to ambient humidity and to delay between coat and exposure, and a defectivity learning curve that took most of a decade. Memory makers (Samsung and SK hynix are widely reported) qualified MOR for DRAM contact layers first; logic makers use it selectively for contacts and vias.

**Lam Research** took the metal-oxide idea one step further with a **dry resist**: the organotin film is deposited by vapor phase (CVD/ALD-like) rather than spin-coated, exposed, and then developed with a dry plasma or thermal step rather than a solvent, all in vacuum-compatible chambers. Dry deposition gives a more uniform, thinner (sub-20 nm) film with no solvent, no edge bead, and better dose efficiency, and dry development avoids the capillary forces that collapse wet-developed lines. Lam's dry resist, branded **Aether**, was developed with imec and SK hynix (partnership announced 2022) and in January 2025 was selected as production tool of record for advanced DRAM by a leading memory maker; it is one of the leading candidates for High-NA, where the depth of focus mandates films of 20 nm or less.

## Resolution Limits and Multipatterning

Rayleigh's criterion from Module 07, $HP = k_1 \lambda / NA$, gives for the NXE at 13.5 nm and NA 0.33 with a practical $k_1$ of ~ 0.32 a half-pitch of ~ 13 nm, i.e. a **minimum pitch of ~ 26 to 28 nm** for dense lines in a single exposure. (The theoretical limit at $k_1 = 0.25$ is 10 nm half-pitch, but at that $k_1$ the contrast and stochastics make yield impossible.) For two-dimensional features such as contact and via arrays the practical single-exposure pitch is larger, ~ 30 to 36 nm.

This maps onto the nodes as follows. TSMC N7+ and Samsung 7LPP (2019) used EUV for a handful of layers at pitches (~ 40 nm) where a single EUV exposure replaced DUV LELE or LELELE. TSMC N5 (2020) used ~ 14 EUV layers with the tightest metal at ~ 28 to 30 nm pitch, all single-exposure EUV. At N3 the tightest metal pitches dropped to ~ 23 to 24 nm, below the single-exposure limit, and TSMC began **EUV double patterning**: **EUV LELE** (litho-etch-litho-etch, two EUV exposures each at a relaxed pitch, interleaved) on the two or three tightest metal layers, and **SALELE** (self-aligned LELE, where a spacer defined by the first exposure self-aligns the second and the cuts) to hold overlay. N2 and Intel 18A continue this pattern, with the number of EUV passes per wafer climbing into the mid-20s. EUV multipatterning is exactly the cost spiral that EUV was supposed to end, which is the demand case for High-NA.

## High-NA and Hyper-NA

### The EXE platform

The **TWINSCAN EXE:5000** (research and development tool, first shipped to Intel in December 2023 in 250 crates and completed in April 2024 at Intel's Hillsboro, Oregon D1X fab, with a second in the joint ASML-imec High-NA lab in Veldhoven) and the **EXE:5200B** (the high-volume model, first shipped to Intel in April 2025 and accepted at Intel's Oregon site in December 2025 ahead of Intel's 14A node, with TSMC, Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron following) raise the numerical aperture from 0.33 to **0.55**. By Rayleigh that lowers the single-exposure half-pitch from ~ 13 nm to ~ 8 nm (ASML quotes 8 nm resolution), which is enough to return the N2/A16-class metal layers to single exposure and to extend to the 14A / A10 generations.

Raising NA on a reflective system with an angled reticle creates a geometric problem. Reticle-side NA is wafer-side NA divided by the reduction ratio; at 4× reduction and 0.55 NA, the illumination cone at the reticle would be ± 8°, larger than the 6° chief ray angle. The incoming and outgoing cones would overlap, so part of the reflected light would go back into the illuminator, and the shadowing from the absorber at those grazing angles would be intolerable. The straightforward fix, going to 8× reduction, would quarter the field area and quarter throughput (a full reticle would print only 13 × 16.5 mm). Zeiss and ASML instead made the optics **anamorphic**: **8× reduction in the scan direction and 4× in the cross-scan direction**. In the direction where the angular problem is worst, the reticle-side cone is halved; in the other direction the field size is preserved. The same 6-inch reticle now prints a **half-field of 26 mm × 16.5 mm** at the wafer, half the 26 × 33 mm of every scanner since the 1990s.

A half-field is fine for a memory die or a mobile SoC. It is a real problem for a reticle-limited die like an NVIDIA GPU or a large data-center CPU, which fills the full 26 × 33 mm (Module 19's ~ 800 mm² dies). Those designs must either be split into two chiplets at the half-field boundary or **stitched**: printed as two half-field exposures whose patterns cross the boundary with sub-nanometer overlay, which ASML and the foundries have demonstrated but which adds overlay budget and edge-placement error exactly where a design would prefer none. The industry's move to multi-die packages (Module 17) makes this less painful than it would have been in 2015.

The rest of the EXE is scaled accordingly. The projection optics have larger mirrors, up to about a meter across, with a central obscuration in the pupil that simplifies the geometry at the cost of imaging behavior OPC must handle. The reticle stage moves at 8× the wafer speed in scan, so its accelerations are the highest in the machine. Depth of focus scales as $\lambda/NA^{2}$ and falls from ~ 120 nm-class to ~ 45 nm-class at 0.55, which drives resist thickness to ~ 20 nm or less (hence toward MORs and dry resist) and tightens flatness, chuck, and focus specs. The 8 nm half-pitch features are close to the secondary-electron blur, which is why High-NA resist development is the pacing item. Throughput is ~ 185 wph for the EXE:5000 at 30 mJ/cm² and 175 wph for the EXE:5200B initially, with ASML targeting 220. The EXE:5200B sells for on the order of €350 million (~ $380 million) and needs higher cleanroom ceilings and a heavier floor. ASML shipped its first ten or so High-NA systems through 2024–2025 and planned around ten in 2027.

Intel's strategy is to use High-NA in earnest at 14A (risk production ~ 2027), where it expects replacing EUV double patterning with single exposure to pay for itself; as a first step, ASML and Intel announced in July 2026 that certain Intel 18A layers (Core Ultra Series 3, "Panther Lake") had been dual-qualified on the EXE platform and were running in high-volume manufacturing on High-NA with yields matched to the NXE tools. TSMC has said it will not use High-NA at A16 and is evaluating it for A14 and beyond, betting that low-NA multipatterning is cheaper for a few more years. Which is right depends on how many layers are double-patterned and on the resist stochastics at 8 nm.

### Hyper-NA

ASML and Zeiss have discussed a further step to **NA 0.75**, "hyper-NA," on a roughly 2030 horizon. At that NA the mirrors approach two meters, polarization effects become significant (s-polarized and p-polarized light interfere differently at large angles, so the illumination must be polarized, which costs another factor of two in light), and the reticle-side angular problem returns even with anamorphic optics. Whether it is built depends on whether the cost per transistor still improves; as of 2026 ASML has said it will decide by about 2026–2027 and has framed hyper-NA as a possibility rather than a commitment.

## The Machine, Its Supply Chain, and Its Economics

An NXE:3800E contains on the order of 100,000 parts from ~ 5,000 suppliers, weighs ~ 180 tonnes, and ships in about 40 freight containers on ~ 20 trucks or three fully loaded Boeing 747s. It is assembled and tested in Veldhoven, Netherlands, then disassembled into modules for shipment and reassembled at the customer. Installation and qualification of a low-NA tool takes months; the first EXE:5000 at Intel took about six months from first crate (December 2023) to first light (April 2024).

ASML is an integrator: it builds the stages, wafer tables, and metrology, writes the software, and does final assembly, while the critical modules come from a few partners in bilateral, mostly exclusive relationships. **Zeiss SMT** (Oberkochen, Germany) makes the illuminator and projection optics, substrates and coatings included; ASML bought 24.9% of Zeiss SMT in 2016 for €1 billion to fund the High-NA optics, and there is no second source. **Trumpf** (Ditzingen) makes the CO₂ drive laser, a business reported to be on the order of €1 billion per year within Trumpf's ~ €4.3 billion total sales. **Cymer** (San Diego, ASML since 2013) builds the source and refurbishes collectors. **VDL ETG** (Eindhoven) builds the vacuum vessel, frames, and large mechanical modules; **Berliner Glas** (ASML since 2020) the wafer and reticle tables; **Prodrive** and **NTS** electronics and mechatronics; **Edwards** vacuum pumps. **Hoya** and **AGC** supply reticle blanks, **Lasertec** the actinic inspection, **Mitsui Chemicals** the pellicles.

ASML has shipped roughly 350 EUV systems cumulatively since the NXE:3100 in 2010: the 100th shipped in early 2020, and revenue was then recognized on 31 in 2020, 42 in 2021, 40 in 2022, 53 in 2023, 44 in 2024, and 48 in 2025 (low-NA plus a handful of High-NA); ASML has scheduled 56 low-NA and 10 High-NA deliveries for 2027. TSMC holds well over half of the installed base, followed by Samsung and Intel, with SK hynix and Micron in the tens for DRAM. The NXE:3800E lists at roughly €180 to 200 million (~ $200 million), and EUV service (spares, collectors, field upgrades, resident engineers at every EUV fab) is a large and growing fraction of ASML's revenue. Tools last 20+ years; the NXE:3300s and 3350s of 2013–2016 still run non-critical layers, and ASML retrofits newer sources and stages in the field.

## Who Has EUV, and Who Is Not Allowed To

EUV has never shipped to China. SMIC ordered an NXE:3400 in 2018 and the Dutch government, under US pressure, never issued the license; EUV has been formally export-controlled since 2019. The US October 2022 and 2023 rules and the Dutch September 2023 (NXT:2000i and above) and September 2024 (NXT:1970i/1980i) rules then restricted the immersion DUV tools that would be used to multi-pattern around the lack of EUV. SMIC nonetheless produced 7 nm-class logic (Huawei Kirin 9000S, 2023) and "5 nm-class" parts by DUV quadruple patterning, at lower yield and higher cost.

China's domestic response: **SMEE** (Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment) is the DUV champion, with 90 nm tools in production and a 28 nm-class immersion tool (SSA800) that has been "about to ship" for years. On EUV, the most public program is the **laser-induced discharge plasma (LDP)** source at Harbin Institute of Technology under Zhao Yongpeng, in which a laser vaporizes tin from an electrode and a high-voltage discharge through the vapor forms the plasma; reported in-band power was ~ 100 to 150 W in 2025, versus ASML's 500 to 600 W, and discharge sources are hard to scale (Ushio's XTREME group, which took over Philips' LDP program in 2010, reached ~ 30 W at the intermediate focus in 2011 and gave up on HVM sources a few years later). A prototype scanner, reportedly assembled with former ASML engineers, was said to be under test in 2025 with a target of ~ 2028; Tsinghua's **steady-state microbunching (SSMB)** synchrotron source is a longer-shot alternative. As of 2026 no Chinese EUV tool has printed a production wafer; the source gap is years, and the Zeiss-class optics are the harder problem behind it.

## History

In 1986 Hiroo Kinoshita at NTT demonstrated soft X-ray projection imaging with multilayer mirrors; Bell Labs and Lawrence Livermore followed, and the technique was renamed EUV to distance it from proximity X-ray lithography, which failed. In 1997 Intel led the formation of **EUV LLC** with Motorola and AMD (later Micron, Infineon, and IBM), a ~ $250 million consortium that funded Sandia, Lawrence Livermore, and Lawrence Berkeley to build the Engineering Test Stand, which printed its first EUV images in 2001. ASML, Nikon, and Canon licensed the technology; ASML shipped two alpha demo tools to imec and Albany in 2006 and the NXE:3100 pre-production tool (NA 0.25, ~ 5 wph) in 2010, and Nikon and Canon dropped out.

The 2010s were a decade of delays. The 2008 roadmaps had EUV in production at 22 nm in 2012; the source delivered a tenth of the promised power with poor availability, and mask blank defects and resist stochastics were unsolved. ASML responded by buying Cymer (2013), by having Intel, TSMC, and Samsung buy a combined 23% of its equity for €3.85 billion in 2012 plus €1.38 billion of R&D funding (Intel alone took a 15% stake and committed $4.1 billion including its $1 billion R&D contribution), and by buying into Zeiss SMT (2016). Cymer's LPP source won over Gigaphoton's, which had strong technology (magnetic mitigation, a 100 kHz droplet generator) but not ASML's integration. NXE:3300B (2013), NXE:3350B (2015), and the **NXE:3400B** in 2017 at 125 wph and 250 W made HVM plausible. **Samsung's 7LPP** was the first EUV node in high-volume manufacturing in early 2019, followed within months by **TSMC's N7+**; N5 in 2020 was the first node designed around EUV. Samsung and SK hynix brought EUV into DRAM in 2020–2021, Micron in 2024–2025. The NXE:3600D (2021), NXE:3800E (2024), EXE:5000 at Intel (December 2023), the first EXE:5200B (April 2025), the 1,000 W source demonstration (February 2026), and the first High-NA layers in high-volume manufacturing (Intel 18A, announced July 2026) followed. From first image to first product took 33 years.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"EUV is just a shorter-wavelength laser, like KrF to ArF."** → There is no EUV laser. The light comes from a tin plasma at ~ 500,000 K created by a CO₂ laser 785 times longer in wavelength; the machine is an all-reflective vacuum system with no lenses, no transmissive mask, and no air.
- **"13.5 nm was chosen because it is the shortest wavelength that could be produced."** → It was chosen because Mo/Si multilayer mirrors have their reflectance peak there. Shorter wavelengths (6.7 nm with La/B mirrors) have been studied; the mirror and the tin emission band, not the source alone, fix the wavelength.
- **"More source power means a smaller feature."** → Source power sets throughput at a given dose, not resolution. Resolution comes from wavelength and NA; power buys the ability to run higher doses (which reduce stochastic defects) without losing wafers per hour.
- **"EUV resolution problems are optical, like DUV."** → The dominant EUV yield limiter is stochastic: with only ~ 20 photons per nm² incident at 30 mJ/cm², and a few absorbed, photon counting noise plus secondary-electron and chemical randomness create missing contacts and line breaks with no deterministic cause.
- **"EUV ended multi-patterning."** → It ended it for one node. At N3 and N2, the tightest metal layers are below the ~ 26 nm single-exposure pitch of 0.33 NA and are double-patterned with EUV LELE or SALELE. High-NA exists to end it again.
- **"High-NA just prints smaller; everything else is the same."** → The optics are anamorphic (4× / 8×) to keep the reticle-side angles workable, which halves the field to 26 × 16.5 mm. Reticle-limited dies such as large GPUs must be stitched or split into chiplets.
- **"China has, or is about to have, its own EUV."** → As of 2026 the strongest Chinese source (Harbin LDP) is reported at ~ 100–150 W versus ASML's 500–600 W in production and 1,000 W demonstrated; no Chinese tool has printed a production wafer, and the Zeiss-class optics are the larger unsolved problem.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 07 delivered the resist chemistry, the track, and the DUV scanner that still exposes the majority of layers on any wafer; this module is the small set of critical layers (typically 15 to 25 of the ~ 80 to 100 lithography passes on an N3/N2 wafer) where only 13.5 nm light can print the pattern in one or two exposures. The inputs consumed here are the photomask blanks and finished EUV reticles from Module 04 (Hoya and AGC blanks, written and inspected in the foundry's mask shop with Lasertec actinic tools), EUV resists and underlayers from JSR, TOK, Shin-Etsu, Fujifilm, and Inpria/Lam, ultra-high-purity hydrogen, tin, and the ~ 1 MW of power and vacuum infrastructure in the sub-fab; the machine itself is the product of Zeiss, Trumpf, Cymer, VDL, and ~ 5,000 other suppliers integrated in Veldhoven. The output is a patterned resist layer, ~ 25 nm thick, with features at 13 nm half-pitch (8 nm with High-NA), that immediately goes to Module 09 for etch transfer into the hardmask and the underlying film, and whose CD, overlay, and stochastic defect populations are what Module 13's metrology and inspection tools spend most of their time measuring.
