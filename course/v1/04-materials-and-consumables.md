# Module 04: Beyond Silicon: Compound Semiconductors and Fab Consumables

Silicon is the substrate of almost every chip that matters, but it is not the only semiconductor in the box, and the polished wafer that arrived at the end of Module 03 is only the beginning of the fab's shopping list. Every one of the ~1,000+ process steps a leading-edge wafer goes through consumes something: a resist, a gas, an acid, a slurry, a sputter target, a precursor, a quartz tube, a photomask. A 300 mm wafer that leaves TSMC N2 at ~$25,000–30,000 has had thousands of dollars of consumables poured over it and pumped through it, and most of those consumables came from a small number of companies, many of them Japanese, that most people have never heard of.

This module has two halves. **Half A** covers the semiconductors that are not silicon, or not plain silicon: strained SiGe inside every modern transistor, silicon carbide in every 800 V electric-vehicle inverter, gallium nitride in the charger on your desk, gallium arsenide in your phone's radio, indium phosphide in the lasers that carry every bit between GPUs in an AI cluster, and the 2D materials being tested as the channel material for the 2030s. **Half B** is the consumables supply chain: what a fab burns through, at what purity, from whom, and why a single Japanese chemical plant or one Ukrainian gas purifier can ripple across the world's semiconductor output.

The hard problem in both halves is the same: making something at part-per-trillion purity and atomic-scale perfection, reproducibly, at industrial scale, with a qualification lock-in so severe that switching vendors mid-node can take a year.

## Half A: The Other Semiconductors

### Why silicon wins, and where it loses

Silicon's dominance rests on four things: an abundant raw material, a stable native oxide (SiO2) that passivates the surface almost perfectly, a melting point (1,414 °C) low enough for Czochralski growth in a quartz crucible, and 60 years of accumulated process learning. But its intrinsic electronic properties are mediocre. Electron mobility in bulk Si is ~1,400 cm²/V·s and hole mobility only ~450 cm²/V·s; its bandgap of 1.12 eV is indirect, so it cannot emit light efficiently; and its **critical electric field** (the field at which avalanche breakdown occurs) is only ~0.3 MV/cm, which forces high-voltage silicon devices to be thick, slow, and lossy.

Every material in this half exists to fix one of those deficiencies. The table below is the reference you will want while reading.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Si | 1.12 (indirect) | ~1,400 | ~450 | ~0.3 | ~150 | 5.431 |
| Ge | 0.66 (indirect) | ~3,900 | ~1,900 | ~0.1 | ~60 | 5.658 |

### Silicon-germanium: the alloy inside every modern transistor

**Silicon-germanium (Si1-xGex)** is a fully miscible alloy: Si and Ge share the diamond cubic structure and you can dial in any Ge fraction x from 0 to 1. The catch is that Ge's lattice constant (5.658 Å) is **4.2% larger** than Si's (5.431 Å), so the mismatch of Si1-xGex to Si scales as roughly 0.042·x. Grow SiGe epitaxially on a silicon wafer and the film wants to be bigger than the template it sits on. For a thin enough film, the crystal accommodates this elastically: the SiGe is squeezed in-plane to match the Si lattice, and it bulges out-of-plane (tetragonal distortion). This is a **pseudomorphic** or **coherently strained** film, and the compressive strain in it is exactly what device engineers want.

Above a **critical thickness** h_c, the stored elastic energy exceeds the energy cost of creating **misfit dislocations** at the interface, and the film relaxes by generating them. The classic Matthews–Blakeslee equilibrium theory gives h_c on the order of 10–20 nm for Si0.8Ge0.2 and only a few nm for Si0.5Ge0.5; in practice films grown at low temperature (~550–650 °C) can be grown metastably several times thicker (the People–Bean curve) because dislocation nucleation is kinetically limited. Misfit dislocations are catastrophic in a transistor: their threading segments punch up through the channel, act as leakage paths, and getter dopants. So all production SiGe in logic is kept safely below h_c and is deposited by **selective epitaxy** in a reduced-pressure CVD reactor (Applied Materials Centura Epi or ASM Intrepid class tools) using dichlorosilane (SiH2Cl2) or silane, germane (GeH4), HCl for selectivity, and diborane (B2H6) for in-situ p-type doping, at ~600–700 °C and ~10–80 Torr.

There are three distinct jobs SiGe does in a modern CMOS process:

**1. Embedded SiGe source/drain (eSiGe) for the pMOS transistor.** Intel introduced this at its 90 nm node in 2003 and every logic node since has used it. After the gate is defined, a recess is etched into the source and drain regions on either side of the pMOS channel, and SiGe is selectively regrown into the recess. Because the SiGe wants to be larger than the Si it fills, it pushes on the channel from both sides, creating uniaxial **compressive strain** along the channel direction. Compressive strain along <110> splits the heavy-hole and light-hole valence bands, reduces the hole effective mass, and reduces intervalley scattering; the result is a hole-mobility boost of ~50% or more and drive-current gains of 20–30%. The Ge fraction has climbed from ~17% at 90 nm to ~40–55% in FinFET and nanosheet nodes, with the recess brought closer to the channel each generation. Boron in-situ doping to >10^20 cm⁻³ simultaneously lowers contact resistance, and Ge lowers the Schottky barrier to the contact metal. For nMOS, the equivalent trick would be a tensile stressor (Si:C with ~1–2% carbon, or SiP), which had a much rougher time in production; nMOS strain has come more from tensile nitride liners and, in nanosheets, from process geometry.

**2. Sacrificial layers in gate-all-around nanosheets.** TSMC N2, Samsung SF3/SF2 and Intel 18A build their channels from a **Si/SiGe superlattice**: alternating layers of ~5–10 nm Si and ~8–12 nm Si0.7Ge0.3 epitaxially grown blanket over the wafer. The whole stack must stay below the critical thickness of the ensemble, which constrains x to roughly 0.2–0.35. Later, during **channel release**, the SiGe is removed selectively with a vapor-phase HCl or a dry-etch chemistry that etches SiGe hundreds of times faster than Si, leaving suspended Si nanosheets around which the gate is wrapped. The Ge fraction is a tradeoff: higher x gives better etch selectivity but lower h_c and rougher interfaces.

**3. SiGe as a channel.** With Ge fraction pushed to 0.5–1.0, SiGe is a candidate pMOS channel in its own right. Intel, imec and others have shown Si0.4Ge0.6 and pure Ge nanosheet pFETs with superior hole mobility, but integration is brutal: Ge's oxide is unstable (see the germanium section), the SiGe/Si superlattice becomes inverted (now Si is the sacrificial layer), and thermal budget must be cut to avoid Ge intermixing. As of ~2025 this remained an R&D option for nodes beyond A14, not a production choice.

> **Worked example: how thick can a Si0.75Ge0.25 layer be before it relaxes?**
> Misfit strain f ≈ 0.042 × 0.25 = 1.05%. In Matthews–Blakeslee form the critical thickness for a single strained layer is approximately h_c ≈ (b / (2πf)) · ((1 − ν cos²α) / ((1 + ν) cos λ)) · ln(h_c / b + 1), with Burgers vector b ≈ 0.38 nm for 60° dislocations, Poisson ratio ν ≈ 0.28, cos α = 0.5 and cos λ = 0.5. The geometric prefactor is (1 − 0.07) / (1.28 × 0.5) ≈ 1.45. Solving iteratively: guess h_c = 15 nm gives ln(15/0.38 + 1) ≈ 3.7, so h_c ≈ (0.38 / (2π × 0.0105)) × 1.45 × 3.7 ≈ 5.76 × 1.45 × 3.7 ≈ 31 nm; plugging 31 nm back gives ln(82.6) ≈ 4.4 and h_c ≈ 37 nm. The equilibrium answer is ~35–40 nm. That is why nanosheet designers use ~10 nm SiGe sacrificial layers at x ≈ 0.25–0.3: each layer is comfortably below h_c, but the total stack of 3–4 SiGe layers plus interleaved Si still has to be engineered to stay coherent. Push x to 0.5 and h_c collapses to ~5–8 nm, which is why high-Ge channels are so hard.

### Strained silicon: the idea that went the other way

**Strained silicon** in its original 1990s form (IBM, MIT, AmberWave) meant the opposite geometry: grow a thick, fully relaxed SiGe **virtual substrate** (graded from 0 to ~20–30% Ge over several microns), then grow a thin Si layer on top. Because relaxed SiGe has a larger lattice than Si, the thin Si is under **biaxial tensile strain**, which lifts the six-fold conduction band degeneracy, puts electrons into the two low-transverse-mass valleys, and boosts electron mobility by up to ~1.8×. It worked in the lab. It failed in production because the relaxed buffer inevitably contains ~10^5–10^7 cm⁻² **threading dislocations** and a "crosshatch" surface roughness, and because CMP of SiGe and Ge outdiffusion into the channel were headaches. The industry instead took the process-induced route: eSiGe, stress liners, stress memorization, and later the FinFET geometry, all of which put local strain into the channel without a defective global substrate. The lesson, which recurs throughout this module, is that the physics is rarely the bottleneck; the defect density and the integration cost are.

### Silicon carbide: the material you cannot melt

**Silicon carbide (SiC)** is the workhorse wide-bandgap material for power electronics. Its critical field of ~2.5–3 MV/cm is roughly ten times silicon's. Because the blocking voltage of a drift region scales with (critical field)² × thickness and the on-resistance scales inversely with (critical field)³, a 1,200 V SiC MOSFET can have a drift layer ~10 µm thick and a specific on-resistance hundreds of times lower than a silicon device of the same rating (this is the **Baliga figure of merit**). SiC's thermal conductivity (~370–490 W/m·K) is three times silicon's, so the die can run hotter and be cooled more easily. And unlike silicon IGBTs, SiC MOSFETs are unipolar, so they switch without a tail current, which lets EV inverters run at higher frequency with smaller passives.

**Why 4H?** SiC exists in more than 200 **polytypes**, stacking sequences of the same Si-C bilayers. The three that matter are 3C (cubic, bandgap 2.36 eV), 6H (hexagonal, 3.02 eV), and 4H (hexagonal, 3.26 eV, stacking ABCB). 4H wins because it has the highest bandgap of the easily grown polytypes, an electron mobility of ~900–1,000 cm²/V·s that is more than twice 6H's, and low mobility anisotropy between the c-axis and the basal plane (6H's mobility along c is only ~100 cm²/V·s, which is fatal for a vertical device where current flows along c). Essentially all commercial SiC power devices are 4H, on wafers cut 4° off the (0001) c-axis toward [11-20] so that subsequent epitaxy proceeds by **step-flow growth** and inherits the substrate's polytype rather than nucleating 3C islands.

**Growth: physical vapor transport.** SiC has no congruent melting point at practical pressures: it decomposes (peritectically) at ~2,830 °C and 35 atm. So there is no Czochralski for SiC. The industrial process is **physical vapor transport (PVT)**, also called **seeded sublimation** or the modified Lely method. A graphite crucible, typically induction-heated, holds SiC powder source at the bottom at ~2,300–2,500 °C and a 4H seed crystal glued to the lid at a slightly lower temperature, ~2,100–2,300 °C, in argon at a few millibar to a few tens of millibar. The source sublimes into Si, Si2C and SiC2 vapor species, which diffuse up the small thermal gradient (~10–30 °C/cm) and condense on the cooler seed. The crucible is a black box: at 2,400 °C you cannot see or probe the growth interface, and the graphite itself slowly reacts. Everything is controlled indirectly through the coil, the pressure, and the axial gradient.

The growth rate is ~100–500 µm/h, so a usable boule of 30–50 mm accumulates over roughly a week, i.e. several millimeters per day at best. Compare a Czochralski silicon puller producing a 2 m, 300 mm-diameter ingot in a couple of days. That single fact explains most of SiC's cost. The other cost drivers:

- **Defects.** Early SiC was riddled with **micropipes** (hollow-core screw dislocations, now driven below 0.1 cm⁻²), but the remaining zoo of **threading screw and edge dislocations (TSD/TED)** at ~10^3 cm⁻² and **basal plane dislocations (BPD)**, which convert into stacking faults under bipolar current and degrade body-diode forward voltage over a device's life, still limits yield. Polytype inclusions (patches of 6H or 15R in a 4H boule) and carbon inclusions scrap whole slices.
- **Slicing.** 4H-SiC has a Mohs hardness of ~9.5 (silicon is ~7). Diamond wire sawing a 150 mm boule takes days and loses ~30–40% of the crystal as kerf and subsurface damage; the wafer then needs diamond lapping and a long CMP. Laser slicing (DISCO KABRA, Infineon's Cold Split from its Siltectra acquisition) uses a sub-surface laser modification layer and cleaves wafers with almost no kerf, raising wafers-per-boule substantially.
- **Diameter.** The industry standardized on 150 mm (6-inch) through the late 2010s and is transitioning to 200 mm. Wolfspeed's Mohawk Valley fab in New York (opened 2022) was the first 200 mm SiC device fab in volume; SICC, TankeBlue, Coherent and others now ship 200 mm substrates. Going to 200 mm gives ~1.8× the die per wafer, but a 200 mm boule needs a bigger crucible, a longer run, and even tighter gradient control to avoid cracking.

The consequence is a 150 mm 4H-SiC substrate that sold for well over $1,000 in 2022 and, after aggressive Chinese capacity additions, had fallen to roughly $400–800 by 2025. The substrate plus epi has historically been 40–50% of the finished SiC MOSFET cost, compared to a few percent for a silicon wafer in a silicon device. Substrate market share has swung sharply: TrendForce put Wolfspeed at ~34% of the ~$1.0 billion n-type substrate market in 2024, with TankeBlue (Beijing) ~17%, SICC (Shandong) ~17% and Coherent (formerly II-VI) ~14%, and by 2025 the two Chinese suppliers were reported to have caught up with or passed Wolfspeed as 150 mm prices collapsed. Wolfspeed itself, having spent billions on the 200 mm transition, filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 on 30 June 2025 and emerged on 29 September 2025 with ~70% of its debt eliminated. ROHM/SiCrystal, Resonac (formerly Showa Denko), SK Siltron and STMicroelectronics (via its Norstel acquisition) are the other substrate makers of note; Soitec's SmartSiC bonds a thin monocrystalline 4H layer onto a low-cost polycrystalline SiC handle to stretch each boule further.

**The epi layer** is where the device actually lives: 5–100 µm of lightly doped n-type 4H-SiC grown by hot-wall CVD at ~1,550–1,650 °C from silane and propane in hydrogen (with HCl or chlorinated precursors for higher rates), doped with nitrogen (n) or trimethylaluminum (p). Epi doping uniformity of ±5% and thickness control of ±2% set the breakdown voltage; a 1,200 V MOSFET needs ~10–12 µm at ~8×10^15 cm⁻³. Aixtron, LPE (now ASM), Nuflare and Tokyo Electron supply SiC epi reactors.

**Why EVs.** Tesla's 2017 Model 3 was the first mass-market EV with a full-SiC main inverter (STMicroelectronics modules), and the industry followed as 800 V architectures (Porsche Taycan, Hyundai E-GMP, Lucid, most new Chinese platforms) made SiC's advantages decisive: a SiC inverter typically adds ~5–10% to driving range versus silicon IGBTs at the same battery size, mostly by cutting switching and conduction losses at partial load, and it shrinks the cooling system. STMicro, Infineon, onsemi, Wolfspeed, ROHM and Mitsubishi Electric lead SiC device supply; automotive is ~70% of the SiC device market, with solar inverters, industrial drives and, increasingly, AI data-center power supplies as the rest.

### Gallium nitride: the heterostructure material

**Gallium nitride (GaN)** has a slightly larger bandgap (3.4 eV) and critical field (~3.3 MV/cm) than SiC and a direct bandgap that made it the blue-LED and Blu-ray-laser material (the 2014 Nobel Prize). For electronics, its killer feature is not a bulk property but an interface: an **AlGaN/GaN heterojunction** forms a **two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG)** without any doping. GaN's wurtzite crystal is strongly polar; the spontaneous polarization of the GaN, plus the piezoelectric polarization of the tensile-strained AlGaN barrier grown on top of it, creates a fixed sheet charge at the interface that is neutralized by ~10^13 cm⁻² of free electrons in a triangular quantum well a few nm thick. Because these electrons are spatially separated from any ionized donors, their mobility is ~1,500–2,000 cm²/V·s, giving a sheet resistance of ~300–400 Ω/sq. A transistor whose channel is this 2DEG is a **high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT)**.

**Bulk GaN is nearly impossible to grow.** The equilibrium nitrogen pressure over GaN at its ~2,500 °C melting point is ~6 GPa, so melt growth is impractical. Bulk GaN substrates (50–100 mm) exist from Sumitomo Chemical/SCIOCS, Mitsubishi Chemical and Kyocera via **hydride vapor phase epitaxy (HVPE)** (GaCl + NH3 at ~1,000 °C, ~100 µm/h, grown thick on a foreign seed and then separated) or the slower **ammonothermal** method (supercritical ammonia at ~500–600 °C and ~100–300 MPa, a nitride analog of hydrothermal quartz growth). They cost thousands of dollars each and are used for laser diodes and emerging vertical GaN power devices, not for volume.

So almost all GaN is a thin film grown by **metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD)** on a foreign substrate. Trimethylgallium (TMGa) and ammonia flow over a substrate at ~1,000–1,100 °C in a hydrogen/nitrogen carrier at 50–500 mbar, with a V/III ratio of several thousand because NH3 cracks so inefficiently; TMAl and TMIn add Al and In. Aixtron (Germany) and Veeco (US) are the MOCVD tool duopoly, with Japanese Taiyo Nippon Sanso and Chinese AMEC also shipping. The three substrate choices define three industries:

- **GaN-on-Si.** Grown on 150–200 mm Si(111), with 300 mm in pilot at Infineon, imec and others. The lattice mismatch to Si is ~17% and the thermal expansion mismatch ~54%, so the wafer would crack on cooldown without a several-micron buffer: an AlN nucleation layer, step-graded or superlattice AlGaN layers that deliberately build compressive strain during growth to cancel the tensile stress that appears on cooling, then a ~1–2 µm carbon-doped GaN insulating buffer, then the GaN channel and the ~20 nm AlGaN barrier. The wafer can be processed in a standard silicon fab (this is what makes GaN cheap). These are the 650 V devices in most compact 65–240 W USB-C chargers since ~2019 (Navitas, Power Integrations, Innoscience, EPC, Infineon's GaN Systems, Texas Instruments), in solar micro-inverters, and now in 48 V data-center power stages for AI racks. Innoscience (China) operates the largest GaN-on-Si capacity on 200 mm wafers.
- **GaN-on-SiC.** Semi-insulating 4H-SiC has only ~3.5% lattice mismatch to GaN and superb thermal conductivity, so it is the substrate for RF power: 5G massive-MIMO base station amplifiers, radar, and satellite links. Sumitomo Electric Device Innovations, Qorvo, MACOM (which bought Wolfspeed's RF business in 2023), Ampleon and NXP lead. The substrate costs more than the whole GaN-on-Si wafer.
- **GaN-on-sapphire.** For LEDs; cheap, insulating, and thermally poor.

A native AlGaN/GaN HEMT is **depletion-mode** (normally on), which is unacceptable for a power switch. Industry solves this with a **p-GaN gate** (a p-doped GaN layer under the gate depletes the 2DEG at zero bias; EPC, Infineon/GaN Systems, Innoscience) or a **cascode** (a low-voltage silicon MOSFET in series; Transphorm, now part of Renesas). Reliability under high-field stress (dynamic on-resistance, trapping in the buffer) was the long pole for automotive qualification and is now largely solved for 650 V; 1,200 V GaN remains contested territory with SiC.

### Gallium arsenide and indium phosphide: the III-V workhorses

**Gallium arsenide (GaAs)** was "the material of the future" for logic in the 1980s and never got there, because it has no good native oxide and its hole mobility is poor, so complementary logic does not work. Where it does win is anywhere you need a direct bandgap or very fast electrons: the power amplifiers and switches in every smartphone's RF front end (heterojunction bipolar transistors and pHEMTs from Skyworks, Qorvo and Broadcom, largely fabbed at WIN Semiconductors in Taiwan, the world's largest GaAs foundry), VCSELs at 850 and 940 nm for face recognition and short-reach datacom, and red/infrared LEDs.

GaAs melts at 1,238 °C, but arsenic's vapor pressure at that temperature is ~1 atm and rising steeply, so an open crucible would boil off its arsenic. Two crystal growth methods dominate:

- **Liquid-encapsulated Czochralski (LEC).** A layer of molten boric oxide (B2O3), about a centimeter thick, floats on the GaAs melt in a pyrolytic boron nitride (pBN) crucible and physically seals the arsenic in; the chamber is pressurized with inert gas to 2–20 atm. Pulling proceeds through the B2O3 layer. The steep thermal gradients produce a **dislocation density (etch pit density, EPD)** of 10^4–10^5 cm⁻², which is fine for semi-insulating RF substrates (undoped LEC GaAs is naturally semi-insulating at ~10^8 Ω·cm because of the EL2 deep donor, ideal for microwave isolation).
- **Vertical gradient freeze (VGF)** and its close cousin **vertical Bridgman (VB)**. The charge sits in a sealed pBN crucible inside a multi-zone furnace; a gentle temperature gradient is walked upward so the melt freezes from a seed at the bottom. The low gradient gives EPD below 10^3 cm⁻², which matters for optoelectronics (dislocations are non-radiative recombination centers and kill laser lifetime). AXT (US-headquartered, China-based production), Freiberger Compound Materials (Germany) and Sumitomo Electric are the main merchant suppliers; 150 mm is standard, 200 mm has been demonstrated.

**Indium phosphide (InP)** is the substrate of long-haul and datacenter optics. The lattice-matched ternaries and quaternaries on InP (In0.53Ga0.47As, InGaAsP, InAlAs) cover the 1,310 nm and 1,550 nm telecom windows where silica fiber has minimum dispersion and minimum loss. InP is worse to grow than GaAs: the phosphorus vapor pressure at its 1,062 °C melting point is ~27 atm, so LEC needs a high-pressure puller, and the crystal is brittle and twins easily. Standard diameters are only 75–100 mm, with 150 mm in development at Sumitomo Electric and AXT; the small wafer, the difficult growth, and a market that is tiny compared with silicon mean an InP wafer costs hundreds of dollars per square inch. Sumitomo Electric, AXT, JX Advanced Metals and InPACT (France) are essentially the entire supply.

Why this matters for AI: a GB200 NVL72 rack, and the scale-out network beyond it, is stitched together with 800 Gb/s pluggable optical transceivers moving to 1.6 Tb/s. An 800G module carries eight 100 Gb/s lanes (or four at 200 Gb/s); a 1.6T module carries eight 200 Gb/s lanes. Each lane at those rates over any distance beyond a few meters is driven by an InP **electro-absorption modulated laser (EML)** or a directly modulated DFB laser from Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Mitsubishi Electric or Sumitomo Electric; even silicon-photonics transceivers, which do the modulation and detection in silicon, still need an InP continuous-wave laser as the light source, because silicon cannot lase. In 2024–2025 InP laser and EML capacity, not switch silicon, was one of the tightest constraints on shipping AI-cluster networking, and both Lumentum and Coherent announced capacity expansions and a move to larger InP wafer diameters. InP also builds the fastest transistors anywhere (InP HBTs and HEMTs with f_max above 1 THz), used in test-and-measurement front ends and 6G research.

### Germanium

**Germanium** was the semiconductor of the first transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) and lost to silicon in the early 1960s for exactly the reasons that still limit it: its oxide is a disaster (GeO2 is water-soluble and GeO sublimes at ~400 °C, so there is no stable passivation), its 0.66 eV bandgap gives ~1,000× higher intrinsic carrier concentration than Si and hence high leakage at operating temperature, and its lower density of states makes it easier to degenerate. What it has is mobility: ~3,900 cm²/V·s for electrons and ~1,900 cm²/V·s for holes, the highest hole mobility of any common semiconductor, which is why high-Ge-fraction SiGe is the standard pMOS booster and pure-Ge channels keep reappearing on roadmaps. Ge's other roles today: the substrate for triple-junction GaAs/InGaP solar cells on satellites (lattice-matched to GaAs), infrared optics, GeO2 as the core dopant of essentially all optical fiber (the single largest use by tonnage), Ge-on-Si photodetectors in silicon photonics (Ge absorbs at 1,310 and 1,550 nm; Si does not), and SiGe heterojunction bipolar transistors.

Ge is a byproduct of zinc refining and coal fly ash; China produces ~60% of refined Ge, with Umicore (Belgium) the main non-Chinese refiner and recycler. China put Ga and Ge under export licensing in August 2023 and banned exports to the US in December 2024; the Ge spot price roughly doubled through 2024. The same controls cover gallium, of which China refines ~90% (from alumina production), which is the raw input of GaN and GaAs.

### The periodic table at 2 nm and below

TSMC N2, Samsung SF2 and Intel 18A all use silicon nanosheets with SiGe stressors, and the immediate successors (TSMC A16 with backside power, A14; Intel 14A) do too. Where does the channel material go after that? imec's public roadmap runs nanosheet → forksheet → **CFET** (complementary FET, nMOS stacked on pMOS, ~2030+) with silicon channels, and puts non-silicon channels around 2035 and beyond. The candidates:

- **High-Ge SiGe and Ge pMOS channels**, discussed above; the most conservative option.
- **2D transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)**: MoS2, WS2, WSe2. A single MoS2 monolayer is 0.65 nm thick, has a direct ~1.8 eV bandgap, and, because it has no dangling bonds and is atomically thin, it keeps electrostatic control at gate lengths below 10 nm where a silicon sheet would have to be thinned to a few atoms and lose its mobility to surface scattering. Measured device mobilities are only ~10–100 cm²/V·s, but at such short channels transport is quasi-ballistic and mobility matters less than injection velocity. The showstopper has been **contact resistance**: metals form Fermi-level-pinned Schottky contacts to MoS2. The 2021 TSMC/MIT result using semimetal bismuth contacts (and later antimony) cut contact resistance to ~100 Ω·µm and revived the field. TMDs are grown by MOCVD on sapphire at 700–1,000 °C and transferred, or grown directly at 300 mm on oxide at lower temperature with worse quality; Intel and TSMC showed gate-all-around 2D nanosheet transistors and the first 2D CMOS inverters and ring oscillators at IEDM 2023–2024. Wafer-scale uniformity, transfer without wrinkles, and a compatible gate dielectric (no native oxide, no dangling bonds to nucleate ALD on) remain unsolved for manufacturing.
- **Oxide semiconductors** such as IGZO (indium gallium zinc oxide), already used in display backplanes; with ~10 cm²/V·s mobility they are useless for logic but are the leading candidate for the DRAM access transistor (ultra-low leakage lets a cell hold charge for seconds) and for low-temperature transistors in the BEOL.
- **Carbon nanotubes** and **III-V channels** (InGaAs nMOS, the 2010s' favorite, abandoned around 2016–2018 for lack of a stable oxide and for mismatch defects) round out the list.

The pattern is consistent: silicon's competitors always win on paper, and silicon's ecosystem always wins on defect density and integration cost, until the geometry itself makes silicon impossible.

## Half B: What a Fab Consumes

### The scale of it

SEMI puts the total semiconductor materials market at ~$67.5 billion for 2024, roughly $43 billion of it **wafer fab materials** (everything from silicon wafers to slurries) and the balance packaging materials. Silicon wafers are the single biggest line (~$12–13 billion), then gases, photomasks, resists and their ancillaries, wet chemicals, CMP consumables, sputter targets, and precursors. Japan's share by value is ~50%, and in several categories (resists ~90%, EUV mask blanks ~100%, high-purity colloidal silica, ultra-high-purity HF) it is close to a monopoly. The rest of this half walks through each category: what the material does, the specification that makes it hard, and who makes it.

### Photoresists

A **photoresist** is a polymer solution that is spin-coated to 30–300 nm, baked, exposed, and developed so that the pattern on the mask becomes a pattern in the resist, which then masks the etch or implant beneath. Module 07 covers the chemistry in depth; here the concern is supply.

The four exposure generations use four chemistries. **i-line (365 nm)** resists are novolac resin plus a diazonaphthoquinone (DNQ) dissolution inhibitor: exposure converts DNQ to a carboxylic acid that makes exposed regions soluble in the alkaline developer. **KrF (248 nm)** introduced **chemically amplified resists (CARs)**: a poly(hydroxystyrene) backbone with acid-labile protecting groups and a **photoacid generator (PAG)**; each absorbed photon releases one acid molecule that, during the post-exposure bake, catalytically deprotects hundreds of sites, so sensitivity is amplified ~100× at the cost of acid diffusion blur. **ArF (193 nm)** resists could not use aromatic polymers (they absorb 193 nm), so they moved to methacrylate backbones with alicyclic adamantyl and lactone side groups for etch resistance and adhesion, plus fluorinated additives for immersion. **EUV (13.5 nm)** resists come in two families: CARs re-engineered for a regime where each 92 eV photon generates a cascade of secondary electrons and only ~10–20 photons land per nm² (hence **stochastic** defects), and **metal-oxide resists (MORs)**, in which tin-oxo clusters with organic ligands (the Inpria chemistry, acquired by JSR in 2021 for $514 million) absorb EUV ~4–5× more strongly than carbon-based polymers and crosslink directly, giving better resolution and line-edge roughness at 15–25 nm film thickness. **Lam Research's dry resist** goes one step further: a tin-based organometallic precursor is deposited as a film by vapor-phase deposition instead of spin coating, exposed, then dry-developed in a plasma, eliminating the solvent, the coat non-uniformity, and much of the pattern collapse; as of ~2025 it is in qualification and early use at memory makers, with details unpublished. A leading-edge fab uses dozens of distinct resist products: several EUV resists, ArF immersion resists for each critical layer, KrF for mid-layers, thick i-line resists for implant masking, plus bottom anti-reflective coatings (BARC), spin-on carbon and spin-on glass hardmask layers, top coats, and developers and rinse solvents to go with them.

The supply base is Japanese to a degree matched by no other category. **JSR** (taken private by the state-backed Japan Investment Corporation in 2024), **Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK)**, **Shin-Etsu Chemical**, **Fujifilm Electronic Materials** and **Sumitomo Chemical** hold, between them, roughly 90% of the world market for KrF/ArF resists and an even larger share of EUV resists, where TOK, JSR and Shin-Etsu are the qualified suppliers at TSMC, Samsung and Intel. **DuPont** (whose electronics business was spun off as the independent company Qnity Electronics in late 2025) and **Merck KGaA** (AZ Electronic Materials) are the non-Japanese players, strong in i-line, KrF, BARC and ancillaries. Chinese suppliers (Red Avenue, Kempur, Shanghai Sinyang) cover i-line and are qualifying KrF; ArF and EUV remain out of reach. Korea's Dongjin Semichem supplies KrF/ArF resists to Samsung.

Why so concentrated? Resist is a product of tacit knowledge: dozens of proprietary monomers, PAGs, quenchers and additives, synthesized in-house at multi-kilogram scale, filtered to remove sub-10 nm particles and metal ions to the low-ppt level, blended to a formulation co-developed over years with one fab's litho team, and qualified layer by layer. An EUV resist costs on the order of several thousand dollars per liter versus roughly a thousand per liter for ArF immersion, and a 300 mm wafer takes ~1 mL per coat after modern low-dispense optimization (most of which spins off), so resist is not a huge line item per wafer; the issue is not price but irreplaceability.

That irreplaceability became geopolitical on 1 July 2019, when Japan removed South Korea from its export "white list" and required individual licenses for three items: photoresist (specifically EUV-relevant resists), high-purity hydrogen fluoride, and fluorinated polyimide (for OLED displays). Samsung and SK hynix scrambled: Soulbrain and RAM Technology accelerated 12N HF production in Korea, Samsung invested in Dongjin and imported resist via Belgium (JSR's JV with imec), and the Korean government launched a materials-localization program. Licenses were in practice issued after a few weeks and the restriction was formally lifted in 2023, but the episode showed that a single upstream country could halt the world's memory output with a customs form.

### Gases

A fab uses on the order of 40–50 different gases, split between **bulk gases** (nitrogen, oxygen, argon, hydrogen, helium) delivered by pipeline or on-site plant at thousands of cubic meters per hour, and **specialty gases** (a few hundred cylinders' worth of etchants, deposition precursors, dopants and cleaning agents) delivered in cylinders, ton containers or ISO tanks and piped through gas cabinets, valve manifold boxes and double-contained 316L electropolished stainless tubing to each tool.

**Nitrogen** is the largest by volume: a large 300 mm fab consumes on the order of 30,000–60,000 Nm³/h, used for purging every tool, load lock and FOUP, as a carrier and dilution gas, and for blanketing chemical tanks. At that scale it is always made on-site by an **air separation unit (ASU)** owned and operated by Air Liquide, Linde, Air Products or Nippon Sanso next to the fab, cryogenically distilling air and then purifying the N2 to 99.9999%+ (sub-ppb O2, H2O and hydrocarbons) with catalytic and getter purifiers. Argon comes from the same ASU (it is ~0.93% of air); hydrogen and oxygen are either piped from a nearby industrial plant or generated on-site. **Helium** is used as a thermal-contact gas on the backside of wafers in etch and implant chucks, as a leak-detection tracer, and as a carrier; it is extracted from a few natural-gas fields (Qatar, the US, Russia's Amur project, Algeria) and its price spikes every few years when one of them hiccups.

**Neon and Ukraine.** DUV excimer lasers (Cymer, owned by ASML, and Gigaphoton, owned by Komatsu) fill their discharge tube with a premix that is ~96–98% neon with ~1–3% argon or krypton and ~0.1% fluorine, replaced continuously as the fluorine is consumed. Neon is 18 ppm of air and is only economical to extract as a side stream of the largest cryogenic ASUs, which historically meant Soviet-era steel plants. Two Ukrainian purifiers, Ingas in Mariupol and Cryoin in Odesa, took crude neon from those plants and refined it to 99.999% laser grade, supplying an estimated 45–54% of the world's semiconductor-grade neon. Both shut down when Russia invaded in February–March 2022. Spot neon prices rose more than tenfold; Chinese ASU operators and Linde expanded purification; and Samsung, SK hynix and TSMC sharply reduced consumption through laser gas-recycling systems (Cymer and Gigaphoton both offer neon reclaim that cuts fresh-gas need by ~50%). Ukraine's share was already declining after the 2014 Crimea shock had prompted diversification; by 2024 China supplied the majority of new neon. The lesson generalizes: many specialty gases are byproducts of some other industry's largest plants, and the purification step, not the raw gas, is the chokepoint.

The specialty-gas suppliers are a mixed group: **Air Liquide** (France; also the largest supplier of on-site bulk plants and of the "ALOHA" precursor line), **Linde** (Ireland/US/Germany), **Air Products** (US), **Nippon Sanso Holdings/Taiyo Nippon Sanso** (Japan, including Matheson in the US), **SK Specialty** (Korea, formerly SK Materials, the world's largest NF3 and a major WF6 producer), **Merck KGaA** (Germany, which bought Versum Materials in 2019 for $6.5 billion), **Resonac** (Japan, formerly Showa Denko, strong in HBr, Cl2 and etch gases), **Kanto Denka**, **Central Glass**, and China's **Peric** and **Huate**. Purity is specified at 99.999–99.9999% with individual contaminants at ppb levels, and cylinders are electropolished, baked, and certified individually.

### Wet chemicals

Between almost every pair of process steps there is a clean, and cleans are wet. A leading-edge flow includes on the order of 200+ wet-processing steps. The core chemistries are decades old but the purity has tightened by four orders of magnitude:

- **SPM / piranha**: H2SO4:H2O2 in ~4:1 at 120–150 °C, strips photoresist and organics. Sulfuric acid is the highest-tonnage fab chemical; a large fab uses thousands of tonnes per year and increasingly recycles it on-site by thermal reconcentration.
- **SC-1 (APM)**: NH4OH:H2O2:H2O in ~1:1:5 at 60–75 °C, removes particles (the alkaline solution slightly etches the oxide beneath a particle and the electrostatic double layer repels it) and organics. Followed by **SC-2 (HPM)**: HCl:H2O2:H2O ~1:1:6, removes metals as soluble chlorides. Together they are the **RCA clean** (1965, Werner Kern at RCA).
- **DHF**: dilute HF at 0.5–1% removes native oxide and leaves a hydrogen-terminated, hydrophobic surface; **BOE** (HF buffered with NH4F) etches oxide at a controlled ~100 nm/min for 6:1.
- **TMAH** (tetramethylammonium hydroxide) at 2.38 wt% (0.26 N) is the universal positive-resist developer; it is also, at higher concentration, an anisotropic Si etchant and it is acutely toxic through the skin.
- **IPA** (isopropanol) for Marangoni or vapor drying: a wafer pulled slowly from water into an IPA-vapor atmosphere dries without watermarks because the surface-tension gradient pulls the water film off.
- Plus hot phosphoric acid (H3PO4 at ~160 °C for SiN removal, an enormous volume consumer in 3D NAND where hundreds of nitride layers are stripped), HNO3, acetic acid, ozonated water, and a growing number of proprietary formulated cleans (post-CMP cleans, post-etch residue removers, selective etches for nanosheet release).

The specification is metals. SEMI's C-series standards define grades; the tightest ("Grade 5"/"UHP") call for each of ~30 metals below 10 ppt, particles below a few per mL at 0.1 µm or smaller, and anions at ppb. A single sodium or iron atom in the gate oxide region is a threshold-voltage shift or a leakage site. Purification is by distillation (sulfuric, HF), sub-boiling distillation, ion exchange, and, crucially, filtration and packaging: the bottle, drum, PFA piping and every valve must not leach. Chemicals arrive in ISO tanks or 200 L drums, are transferred to a **bulk chemical distribution system (BCDS)** with day tanks and PFA loops, filtered again at point of use, and blended on-tool (SC-1 and DHF are mixed at the process chamber from concentrates and ultrapure water).

The suppliers: **BASF** (Germany, large in electronic-grade H2SO4, NH4OH, HCl), **Kanto Chemical**, **Fujifilm Wako**, **Mitsubishi Gas Chemical** (H2O2), **Mitsubishi Chemical** (IPA), **Tokuyama** and **Sumitomo Chemical** (Japan), **Honeywell**, **Avantor/J.T. Baker** and **Entegris** (which acquired KMG Chemicals in 2018; US), **Soulbrain** and **Dongjin Semichem** (Korea), and China's **Jiangyin Jianghua Micro-Electronic Materials**. The most famous chokepoint is ultra-high-purity HF: **Stella Chemifa** and **Morita Chemical Industries**, both in Osaka, are the two suppliers of 12N ("99.9999999999%") HF that Japan restricted to Korea in 2019, and between them supplied the large majority of the world's leading-edge etching-grade HF. HF's raw material is fluorspar, ~60% of which is mined in China, so the chain is China → Japan → the world.

### Ultrapure water

Water is the fab's largest single consumable by mass. It rinses after every wet step, dilutes every chemical, feeds every scrubber and cools every chiller. Process rinse water must be **ultrapure water (UPW)**, and its specification is the most extreme of any liquid produced industrially:

| Parameter | Typical leading-edge UPW spec | Ordinary drinking water |
|---|---|---|
| Resistivity | 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C (the theoretical maximum, 18.18, for water with zero ions) | ~0.002–0.02 MΩ·cm |
| Total organic carbon (TOC) | < 1 ppb (< 0.5 ppb for advanced fabs) | ~1,000–5,000 ppb |
| Dissolved oxygen | < 1–10 ppb | ~8,000 ppb |
| Dissolved silica | < 0.1–0.5 ppb | ~10,000 ppb |
| Boron | < 0.05 ppb | ~50–500 ppb |
| Individual metals | < 1 ppt each (sub-ppt targets for Fe, Cu, Ni, Zn) | ppb to ppm |
| Particles | on the order of 100–1,000 per liter above 20 nm; sub-10 nm counts are now specified | millions per liter |
| Bacteria | < 1 CFU per liter | tens to hundreds per mL |
| Temperature | 20–25 °C ± 0.5 °C (plus a hot-UPW loop at 60–80 °C) | |

Resistivity is the easy one: 18.2 MΩ·cm just means essentially no ions. TOC, boron, silica and particles are the hard ones. Boron passes reverse osmosis because boric acid is a weak acid that is mostly un-ionized at neutral pH; silica is similar; organics from resin leachates and the piping itself are continuous sources; and a 10 nm particle is not filterable by anything except an ultrafilter that itself sheds.

A UPW plant (built by Ovivo, Veolia, Kurita, Organo, Nomura Micro Science, or Xylem/Evoqua, with ion-exchange resins from DuPont, Lanxess and Mitsubishi Chemical) is a long train:

1. **Pretreatment**: coagulation and multimedia filtration, activated carbon (to remove chlorine, which destroys RO membranes), softening or antiscalant dosing, and 5 µm cartridge filtration.
2. **Reverse osmosis**, usually two passes in series; each pass rejects ~98–99% of ions and most organics. RO recovery is ~75–85%, so 15–25% of feed goes to reject.
3. **Membrane degasification** to strip CO2 (which forms carbonic acid and loads the ion-exchange beds) and O2.
4. **Electrodeionization (EDI)**: ion-exchange resin between membranes with an applied DC field, continuously regenerated by electrolysis instead of by acid and caustic.
5. **Primary mixed-bed ion exchange** to reach ~18 MΩ·cm.
6. **185 nm UV** oxidizes organics to CO2 and organic acids (TOC destruction), which the next bed removes.
7. **Polishing mixed-bed** (non-regenerable, replaced when exhausted) to reach 18.2 MΩ·cm and ppt metals.
8. **254 nm UV** for sterilization, then **ultrafiltration** (hollow-fiber, ~10 nm rated) as the final particle barrier.
9. A **recirculating distribution loop** in PVDF or clean PVC piping at ~1–2 m/s, never stagnant, returning everything not used to the polishing stage. Points of use get a final 0.05 µm filter.

The energy and capital are large (a UPW plant for a large fab is a $100–300 million facility), and the reason it is so big is volume. A 300 mm wafer at an advanced node consumes on the order of 1,500–2,500 gallons (6–9 m³) of UPW across its ~1,000+ steps. Fabs report total daily draw of 2–10 million gallons: TSMC's first Arizona fab is cited at ~4.75 million gallons/day, and a large gigafab complex in Taiwan more. Because UPW is so pure, most of the rinse water leaving a tool is still cleaner than city water, so fabs **reclaim** it: segregated drains route lightly used rinse water back to the RO feed, more contaminated streams to treatment and reuse in scrubbers and cooling towers. TSMC reports a process-water recycling rate of ~90% in Taiwan (90.3% in 2023) and its Arizona site runs ~65% today with an on-site industrial reclamation plant due in 2028 targeting 85–90%. The 2021 Taiwan drought, which forced TSMC to truck in water, made this a board-level topic.

> **Worked example: UPW for a 100,000-wafer-per-month fab.**
> Assume 1,800 gallons (6.8 m³) of UPW per 300 mm wafer, a mid-range figure for a ~1,000-step logic flow, and a 30-day month.
> UPW demand = 100,000 wafers × 1,800 gal / 30 days = **6.0 million gallons per day**, or ~22,700 m³/day (roughly nine Olympic swimming pools every day, or the water use of ~20,000 US households). At 2 m³/wafer-equivalent for chillers, scrubbers and cooling towers on top, total water throughput is ~8–9 million gallons/day.
> Now the intake. RO recovery of ~80% means the UPW plant must be fed 6.0 / 0.8 = 7.5 million gallons/day. If 85% of the process water discharged is reclaimed and returned to the RO feed, fresh make-up water is only what is lost: evaporation in cooling towers and scrubbers, RO reject, and the ~15% not reclaimed. A reasonable outcome is a fresh-water intake of roughly 2–3 million gallons/day (~8,000–11,000 m³/day) for a fab whose internal UPW flow is 6 million. Without reclaim, the same fab would draw ~8–9 million gallons/day of municipal water, which is why the reclaim rate, not the UPW spec, is what a city negotiates over before a fab is permitted. Note also that the UPW polisher beds, at ~1 ppb of allowed leakage, must be sized for 22,700 m³/day of flow: ion-exchange resin, UV lamps and ultrafilters are themselves significant recurring consumables.

### CMP slurries and pads

**Chemical-mechanical planarization (CMP)** appears 20–30+ times in a leading-edge flow: shallow-trench isolation oxide, poly and replacement-metal-gate planarization, tungsten and cobalt contacts, and one copper-plus-barrier CMP per metal level for 15–18 levels. Each CMP step consumes slurry at ~100–300 mL/min per platen for a minute or two, plus pad wear and conditioner wear. The wafer is pressed face-down against a rotating polyurethane pad while slurry flows in; the chemistry softens or oxidizes the surface, the abrasive removes the softened layer, and high points are removed faster than low points because pressure concentrates there.

A **slurry** is an engineered colloid: 30–150 nm abrasive particles (colloidal silica for oxide, poly, Cu and barrier; fumed silica historically; ceria for STI because it removes SiO2 ~50× faster than SiN, making the nitride a stop layer; alumina for older W processes) suspended at a few percent solids in water with an oxidizer (H2O2, KIO3 or ferric nitrate for tungsten; H2O2 for copper), a complexing agent (glycine for Cu), a corrosion inhibitor (benzotriazole, BTA, for Cu), pH buffers, surfactants and biocide. The chemistry defines the selectivity between the metal, the barrier and the dielectric; the particle-size distribution defines the scratch rate. Slurry is filtered at point of use with Entegris or Pall filters because a single 1 µm agglomerate scratches a line into the copper. Slurry suppliers: **Entegris** (which acquired CMC Materials, formerly Cabot Microelectronics, in 2022 for $6.5 billion; the leader in W and Cu slurries), **Fujimi** (Japan; the leader in colloidal-silica slurries for oxide, poly and bare-wafer polishing), **DuPont/Qnity** (ceria and Cu), **Resonac/Showa Denko** (ceria STI), **AGC**, **Merck**, **Fuso Chemical** (Japan; makes most of the world's ultra-high-purity colloidal silica raw material that other slurry makers formulate), **Anji Microelectronics** (China) and **KC Tech** (Korea).

The **pad** is a cast, sliced, and grooved polyurethane sheet of controlled porosity and hardness, usually a hard top pad (DuPont's IC1000 family and its successors) laminated to a soft sub-pad (Suba IV) for global-plus-local planarization. DuPont/Qnity has held the majority of the pad market for two decades; Entegris (via CMC), 3M, FNS Tech, SKC and Japan's Fujibo compete. A pad is **conditioned** continuously with a diamond-grit disk (3M, Kinik, Entegris, Saesol) to keep its surface open, and lasts ~12–36 hours of polishing before it is replaced. Post-CMP cleaning with brush scrubbers and dedicated cleaning chemistries (from the same slurry vendors) removes the slurry residue that would otherwise be the biggest particle source in the fab.

### Sputter targets

**Physical vapor deposition (PVD)**, covered in Module 06, ejects atoms from a **sputter target** with argon ions and deposits them on the wafer. In a logic fab the targets are: **tantalum** (and TaN by reactive sputtering in N2) for the copper diffusion barrier, **copper** for the electroplating seed layer (and for TSV seed in packaging), **titanium** and TiN for contacts and hardmasks, **cobalt** and **ruthenium** for liners and caps, **tungsten** for some metal gates and hardmasks, **aluminum** (with 0.5% Cu) for bond pads, and **nickel-platinum** for silicides. Memory fabs add many more.

A target is a 300–450 mm disk of 5N–6N (99.999–99.9999%) metal, bonded to a copper backing plate, with a controlled grain size (typically < 50 µm, sometimes < 20 µm) and crystallographic texture, because grain size and texture set the angular distribution of sputtered atoms and thus the film uniformity across the wafer; a target with coarse or mixed-texture grains gives a film with ±5% instead of ±1% thickness variation. Targets are consumed to an erosion groove and replaced every few hundred kilowatt-hours, and the spent target (still 60–70% of the metal) is returned for refining. Tantalum is the strategic one: it is refined from tantalite mined largely in the DRC and Rwanda (a "conflict mineral" under Dodd-Frank Section 1502), reduced to powder by Global Advanced Metals or TANIOBIS (H.C. Starck), then electron-beam melted, forged and rolled to a fine-grain plate.

The supply is a Japanese-American oligopoly: **JX Advanced Metals** (formerly JX Nippon Mining & Metals, spun out and listed in March 2025) claims the largest global share, on the order of half or more of semiconductor targets, particularly in Ta, Cu and Ti; **Honeywell Electronic Materials** (Spokane, US) and **Tosoh** (Japan) are the other two majors; **Materion** and **Linde/Praxair Surface Technologies** (US), **Ulvac** and **Proterial/Hitachi Metals** (Japan), **Plansee** (Austria, W and Mo) and China's **KFMI (Konfoong)** fill out the market.

### Precursors for ALD and CVD

Every film in the FEOL and MOL that is not grown by oxidation or sputtered arrives as a volatile molecule that decomposes or reacts on the wafer. Module 06 covers the deposition physics; the consumable question is the molecule. The important ones:

- **TEOS** (tetraethyl orthosilicate, Si(OC2H5)4): the workhorse for PECVD and sub-atmospheric CVD SiO2, because it decomposes cleanly at ~650–750 °C (or at ~400 °C with O3 or plasma) and gives conformal oxide. Liquid, delivered by bubbler or direct liquid injection.
- **SiN precursors**: DCS + NH3 for furnace LPCVD; BTBAS, TSA (trisilylamine), DIPAS and HCDS (hexachlorodisilane) for lower-temperature ALD nitrides needed after metal gate.
- **TMA** (trimethylaluminum, Al(CH3)3): with H2O or O3 the textbook ALD reaction for Al2O3 (~1.1 Å per cycle at 300 °C); pyrophoric; also the Al source for AlN and Al-doped HfO2. Made by Nouryon, Albemarle and Lanxess and purified to semiconductor grade by Merck, Entegris and Adeka.
- **Hafnium precursors** for the high-k gate dielectric: **HfCl4** (a solid that must be sublimed at ~150–170 °C in a heated solid-source delivery vessel; the chemistry Intel used at 45 nm in 2007) and the amide-based liquids **TDMAH** and **TEMAH** (tetrakis(dimethylamido)/(ethylmethylamido)hafnium) that are easier to deliver but leave carbon and nitrogen. **ZrCl4** and **TEMAZ** likewise for the ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 capacitor dielectric in every DRAM cell; Adeka (Japan) and SK trichem (Korea) have very large shares in these.
- **Titanium nitride precursors**: TiCl4 + NH3 for ALD TiN (the metal-gate work-function and barrier metal) and **TDMAT** (tetrakis(dimethylamido)titanium) for MOCVD TiN.
- **Tungsten**: WF6 nucleated with B2H6 or SiH4 then reduced with H2. **Molybdenum** (MoO2Cl2, MoCl5) is displacing W in 3D NAND wordlines and is a candidate for logic contacts because Mo has lower resistivity at small dimensions and needs no fluorine-blocking liner.
- **Cobalt and ruthenium**: for MOL contacts, liners and caps; Co from dicobalt-carbonyl-based molecules (e.g. CCTBA) and amidinates; Ru from cyclopentadienyl- and carbonyl-based compounds and from RuO4 solutions (Air Liquide's ToRuS). Ru precursors are expensive (Ru itself is a ~$500–800/oz platinum-group metal supplied mainly by Tanaka and Heraeus) and their nucleation delay on dielectrics is a chronic problem.

Precursors ship in electropolished stainless-steel ampoules of a few hundred grams to a few kilograms, with heated lines to the tool. A single 300 mm fab spends tens of millions of dollars per year on them. Suppliers: **Merck KGaA/EMD Electronics** (the Versum legacy; the broadest portfolio), **Entegris**, **Adeka**, **Air Liquide Advanced Materials**, **Gelest** (Mitsubishi Chemical), **Tanaka**, **DNF**, **Soulbrain**, **UP Chemical/Yoke**, **SK trichem** and **Hansol Chemical** (Korea, largely for Samsung and SK hynix), and **Nata Opto-electronic** and **Jiangsu Yoke** (China).

### Quartzware and SiC furnace parts

The parts inside a hot tool are consumables too. Diffusion and LPCVD furnaces (Kokusai, TEL) use process tubes, liners and wafer boats of **fused silica (quartz)**; single-wafer etch chambers use quartz windows, rings and gas-distribution plates, along with **silicon** and **silicon carbide** edge rings that surround the wafer on the electrostatic chuck. An etch **focus ring** erodes under ion bombardment and is replaced every few hundred RF-hours, so a busy etcher consumes tens of thousands of dollars of rings per year; it is a business Lam Research and TEL themselves compete in.

Semiconductor quartz is either **electrically fused** from the same Spruce Pine, North Carolina sand that supplies Czochralski crucibles (Module 02) or **synthetic fused silica** made by flame hydrolysis of SiCl4, which is purer (ppb alkali metals) but costlier. **Heraeus Conamic** (Germany), **Tosoh Quartz**, **Shin-Etsu Quartz Products**, **Momentive** (US) and China's **Ferrotec** supply ingots and shapes. Above ~1,200 °C, where quartz sags and devitrifies, boats and paddles are **SiC**: sintered or reaction-bonded SiC sealed with a **CVD-SiC coating** deposited from methyltrichlorosilane at ~1,300 °C; CVD-SiC-coated graphite susceptors carry the wafers in every SiC and GaN epi reactor. CoorsTek (US), Tokai Carbon, Kyocera and NGK (Japan) supply these.

### Photomasks: the supply chain in depth

A **photomask** (or **reticle**) is the master pattern that the scanner images onto the wafer, four times reduced, one field at a time. A leading-edge product needs 80–100+ of them, each different, each perfect, because a single printable defect on a mask is repeated on every die on every wafer. The mask supply chain is short, opaque, and expensive, and it has its own equipment industry.

**Blanks.** The universal substrate is the **6025** blank: 6 inches × 6 inches × 0.25 inch (152 mm × 152 mm × 6.35 mm) of synthetic fused silica, polished to a flatness of a few hundred nanometers for DUV and a surface roughness below 0.2 nm RMS, on which the absorber stack is deposited by sputtering. For **DUV** the stack is either ~70–100 nm of chromium with a CrOxNy anti-reflective layer (a **binary mask**, or **chrome-on-glass, COG**), or, for critical ArF layers, a ~70 nm molybdenum silicide film tuned to transmit ~6% of the light with a 180° phase shift (**attenuated phase-shift mask, att-PSM**), which sharpens the aerial image at feature edges; the thinner "opaque MoSi on glass" (OMOG) stack reduces electromagnetic shadowing at small pitches.

An **EUV blank** is a different animal, because 13.5 nm light is absorbed by everything, so the mask must be a mirror. The substrate is a **low-thermal-expansion material (LTEM)**, TiO2-doped silica (Corning ULE or Shin-Etsu's equivalent) with a thermal expansion coefficient within ±5 ppb/°C of zero, because the mask absorbs ~30–40% of a 500 W EUV source's light and any expansion is an overlay error; flatness below ~30 nm and roughness ~0.05 nm RMS are required. On it is deposited a **Bragg reflector** of 40 bilayers of molybdenum (~2.8 nm) and silicon (~4.1 nm), a 6.9–7 nm period tuned so that reflections from each Mo/Si interface add constructively at 13.5 nm and the 6° chief-ray angle, giving ~64–67% peak reflectivity, capped with 2.5 nm of ruthenium to stop oxidation during cleaning. The absorber is ~55–70 nm of tantalum boron nitride (TaBN) with a TaBO anti-reflective top; for High-NA, where the thick absorber's shadowing becomes a major imaging error, thinner (~30 nm) high-absorption alloys of Ru, Pt, Ni or Te are being introduced. The backside gets a conductive CrN layer for the scanner's electrostatic chuck.

The multilayer is deposited by **ion-beam sputtering** (Veeco's Nexus IBD tools are the industry standard) in a process that must add zero defects larger than ~20 nm across 40 layers and 15,000 mm², because a particle buried in the multilayer becomes a **phase defect** that prints and cannot be repaired. Only two companies have achieved this at production yield: **Hoya** (Japan, roughly two-thirds of the market) and **AGC** (Japan, the balance), a near-perfect duopoly that has held for a decade. An EUV blank is inspected on a **Lasertec ABICS** actinic (13.5 nm) blank-inspection tool, and its defect map ships with it so the mask shop can rotate or shift the pattern to hide any remaining defects under absorber. EUV blank prices are not published; industry estimates run from tens of thousands of dollars toward $100,000 each, which makes the blank alone more expensive than most finished DUV masks.

**Writing.** Mask data preparation converts the design's OPC- or ILT-corrected layer (Module 07) into a writer format and applies mask-process corrections. For decades masks were written by **variable-shaped-beam (VSB)** electron-beam tools (NuFlare EBM-9500, JEOL JBX-3200), which flash rectangles one at a time; write time scales with shot count and had grown to 20–40 hours for the most complex EUV layers, and it made the curvilinear patterns that ILT wants essentially unwritable. **Multi-beam mask writers** solved this: an array of ~262,144 (512 × 512) beamlets in IMS Nanofabrication's MBMW series or ~500,000 in NuFlare's MBM series are individually blanked as the stage scans, so write time is ~8–12 hours regardless of pattern complexity. IMS (Vienna, Austria; owned by Intel since 2017, with TSMC and Bain taking minority stakes in 2023) shipped the MBMW-101 in 2016, the **MBMW-201** in 2019, and the **MBMW-301** in 2023 for the 2 nm generation and High-NA; NuFlare (Japan, Toshiba group) followed with the **MBM-2000**, MBM-2000PLUS and, in 2023, the **MBM-3000** with 12 nm beamlets at 3.6 A/cm² and a ~10 hour write time. Each writer costs on the order of $30–50 million, and a leading mask shop runs a fleet.

The written resist (a chemically amplified e-beam resist, ~50–100 nm) is developed, the absorber is dry-etched (Cl2/O2 for Cr; chlorine then fluorine chemistries for TaBN), the resist is stripped, and the mask is cleaned, an especially delicate step for EUV because the Ru cap and the Mo/Si stack cannot tolerate the SPM chemistry used on DUV masks.

**Inspection, repair and metrology.** A DUV mask is inspected die-to-database on a KLA Teron 6xx-series 193 nm optical tool or an e-beam inspector. An EUV mask has a problem: a DUV inspector cannot see the phase defects or the small absorber defects that matter at 13.5 nm. The answer is **actinic patterned-mask inspection (APMI)** using an EUV source: **Lasertec's ACTIS A150** (introduced 2019, with a ~$100 million+ price) was for years the only such tool in the world and made Lasertec one of the most important small companies in the industry; the ACTIS A300 followed in November 2023 for High-NA (anamorphic) masks. Zeiss's **AIMS EUV** emulates the scanner's illumination to show what a defect will actually print as. Repair uses Zeiss **MeRiT** electron-beam gas-assisted etch and deposition (removing or adding absorber with ~10 nm precision), or Bruker's nanomachining. Registration (pattern placement) is measured on KLA IPRO or Zeiss PROVE tools to ~1 nm, and CD on Holon or Hitachi mask CD-SEMs. Each EUV mask spends days in this loop.

**Pellicles.** A **pellicle** is a thin transparent membrane stretched on a frame ~6 mm above the mask surface, so that any particle landing on it sits far out of the focal plane and does not print. DUV pellicles are ~0.8 µm fluoropolymer films with > 99% transmission and cost a few hundred dollars; they are on every DUV mask and nobody thinks about them. EUV pellicles are hard because nothing is transparent at 13.5 nm: the membrane must be ~20–50 nm thick, survive 500+ W of EUV in vacuum at hundreds of degrees Celsius, and be mechanically stable across 110 mm × 140 mm. ASML's original polysilicon design reached ~83% transmission; the current metal-silicide-coated polysilicon type, manufactured under license by **Mitsui Chemicals** since 2021, achieves ~88–92%. Because the light passes through twice (in and out), a 90% pellicle costs (0.9)² ≈ 81% of the dose, i.e. ~19% of scanner throughput, and it degrades with dose. **Carbon-nanotube (CNT)** pellicles developed at imec and being commercialized by Mitsui (targeting ≥ 94% transmission, with a 5,000-sheet-per-year line at Iwakuni-Ohtake scheduled for completion in December 2025) and by Canatu (Finland) are the next generation; membrane-only lab results exceed 97%. Because of the throughput penalty and the cost (EUV pellicles run on the order of tens of thousands of dollars each), TSMC ran its early EUV layers without pellicles and relied on frequent mask inspection instead; pellicle adoption has broadened as source power and yields have risen, and it is essentially mandatory for memory makers' high-volume layers.

**Cost and who makes them.** A binary DUV mask for a non-critical layer costs on the order of $5,000–20,000; a critical ArF-immersion att-PSM with aggressive OPC costs ~$50,000–150,000; an EUV mask costs on the order of $300,000–500,000 (and more for a High-NA mask), driven by the blank, the multi-beam write, and the actinic inspection loop. The three companies that consume the most masks, TSMC, Samsung and Intel, all run **captive mask shops** (TSMC's is believed to be the largest mask operation in the world by output; Intel's Mask Operations is in Hillsboro) that make essentially all of their leading-edge masks. The **merchant** mask makers, **Photronics** (US; with joint ventures PDMC in Taiwan with DNP and Xiamen in China), **Tekscend Photomask** (the former Toppan Photomask, sold by Toppan to the investment firm Integral in 2025), and **DNP** (Dai Nippon Printing), serve the fabless companies' DUV layers, foundries' mature nodes, and some EUV work (DNP and Tekscend both have EUV mask processes and the IBM joint development for 2 nm and High-NA masks); Hoya and Taiwan Mask Corp fill niches. Merchant EUV mask volume is small; the leading-edge mask business is, in practice, internal to the three companies who can afford it.

> **Worked example: the cost of one mask set at a 2 nm-class node.**
> A TSMC N2-class SoC uses roughly 80–100 mask layers. Take a representative split: ~25 EUV layers (fin/sheet, gate cut, contacts, the first ~8–10 metal levels and their vias) at ~$400,000 each; ~50 critical and semi-critical ArF-immersion PSM layers at ~$100,000; ~20 KrF/i-line layers (implants, pads, thick oxides) at ~$15,000.
> EUV: 25 × $400k = $10.0M. ArFi: 50 × $100k = $5.0M. KrF/i-line: 20 × $15k = $0.3M. Base set ≈ **$15M**. Add EUV pellicles (~25 × ~$25k ≈ $0.6M), a set of engineering-change spares and re-writes (no mask set survives first silicon unchanged; a metal-layer respin re-makes ~5–10 masks, $1–3M), plus qualification and inspection time, and a realistic all-in for the program is **$20–30M**, consistent with industry estimates; High-NA half-field masks would roughly double the EUV line item for the affected layers. Now amortize it. At ~$25,000–30,000 per N2 wafer, a product that ships 100,000 wafers adds only ~$250 per wafer in mask cost, under 1%. A product that ships 2,000 wafers (a specialty ASIC, a research chip) adds $12,500 per wafer, half the wafer's price. This is the arithmetic that drives every fabless company toward high-volume products or multi-project-wafer shuttles at leading nodes, and it is why a $20–30M mask set sits next to a $500M+ design cost in the economics of a 2 nm chip (Module 19).

### Why Japan dominates materials

Stepping back over the categories: Japanese firms hold ~90% of photoresists, ~100% of EUV mask blanks, ~55% of silicon wafers (Shin-Etsu and SUMCO), the majority of high-purity HF, colloidal silica for slurries, quartz, sputter targets, DRAM high-k precursors, dicing tape and die-attach film (Lintec, Nitto Denko, Resonac), and substrates' build-up film (Ajinomoto, Module 16). Estimates put Japan's overall share of wafer-fab materials at ~half by value, a share that has not fallen even as its share of chip production collapsed from ~50% in 1988 to under 10%.

The reasons are structural. Materials do not scale like chips; they scale like craft. The value in a resist or a blank is a decades-long accumulation of small process improvements against purity and defect specifications that tighten every node, in an industry where qualification takes 6–18 months and the customer will not requalify unless forced. That favors incumbents with patient capital, a supply base that grew up next to the dominant chipmakers of the 1980s (NEC, Toshiba, Hitachi, Fujitsu) and kept improving after they faded, and chemical majors sitting atop deep domestic fluorine, silicone and specialty-monomer chains. Korea's 2019 localization drive and China's current one show how slow replacement is: China's resist makers reached KrF in five years and are still years from ArF immersion, let alone EUV.

The implication for the supply chain is that the fab's risk register looks nothing like its cost sheet. Consumables are maybe 10–15% of the cost of a leading-edge wafer, but the Tohoku earthquake of March 2011 (which took out Shin-Etsu's Shirakawa wafer plant and MEMC's Utsunomiya plant, ~25% of world wafer supply, for weeks, and Mitsubishi Gas Chemical's BT resin plant), the 2019 Japan–Korea spat, the 2022 neon shock and the 2023 Ga/Ge controls all demonstrated that a fab is only as robust as its least substitutable bottle.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"SiC wafers are expensive because SiC is a rare material." → Reality:** Silicon and carbon are two of the most abundant elements on Earth. SiC substrates cost tens of times more than a silicon wafer of the same diameter (up to ~100× before the 2023–2025 price collapse) because SiC cannot be melted and pulled; it must be sublimed onto a seed at ~2,400 °C at a few hundred microns per hour, then sawn with diamond, and the yield is set by dislocations and polytype inclusions.
- **"GaN chargers use gallium nitride wafers." → Reality:** Almost all power GaN is a few microns of GaN grown by MOCVD on a 150–200 mm silicon wafer, processed in a silicon fab. Bulk GaN substrates exist but are small, cost thousands of dollars, and are used for lasers and R&D vertical devices.
- **"Strained silicon means silicon grown on SiGe." → Reality:** That was the 1990s virtual-substrate approach, which failed on defect density. Production strain since 2003 comes from embedded SiGe source/drain that squeezes the pMOS channel, from stress liners, and from the fin or nanosheet geometry, all without a relaxed SiGe buffer.
- **"Ultrapure water is just distilled water." → Reality:** Distilled water is ~1 MΩ·cm with ppm-level organics and dissolved gases. UPW is 18.2 MΩ·cm with organics below 1 ppb, metals below 1 ppt and particles counted per liter at 20 nm, produced by a nine-stage train (RO, EDI, mixed beds, 185 nm UV, ultrafiltration) and never allowed to stand still in a pipe.
- **"The EUV scanner is the chokepoint; everything else is commodity." → Reality:** An EUV layer also depends on Hoya/AGC blanks (a duopoly), a multi-beam writer (IMS/NuFlare duopoly), Lasertec actinic inspection (a monopoly), Mitsui pellicles, and TOK/JSR/Shin-Etsu resists. Any one of these is as hard to replace as ASML, and several are single-source.
- **"Mask cost is a rounding error at $20–30M when a fab costs $20B." → Reality:** It is a rounding error for a product shipping 100,000 wafers (~$250/wafer) and a business-killer for one shipping 2,000 (~$12,500/wafer, half the wafer price). Mask-set cost is what determines which chips can economically exist at a leading node.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 03 ended with a polished, epi-ready 300 mm silicon wafer; this module adds the other substrates that enter fabs beside it (150–200 mm SiC, GaN-on-Si and GaN-on-SiC epiwafers, 100–150 mm GaAs, 75–100 mm InP) and the entire stream of consumables that the front end will pour over those wafers: Japanese resists and ancillaries from JSR, TOK and Shin-Etsu; bulk nitrogen from an Air Liquide or Linde plant next door and NF3/WF6/HBr from SK Specialty and Resonac; ppt-grade acids from BASF, Stella Chemifa and Morita; ultrapure water from an on-site plant; slurries and pads from Entegris, Fujimi and DuPont/Qnity; targets from JX and Honeywell; precursors from Merck and Adeka; quartz and SiC parts from Heraeus and CoorsTek; and photomasks built on Hoya/AGC blanks, written on IMS/NuFlare tools and inspected on Lasertec, either in TSMC's own mask shop or at Photronics, Tekscend and DNP.
