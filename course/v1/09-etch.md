# Module 09: Etch: Transferring the Pattern

Lithography (Modules 07 and 08) leaves the wafer covered in a stencil: a few tens of nanometers of organic photoresist with holes and lines in it. The resist itself is useless. The transistor is made of silicon, silicon-germanium, silicon nitride, silicon dioxide, hafnium oxide, titanium nitride, tungsten, copper. Something has to cut the resist's pattern into those materials, in the right places only, to the right depth only, with vertical walls, without damaging what is underneath, and do it a few hundred times per wafer at a rate of tens of wafers per hour. That something is **etch**.

The reason etch is hard is a single, unavoidable tension. The mechanisms that remove material *selectively* (chemistry: a fluorine atom reacts with silicon and not with silicon dioxide, or vice versa) are inherently *isotropic*: a chemical reaction does not know which direction is "down." The mechanisms that remove material *directionally* (a beam of ions arriving perpendicular to the wafer) are inherently *unselective*: a 500 eV argon ion sputters whatever it lands on. Every serious etch process is an engineered compromise between these two, and the whole of plasma etching is essentially a 45-year effort to get the selectivity of chemistry with the directionality of ions.

As of ~2025 the stakes look like this. A leading-edge logic wafer (TSMC N3, N2) goes through on the order of 200 to 300 plasma etch and strip chamber visits among its roughly 1,000+ process steps. A 3D NAND wafer needs holes etched more than 60 times deeper than they are wide, through nearly 300 alternating film pairs, and those single etch steps take the better part of an hour per wafer and consume hundreds of chambers in a single fab. Etch equipment is a ~$20–25 billion per year market, roughly one-fifth of all wafer fab equipment spending, and it is dominated by three companies (Lam Research, Tokyo Electron, Applied Materials) with two Chinese entrants (AMEC, Naura) climbing fast.

## The Metrics: What an Etch Has to Deliver

Before mechanisms, vocabulary. Every etch recipe is judged on the following, and every one of them trades against the others.

**Etch rate** is removal per unit time, typically quoted in nm/min (or Å/min for slow, precise etches). Production dry etches run from ~1 nm/min (atomic layer etching) to ~1 µm/min (deep silicon etch); wet etches span a similar range. Rate sets throughput and therefore how many chambers a fab must buy.

**Selectivity** is the ratio of the etch rate of the material you want removed to the etch rate of a material you want preserved: the mask above it, or the layer beneath it (the **etch stop**). A gate etch might need poly-Si:SiO2 selectivity above 100:1 because it lands on a gate dielectric ~1–2 nm thick after a 30–50% over-etch. Selectivity to the mask determines how thick the mask must be.

**Anisotropy** describes directionality. If the lateral etch rate is r_l and vertical is r_v, anisotropy A = 1 − r_l / r_v. A purely chemical etch has A = 0 (isotropic); an ideal ion-driven etch has A = 1. Most production etches sit at 0.9–0.99 and the residual lateral component appears as **undercut** beneath the mask edge.

**Uniformity** is the variation of etch depth or CD across the wafer (typically quoted as 3σ or range as a percent of mean, and leading-edge specs are ~1–2% across 300 mm). Non-uniformity comes from radial gradients in plasma density, gas depletion from center to edge, temperature gradients on the chuck, and the electrical discontinuity at the wafer edge (the reason **edge rings** exist, discussed later).

**Loading** is the dependence of etch rate on the total exposed area of the material being etched: more open area consumes more reactant, so rate falls. **Microloading** is the local version: a dense array of lines etches at a different rate than an isolated line a few microns away, because the local reactant supply and byproduct removal differ. Both are fought by running at higher gas flow and lower pressure and by adding dummy patterns at layout time.

**ARDE**, **aspect-ratio-dependent etching** (also called RIE lag), is the observation that narrow, deep features etch more slowly than wide ones. Three causes stack up: the neutral radicals reach the bottom of a deep hole with Knudsen (molecular-flow) transport and are consumed on the sidewalls on the way down; the ion angular distribution has a finite spread, so some ions hit the sidewalls instead of the bottom; and the sidewalls charge positively (ions land on them) while the bottom charges negatively (electrons, being isotropic, hit near the top), deflecting subsequent ions. ARDE is the central enemy of contact and 3D NAND etching.

**CD bias** is the difference between the printed resist CD and the etched CD. It can be positive (feature grows because the mask eroded) or negative (feature shrinks because polymer built up on the sidewall). CD bias is not only tolerated but exploited: **etch trim** deliberately shrinks the resist line before transfer to reach a CD below what lithography can print, at the cost of line-edge roughness.

**Profile** defects have their own vocabulary. **Bowing** is a sidewall that bulges outward below the mask, caused by ions scattered off the mask facet or by insufficient passivation near the top of a deep hole. **Notching** is a lateral gouge at the very bottom of a feature where it meets an insulating etch stop; it comes from charge accumulation on the insulator deflecting ions sideways into the foot of the line, and it is worst on the last line of a dense array facing open space. **Footing** is the opposite of notching: material left at the base, a small fillet where the feature meets the substrate, from too much passivation or too little over-etch. **Micro-trenching** is a small extra-deep groove at the base of each sidewall, from ions grazing the sidewall at a small angle and being specularly reflected down into the corner, concentrating flux there. **Faceting** is the beveling of the mask's top corners by sputtering, which then reflects ions into the feature and contributes to bowing.

**Damage** covers everything the plasma does that you did not ask for: ions amorphizing the top few nanometers of silicon, vacuum-ultraviolet photons creating traps in gate dielectrics, charge accumulation punching through thin oxides, carbon depletion in porous low-k dielectrics, and residues (fluorocarbon polymer, metal halides) that must be cleaned.

Keep this list in mind: every chemistry and every hardware choice below exists because it moves one of these metrics.

## Wet Etch: Still the Workhorse for Selectivity

Wet etching was the only etching until plasma etchers entered fabs in the mid-1970s, and it remains everywhere in the fab wherever selectivity matters more than directionality: removing sacrificial layers, stripping hardmasks, recessing films, cleaning. Its great virtue is chemistry-limited selectivity, which can reach 1,000:1 or more; its great vice is isotropy.

### Hydrofluoric acid and silicon dioxide

Silicon dioxide is chemically robust (the Si–O bond is ~4.8 eV, among the strongest single bonds in the periodic table) but it has one specific vulnerability: fluorine forms an even stronger bond with silicon, and the product SiF4 is a gas or, in water, a soluble complex. The textbook equation is

SiO2 + 6 HF → H2SiF6 + 2 H2O

where hexafluorosilicic acid, H2SiF6, is fully water-soluble. The mechanistic reality is that the etching species is not the neutral HF molecule so much as the bifluoride ion HF2⁻ and HF dimers, whose attack on the Si–O bond is much faster; this is why the etch rate depends strongly on pH and on the ratio of HF to fluoride salts.

**Buffered oxide etch (BOE)**, typically 6:1 or 7:1 by volume of 40% NH4F to 49% HF, exists because pure HF has two defects: its etch rate drifts as HF is consumed, and it lifts photoresist. The ammonium fluoride buffers the pH (holding the HF2⁻ concentration steady) and keeps the resist adhering. 6:1 BOE etches thermal oxide at ~100 nm/min at room temperature; 49% HF etches it at ~2 µm/min; dilute HF (**DHF**, 100:1 water:HF) at ~2–3 nm/min, which is the range used for controlled recesses and pre-gate cleans where removing 1 nm too much matters. Deposited oxides (PECVD, TEOS) etch faster than thermal oxide because they are less dense and contain hydrogen; an un-annealed PECVD oxide can etch 3–10x faster, and the **wet etch rate ratio (WERR)** to thermal oxide is in fact the standard quick quality metric for a deposited oxide.

Selectivity is the point: HF etches SiO2 at ~100 nm/min while barely touching silicon (it does strip the native oxide and leaves a hydrogen-terminated surface, which is why HF-last cleans precede epitaxy and gate stack formation) and silicon nitride at ~1 nm/min in BOE. That is a ~100:1 selectivity that no plasma achieves with the same ease.

### Hot phosphoric acid and silicon nitride

Silicon nitride is the fab's favorite hard mask and etch stop precisely because so little attacks it. The one thing that does, selectively, is concentrated (85%) phosphoric acid at 150–180 °C, typically ~160 °C, which hydrolyzes Si3N4 to silicic acid and ammonia at ~4–6 nm/min while etching SiO2 at only 0.1–0.5 nm/min: a selectivity of 10–50:1 that depends critically on how much dissolved silica the bath already contains. A fresh bath etches oxide too fast; a silica-saturated bath is more selective but precipitates. Fabs therefore manage the silica concentration deliberately (some tools spike the bath with silicate, others cycle it). Hot phosphoric baths are corrosive, boil off water (the water content sets the etch rate, so it is replenished continuously), and are hard to make single-wafer, so nitride strip remains one of the last big batch wet processes. Its most famous modern use is the "nitride pull" in 3D NAND: after the channel holes and slits are cut, hot phosphoric flows into the slit and dissolves every sacrificial nitride layer of the ONON stack laterally over hundreds of nanometers, leaving air gaps that are then filled with tungsten word lines. This is an isotropic etch used on purpose.

### Anisotropic silicon etch: KOH and TMAH

Wet etch is not always isotropic. Alkaline etchants (potassium hydroxide, **tetramethylammonium hydroxide, TMAH**) etch silicon by a mechanism in which hydroxide attacks surface Si–Si bonds and the rate depends on how many bonds a surface atom exposes. The (111) plane presents atoms with three back-bonds into the crystal and one dangling bond; (100) surface atoms have two back-bonds and two dangling bonds. The result is an etch that is 30–400x faster on (100) than on (111). Etching through a square mask opening on a (100) wafer therefore produces a pit bounded by (111) planes at 54.74° to the surface, self-terminating in a V-groove or, if stopped early, a flat-bottomed trapezoidal trench. 30% KOH at 80 °C etches (100) silicon at ~1 µm/min; 25% TMAH at 80–90 °C at ~0.5–1 µm/min with lower (111) selectivity but no potassium (a mobile ion that destroys MOS devices), which is why TMAH is the CMOS-compatible choice. These etches are the basis of bulk-micromachined MEMS (pressure sensor diaphragms, inkjet nozzles, cantilevers) and are used in the CMOS flow for things like recessing the silicon under a channel or forming sigma-shaped source/drain cavities (a dual-step dry-then-TMAH etch that produces a {111}-faceted cavity for embedded SiGe stressors).

### The isotropic undercut problem

The reason wet etch cannot pattern a modern transistor is geometric. If a film of thickness t is etched isotropically through a mask opening of width w, the etch proceeds sideways under the mask by t on each side, so the finished feature is w + 2t wide with rounded sidewalls. For a 20 nm line in a 20 nm film the undercut is 100% of the line. There is no fix: an isotropic etch cannot resolve features smaller than roughly twice the film thickness. Wet etching survives in the front end only where the geometry is inherently blanket (strip a whole film) or where the undercut is the intent (release a nanosheet, pull a nitride).

### Batch versus single-wafer wet tools

Wet processing hardware comes in two families. **Batch wet benches** immerse a cassette of 25 or 50 wafers in a sequence of quartz or PFA tanks (etch, rinse, dry). They have excellent throughput and low chemical cost per wafer, and remain the tool of choice for long, hot etches like phosphoric nitride strip and for bulk cleans. Their weaknesses are cross-contamination between wafers in the same bath, bath aging (etch rate drifts as the bath loads with byproduct), and poor control on the sub-nanometer scale. **Single-wafer spin tools** hold one wafer on a spinning chuck under a nozzle arm and dispense fresh chemistry across the surface, giving repeatable, fresh-chemistry, tightly controlled removal: a 100:1 DHF recess of 1.5 nm is a single-wafer job. SCREEN (SU-3300 single-wafer series; FC-3100 batch), Tokyo Electron (CELLESTA single-wafer; EXPEDIUS batch), Lam (DV-Prime and the Da Vinci family, single-wafer clean and etch) and SEMES (Samsung's affiliate) dominate; SCREEN alone holds roughly 40–50% of the wet clean and etch tool market. The industry trend since the 90 nm node has been steadily toward single-wafer, with batch surviving for the long hot etches.

## Plasma Physics for Engineers

Dry etching solves the undercut problem by supplying directional ions from a plasma. An engineer needs a working model of what that plasma is, and the model is not complicated.

### Making a plasma

Fill a chamber with gas at 1–100 mTorr (a thousandth to a tenth of a percent of atmosphere). Apply radio-frequency power, historically at **13.56 MHz**, a frequency chosen because it is an ISM (industrial, scientific, medical) band where unlimited radiation is legal, not for any physical reason. Free electrons (there are always a few, from cosmic rays) are accelerated by the RF electric field; because they are ~70,000 times lighter than an argon ion, they respond to the field while ions barely move. An electron that reaches ~15 eV can ionize an argon atom (ionization energy 15.76 eV), producing another electron, and the population avalanches until losses (recombination on the walls) balance creation. The result is a weakly ionized gas: an **ionization fraction** of only 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻³, meaning for every ion there are a thousand to a million neutral molecules.

This gas has two temperatures. The electrons, which absorb the RF energy, have a Maxwellian-ish distribution with an **electron temperature** of 2–5 eV (1 eV corresponds to 11,600 K, so the electrons are at ~30,000–60,000 K). The ions and neutrals, which collide too rarely to equilibrate with electrons, stay near room temperature (~300–500 K). This is why plasma etching is "cold" processing: the wafer can sit at 20 °C while 40,000 K electrons dissociate CF4 into CF3 and F radicals above it. The electrons are hot enough to break any molecular bond (C–F is 5.6 eV) and to excite atoms into states that emit visible light, which is why plasmas glow and why optical emission works for endpoint.

**Ion density** ranges from ~10⁹–10¹⁰ cm⁻³ in a capacitively coupled plasma to 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻³ in an inductively coupled one. For scale, the neutral gas at 10 mTorr has a density of ~3×10¹⁴ cm⁻³. The **radical** density (F, Cl, O, CFx: neutral fragments with unpaired electrons, made when electrons break molecules) is generally 10–1,000x the ion density, which is the first hint that the chemistry of a plasma etch is dominated by neutrals and the directionality by ions.

### The sheath and DC self-bias

The single most important concept in plasma etching is the **sheath**. Because electrons move so much faster than ions, any surface in contact with the plasma is bombarded by electrons first and charges negative until it repels enough electrons to balance the ion arrival. The plasma bulk sits at a positive **plasma potential** V_p (typically 10–30 V above the walls), and a thin electron-depleted boundary layer forms at every surface across which this potential drops. This is the sheath: dark (no electrons to excite light), 0.1–several mm thick (a few Debye lengths, ~100 µm at 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ and 3 eV, thickening as the voltage across it grows), and containing a strong electric field pointing into the surface.

Ions that drift to the sheath edge at the **Bohm velocity** (roughly √(kT_e/M), about 2.7 km/s for argon at 3 eV) are then accelerated straight across the sheath, perpendicular to the wafer, gaining energy equal to the sheath voltage. Because the sheath is thin compared with the ion mean free path at low pressure, ions cross it almost without collision and arrive within a few degrees of normal. This is the origin of anisotropy: the plasma manufactures a beam of ions aimed at the wafer, out of a gas that is otherwise isotropic.

The **DC self-bias** turns a modest sheath into a powerful one. Apply RF to the wafer electrode through a blocking capacitor. During the positive half-cycle the electrode collects a huge electron current; during the negative half-cycle only a small ion current. Since the capacitor cannot pass net DC current, the electrode's average potential must drift negative until electron and ion currents over a cycle balance. The result is a negative DC offset V_dc on the powered electrode that scales with RF voltage and with electrode asymmetry (a small powered electrode facing a large grounded chamber gets the largest bias). Ions then arrive at the wafer with energy ~e(V_p − V_dc), which can be tuned from ~20 eV to several kilovolts. In a capacitive plasma the sheath at the small electrode carries most of the RF voltage.

> **Worked example: ion energy and flux at the wafer.**
> A dielectric etch chamber runs 2 MHz bias power that develops a self-bias of −1,200 V on the chuck, with plasma potential +25 V. Ions arrive with up to ~1,225 eV; because 2 MHz is below the argon ion plasma frequency (about 3 MHz at 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, computed from f_pi = (1/2π)·√(n e²/ε₀ M)), the ions actually follow the oscillating sheath and arrive with a broad bimodal distribution between roughly 0 and ~2,400 eV peak. Meanwhile, ion flux is set by the plasma density, not the bias: with n = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ and Bohm velocity 2.7×10⁵ cm/s, the Bohm flux is Γ ≈ 0.61·n·u_B ≈ 1.6×10¹⁵ ions cm⁻² s⁻¹, or ~0.26 mA/cm² of ion current. A monolayer of SiO2 is ~10¹⁵ atoms/cm²; at one atom removed per ion (a high ion-enhanced yield), this flux removes about one monolayer per second, ~0.3 nm/s, ~20 nm/min. That is the raw ion budget, and it explains why dielectric etches that need 200 nm/min must run at 10x that density with a yield well above one atom per ion, delivered by chemistry.

### Frequency, ion energy versus ion flux, and dual-frequency CCP

The two knobs a process engineer wants are **ion flux** (how many ions per second, which drives etch rate) and **ion energy** (how hard they hit, which drives anisotropy, yield, and damage). In a single-frequency capacitive reactor these are tied together: raising the RF power raises both the density and the sheath voltage. That coupling was the central limitation of 1980s **reactive ion etching (RIE)** tools.

The escape comes from the different masses of electrons and ions. The **electron plasma frequency** at 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ is ~900 MHz, so electrons follow any RF field used in etching. The ion plasma frequency is a few MHz, so ions respond to fields below ~3–5 MHz but only to the time-averaged field above ~10 MHz. Therefore:

- A high frequency (27, 40, 60, 100 MHz) heats electrons efficiently and generates plasma density (ion flux) but, because ions cannot follow it and because sheath voltage falls with rising frequency at a given power, adds little ion energy.
- A low frequency (2 MHz, 400 kHz, sometimes 13.56 MHz used as the "low" one) generates a large sheath voltage and directly modulates ion energy, and ions follow it, giving high-energy, broad-distribution bombardment.

A **dual-frequency capacitively coupled plasma (CCP)** applies both to the wafer electrode (or one to each electrode): 60 MHz for density, 2 MHz or 400 kHz for energy. Lam's Flex and TEL's Vigus dielectric etchers are built on this principle, and 3D NAND channel-hole etchers push the low-frequency power to tens of kilowatts to drive ions to multi-keV energies down 10 µm holes. Triple-frequency chambers (for example 100 MHz / 13.56 MHz / 400 kHz) exist for even finer decoupling.

### CCP versus ICP versus ECR

**Capacitively coupled plasma (CCP)**: two parallel plate electrodes, the upper one usually a gas **showerhead** made of silicon, the wafer on the lower one. Density 10⁹–10¹⁰ cm⁻³, pressure 10–200 mTorr, ion energies up to several keV. The high ion energy and modest radical density make CCP the platform for **dielectric etch** (SiO2, Si3N4, low-k), where you must break 4.8 eV Si–O bonds and must protect against lateral etching with polymer.

**Inductively coupled plasma (ICP)**: RF (13.56 MHz, or 2 MHz in some TEL and Lam designs) is driven through a planar or cylindrical coil sitting outside a dielectric window on top of the chamber. The coil's oscillating magnetic field induces an azimuthal electric field inside the chamber that heats electrons like the secondary of a transformer, generating density 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻³ at 1–20 mTorr. Crucially, the wafer chuck has its own independent RF bias (13.56 or 2 MHz), so ion energy (bias) and ion flux (coil power) are fully separated. Low pressure means a long ion mean free path and a tight ion angular distribution; high density means high radical flux and fast rates at low ion energy (20–200 eV). ICP is the platform for **conductor etch**: silicon, poly, SiGe, metals, hardmasks, where the material is easy to etch chemically and the challenge is precision and low damage. Lam Kiyo, TEL's ICP chambers, and AMAT's Centura-based silicon etch chambers are ICPs. (TEL also fields a microwave surface-wave source, **RLSA**, radial line slot antenna, at 2.45 GHz, which gives very low electron temperature and hence low damage.)

**Electron cyclotron resonance (ECR)**: 2.45 GHz microwaves injected into a chamber with an 875 gauss magnetic field, where electrons gyrate at exactly the microwave frequency and absorb energy resonantly, giving high density at very low pressure (<1 mTorr). ECR was important in the 1990s and survives at Hitachi High-Tech, whose ECR-based conductor etchers hold a meaningful niche in gate and fin etching (Hitachi is the fourth etch vendor after the big three). Most of the industry has moved to ICP because it is simpler and scales to 300 mm more easily.

**Wafer temperature** is a first-class knob in all of them. The wafer sits on an **electrostatic chuck (ESC)** that clamps it electrostatically; helium at a few Torr is flowed into the gap behind the wafer to conduct heat to the temperature-controlled chuck, since the chamber vacuum would otherwise insulate the wafer thermally. Modern ESCs have dozens of independent heater zones used to correct etch non-uniformity, and chiller ranges span from −70 °C (cryogenic dielectric etch) to +150 °C.

**Pulsed plasma**: switching source or bias power on and off at 1–10 kHz with a controllable duty cycle. During the off period electrons cool and charge accumulated in features neutralizes, and the time-averaged ion energy can be set independently of the peak. Synchronous pulsing is standard on leading-edge conductor etchers to reduce notching and charging damage.

## Etch Mechanisms: Chemical, Physical, and the Synergy Between Them

### Pure chemical etching (radicals)

A fluorine atom hitting silicon reacts spontaneously at room temperature: 4 F + Si → SiF4, with SiF4 a gas at −86 °C boiling point that desorbs immediately. A remote plasma of CF4 or SF6 or NF3, with the ions filtered out, etches silicon isotropically at hundreds of nm/min with high selectivity to SiO2 (F barely attacks SiO2 thermally). XeF2 vapor does the same without any plasma at all, and is used to release MEMS structures. This is chemical etching: fast, selective, gentle, isotropic. It cannot make a vertical sidewall, but it is exactly what you want to remove a sacrificial layer.

### Pure physical etching (sputtering)

Fire 500 eV argon ions at a surface. Each ion transfers momentum in a collision cascade and knocks out ~0.5–1.5 surface atoms (the **sputter yield**, which has a threshold of 20–50 eV and rises roughly with √energy). This is **ion milling**: perfectly directional, nearly zero selectivity (all materials sputter within a factor of ~3 of each other), slow, and dirty, because sputtered atoms are not volatile and redeposit on sidewalls and mask. It also facets the mask corners. Pure sputter etching is used only for materials with no volatile compounds (some magnetic and noble-metal films for MRAM, for instance) and for the argon "breakthrough" step that clears native oxide at the start of an etch.

### Ion-enhanced etching: the Coburn and Winters experiment

The whole field rests on an experiment John Coburn and Harold Winters published from IBM San Jose in 1979. They exposed silicon to a beam of XeF2 gas alone and measured the etch rate: about 5 Å/min. They exposed it to a 450 eV Ar⁺ ion beam alone: about 2 Å/min of sputtering. Then they turned on both together and measured about 55 Å/min, roughly eight times the sum. Switching the XeF2 off dropped the rate back to sputtering; switching the ions off dropped it back to the slow chemical rate. This is **ion-enhanced etching**, and the synergy is the reason plasma etching works at all.

The mechanism has several contributions and their relative weight depends on the system. Ions break surface bonds and amorphize the top ~1–3 nm, creating dangling bonds that adsorb F more readily and raising the reaction rate. Ions supply the energy to form and desorb volatile products (SiF4 from a fluorinated SiFx surface layer), which is the rate-limiting step in the thermal reaction. Ions sputter away non-volatile or slow-desorbing products and any contaminating layer (native oxide, polymer) that would block the chemistry. Ions also physically mix reactant into the surface. The consequence is that where ions land (the horizontal surfaces) the etch is fast; where they do not (vertical sidewalls) the etch proceeds at the slow chemical rate. If the chemical rate is low enough, or the sidewall is protected, the result is a vertical profile.

The most important variable in an ion-enhanced etch is therefore the ratio of neutral (radical) flux to ion flux at the surface. Neutral-rich: fast, chemical-dominated, less anisotropic. Ion-rich: slower, physical-dominated, more anisotropic, more damage. Every chamber and recipe knob (pressure, power, frequency, gas mix, temperature) ultimately tunes this ratio and the ion energy.

### Sidewall passivation

For most etches the spontaneous chemical rate on the sidewall is not zero, and something must protect the sidewall. That something is a deliberately deposited **passivation layer**, and the choice of passivation chemistry is what distinguishes the major etch families.

**Fluorocarbon chemistry for oxide and nitride.** Feed gases such as C4F8, C4F6, CHF3, CH2F2 or CF4 (with Ar as a diluent and O2 as a polymer trimmer) dissociate into CFx radicals that polymerize on every surface into a thin fluorocarbon film, plus F atoms. Where ions land, the film is continuously bombarded and thinned; on the sidewalls it accumulates to a few nanometers and blocks F attack. The elegance is that the film's steady-state thickness on the bottom depends on the substrate: on SiO2, the oxygen liberated by etching reacts with the polymer's carbon to make CO, CO2 and COF2, consuming the polymer so it stays thin (~1 nm) and the etch proceeds through it. On silicon or silicon nitride, there is no oxygen to consume the carbon, the polymer thickens until ions can no longer penetrate it, and the etch stops. This is the origin of oxide-to-silicon and oxide-to-nitride selectivity of 10–30:1 in a contact etch. The rule of thumb is the fluorine-to-carbon ratio of the gas: high F/C (CF4, F/C = 4) etches everything with little selectivity; low F/C (C4F6, F/C = 1.5) deposits heavily and is very selective but risks etch stop in the hole. Engineers tune the mix, often adding H2 (which scavenges F as HF, effectively lowering F/C) or O2 (which burns polymer, raising it). The CFx film is also what protects the top corner of the mask and what, when it builds up too far in a narrow hole, causes twisting and etch stop.

**HBr/O2 (and Cl2) chemistry for silicon.** Bromine etches silicon via SiBr4 (boiling point 153 °C, so it desorbs readily under ion bombardment but not spontaneously), and bromine's spontaneous reaction with silicon at room temperature is very slow, so the sidewall rate is intrinsically low. Adding a few percent O2 forms a thin SiOxBry passivation film on the sidewalls, robust enough to give near-perfectly vertical profiles. Chlorine (SiCl4, boiling point 58 °C) is more reactive and etches faster but with more lateral attack, so poly gate main etches typically use Cl2/HBr mixes and finish in pure HBr/O2. Because HBr/O2 barely etches SiO2 (bromine does not attack the Si–O bond without high ion energy, and the O2 oxidizes any exposed oxide further), selectivity to the gate oxide exceeds 100:1 in the "soft landing" step, which is the whole reason a gate can be etched onto a 1 nm dielectric.

**Cl2/BCl3 for aluminum and other metals.** Aluminum chloride, AlCl3, sublimes at ~180 °C, so chlorine etches aluminum readily (spontaneously, in fact, which is a problem). But aluminum is always covered by 2–5 nm of native Al2O3 that chlorine cannot penetrate; BCl3 is added because boron reduces the oxide (forming BOCl and B2O3-like products) and BCl3 scavenges water and oxygen in the chamber. Sidewall passivation comes from the resist's own carbon (CHCl3 or N2 additives were used to enhance it). After etch the wafer must be immediately stripped and rinsed, because residual chlorine on the sidewalls combines with atmospheric moisture to form HCl that corrodes the aluminum lines. Titanium nitride (the gate metal and BEOL hardmask) etches in Cl2/BCl3 as TiCl4; tantalum and tungsten etch in fluorine or chlorine (WF6 is a gas at room temperature, which is why tungsten is the easiest metal to etch and the standard for word lines and contact plugs). Copper has no volatile halide at practical wafer temperatures (CuCl2 needs well above 200 °C to leave the surface), which is why copper is never etched in production and the entire BEOL uses the damascene approach (Module 12): etch the dielectric, fill with copper, polish. The same holds for cobalt and ruthenium, though subtractive etching of Ru and Mo lines is being developed for future nodes.

### The Bosch process: deep silicon etching for MEMS and TSVs

Time-multiplexing the etch and passivation steps rather than running them simultaneously is the idea patented by Franz Laermer and Andrea Schilp of Robert Bosch GmbH in the mid-1990s, hence the **Bosch process**. In an ICP chamber, alternate: (1) a few seconds of SF6 plasma at low bias, in which F radicals etch silicon isotropically and fast (several µm/min); (2) a few seconds of C4F8 plasma, which conformally coats the entire feature with fluorocarbon polymer; then back to (1), where the ion bombardment (a modest bias of tens of volts) clears the polymer from the horizontal bottom but not from the sidewalls, so the next isotropic etch bite proceeds downward from the cleared floor. Each cycle removes 0.3–1 µm and leaves a rounded lateral bite in the sidewall; the resulting periodic ripples are **scallops**, typically 100–500 nm in period and tens of nanometers deep, and can be reduced (to ~10 nm) by shortening the cycle at the cost of throughput. The Bosch process delivers ~5–30 µm/min average rates in production (higher in specialized high-rate chambers), aspect ratios of 30–50:1 in production and >100:1 in the lab, and selectivity to oxide masks above 100:1. It is the etch behind through-silicon vias (a 10 µm diameter, 100 µm deep TSV in an HBM die or a CoWoS interposer takes several minutes), MEMS inertial sensors and microphones, and silicon photonics. Tools: Lam Syndion (TSV), the Omega Rapier and DSi product lines from SPTS (now part of KLA), Plasma-Therm, Oxford Instruments, and AMEC for TSV.

**Cryogenic silicon etch** is the continuous alternative, invented at Hitachi (Tachi, 1988): SF6/O2 at −100 to −120 °C, where a SiOxFy layer condenses on the sidewalls and is stable only at that temperature, giving smooth (scallop-free) vertical walls. It never displaced Bosch for MEMS because the temperature control and the wafer's thermal cycling were operationally painful, but the *idea* of cryogenic passivation returned three decades later, for dielectrics, and now dominates 3D NAND, as described below.

## The Critical Etches in a Modern Flow

The best way to understand the constraints is to walk through the specific etches of a leading-edge process.

### STI: shallow trench isolation

The first etch on a logic wafer. A pad oxide (~5–10 nm) and a thick nitride (~50–100 nm) are deposited, patterned, and the stack plus the silicon is etched to form trenches that will be filled with oxide to isolate neighboring transistors. In a planar process the trench is 300–400 nm deep; in a FinFET or nanosheet process the STI etch is effectively the fin etch (the trench between fins defines the fins). The recipe is an ICP HBr/Cl2/O2 etch with three requirements: a slight taper (85–88°, not fully vertical) so the subsequent oxide fill has no void; rounded top and bottom corners (sharp corners concentrate electric field and stress and cause leakage), obtained by a brief isotropic or high-pressure step; and smooth sidewalls, since the trench wall is also the fin sidewall on which the transistor channel will conduct. A liner oxidation after etch consumes the damaged top few nanometers of silicon.

### Fin etch by SAQP hardmask transfer

At N7 through N3, fins are defined by **self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP)** (Module 07): one lithographic pitch is divided by four via two rounds of spacer deposition and etch. The etch flow is a sequence of pattern transfers through a hardmask stack that typically looks like, from top: photoresist / Si-containing anti-reflective coating / spin-on carbon / SiN or SiON / amorphous carbon (Applied's APF, or a boron-doped carbon) / pad nitride / pad oxide / silicon. Each transfer has its own chemistry: the spacers (SiO2 or SiN, deposited by ALD at ~5–10 nm) are etched with a highly anisotropic fluorocarbon etch that must remove the horizontal film without thinning the vertical spacer or etching into the mandrel; the mandrels (amorphous silicon or carbon) are removed selectively; the final carbon mask is opened with O2/N2 or COS/O2. The fin etch itself must produce a fin 6–7 nm wide and 100–120 nm tall (of which only the top ~50–60 nm is exposed above the STI as the active fin), an aspect ratio of ~15–20:1, at a pitch of ~26–30 nm, with sidewall angle >88°, and with fin width matched between the two spacer generations. **Pitch walking** (the two families of spacers landing at slightly different spacing, so alternate fins have different widths and spacings) is a mask problem that shows up as fin CD variation, and a source of variation that etch cannot fix. The fin CD uniformity spec is at the ~1 nm level, and at N2 the nanosheet stack (alternating Si/SiGe epi, Module 11) is etched into "fins" with the same flow, now with EUV single-print or SADP replacing SAQP.

### Gate etch and the footing problem

The **dummy gate** (poly-Si or amorphous Si, ~60–100 nm, that will be replaced by the metal gate in the replacement-metal-gate flow) is patterned with a nitride/oxide hardmask and etched in a multi-step ICP recipe. The steps are: a fluorocarbon or CF4 **breakthrough** to punch through native oxide on the poly; a Cl2/HBr **main etch** at high rate; a **soft-landing** step in HBr/O2 at low bias as the etch approaches the gate dielectric; and an **over-etch** at even lower bias. The over-etch is the crux in a 3D geometry: the poly wraps over the fins, so when the etch front reaches the top of the fins it still has to clear the entire fin height (~100 nm) of poly running down the fin sidewalls and in the corners between fins and STI, all while the gate dielectric on the fin tops is exposed to plasma. That 100+ nm of extra etch is done at ~100:1 selectivity to a 2 nm dielectric. Gate CD control is at the ~1 nm level, LER below 2 nm, and the profile must be vertical: **footing** (a fillet of poly at the base of the gate, caused by excessive SiOxBry passivation accumulating where the sidewall meets the substrate, or by insufficient over-etch) lengthens the effective gate and creates a short between adjacent gates; **notching** at the base (charging on the exposed dielectric deflecting ions) undercuts the gate and shortens it. The gate etch is where pulsed plasmas earned their place, because the notching mechanism is fundamentally a charge accumulation problem, and where the fin etch's residual roughness comes back to bite: a rough fin makes for a poly sidewall that etches unevenly.

### Spacer etch

A conformal dielectric (SiN, SiOCN, SiBCN, 5–10 nm, deposited by ALD at low temperature) is deposited over the gate, then etched anisotropically so that it is removed from all horizontal surfaces and remains only on the gate sidewalls, where it sets the offset between gate edge and source/drain implant or epi. The etch (typically CH3F/O2 or CH3F/CH4/O2, with hydrofluorocarbon polymer providing the selectivity) must clear the film from the fin tops and the STI without recessing the silicon of the fin (which becomes the source/drain), without thinning the spacer laterally (which would change the gate-to-S/D distance), and without leaving spacer residue on the fin sidewalls, which would block the source/drain epi. The 3D geometry again hurts: the spacer film on the fin sidewalls is "vertical" from the point of view of the etch and does not clear, so an extra step is needed to remove it, and that step attacks the gate spacer. The precision required here (sub-nanometer spacer thickness control, zero recess) is one of the two places where atomic layer etching first entered production.

### Contact hole etch

The **contact** (the tungsten or cobalt plug connecting the source/drain and gate to metal 1) requires a hole ~20–25 nm in diameter through 100–200 nm of oxide, an aspect ratio of ~8–10:1, landing on a titanium silicide or the S/D epi. It is a dual-frequency CCP fluorocarbon etch (C4F6/C4F8 with Ar and O2), using the CFx polymer selectivity to stop on the thin SiN **contact etch stop layer (CESL)** and then a separate nitride etch to open it. In the **self-aligned contact (SAC)** scheme used since the 22 nm node, a SiN cap sits on top of the metal gate and the contact hole is intentionally printed overlapping the gate; oxide-to-nitride selectivity of >20:1 lets the etch open the hole between gates while the cap protects the gate from shorting. What can go wrong: the hole **twists** or wanders off its center at depth, driven by asymmetric polymer deposition and by charging, so the hole misses the target; the polymer clogs the top of the hole (necking) and the etch stops (**etch stop**), leaving an open contact that shows up as a single-bit failure in an SRAM; ARDE makes the smallest holes (or the isolated ones) shallower than the large ones so the over-etch must be tuned to the slowest hole; bowing widens the middle of the hole so that adjacent contacts short. At N3 and beyond the contact is small enough that the entire hole is near the ion angular spread, and etch rate is sub-100 nm/min.

### The 3D NAND channel hole: the hardest etch in the industry

A 3D NAND array (Module 15) is built by depositing a stack of alternating SiO2 and Si3N4 films (the **ONON stack**), ~28–35 nm each pair, then etching a vertical hole through the entire stack, lining it with the charge-trap layers and a polysilicon channel, and later replacing the nitride with tungsten word lines. The stack thickness as of ~2025 is dizzying: 200–300 pairs (Samsung V9 at 286 layers, SK hynix at 321, Micron G9 at 276, Kioxia/SanDisk BiCS8 at 218, YMTC at ~270–300) stacked in two or three separately-etched decks. Each deck is 4–6 µm; a full stack is 8–10 µm; and the hole is ~100 nm in diameter at the top and must be within a few nanometers of that at the bottom, an aspect ratio of 60–100:1. The etch is done in a dual- or triple-frequency CCP with tens of kilowatts of low-frequency (400 kHz to 2 MHz) bias power to accelerate ions to several keV, through a 2–3 µm thick amorphous or boron-doped carbon hardmask that is itself eroded at a selectivity of only ~4–8:1 (which is why the mask must be so thick, and which sets a hard ceiling on how deep a single deck can be).

Everything that can go wrong in an etch goes wrong here, at once. ARDE makes the etch rate collapse as the hole deepens (the rate at 8 µm can be half the rate at 1 µm). Bowing at the top from mask faceting creates a hole wider in the middle than the top, so that neighbors merge. Twisting at the bottom moves the hole off center by tens of nanometers. Tilting of the whole hole across the wafer (systematic, worse at the wafer edge, caused by sheath curvature near the edge ring) misaligns it with the underlying source plate. Charging at the bottom of a 60:1 insulating hole is severe. The oxide and nitride layers etch at different rates and with different sidewall passivation, so the hole wall develops a periodic corrugation. The ion angular distribution must be within a degree or two of normal, or the ions never reach the bottom. And the throughput is dreadful: at a conventional etch rate of 100–150 nm/min a 10 µm hole takes 60–100 minutes of single-wafer chamber time, for a single step, per wafer.

The 2023–2025 breakthrough was **cryogenic dielectric etch**. Cooling the wafer to −40 to −70 °C (Lam Cryo 3.0, introduced 2024; TEL's channel-hole process, introduced 2023) fundamentally changes the surface chemistry. Instead of carbon-rich fluorocarbon polymer, the etch uses hydrogen fluoride-based chemistry (TEL's is HF with PF3 as a co-reactant; Lam's is also a fluorine-hydrogen chemistry) in which HF physisorbs onto the cold surface as a condensed, thick reactive layer that would immediately re-evaporate at room temperature. The ion bombardment then drives a reaction that looks a lot like the wet HF etch (SiO2 + 4 HF → SiF4 + 2 H2O, with nitride going to SiF4 and ammonium fluoride-like products), which needs far less ion energy per atom removed than breaking Si–O bonds through polymer, and which proceeds almost as fast at the bottom of the hole as at the top because the reactant arrives by condensation rather than ion-driven activation. The results claimed by the vendors: TEL reports a 10 µm deep, high-aspect-ratio etch in 33 minutes, about 2.5 times faster than its conventional process, with an 84% reduction in global-warming-potential (much less C4F6/C4F8, with HF replacing ~90% of the fluorocarbon gas); Lam claims 2.5x the etch rate of conventional dielectric etch, sub-0.1% top-to-bottom CD deviation over a 10 µm hole, 40% less energy per wafer and up to 90% lower emissions. Lam also notes that Cryo 3.0 is its third cryogenic generation and that earlier generations had already processed more than five million wafers before the 2024 announcement, so "cryogenic" was in production before it was a headline. SK hynix has publicly said it will use TEL's cryogenic process for 400+ layer NAND, with TEL targeting low-volume production in 2025 and high volume in 2026. Cryogenic etch is the enabling technology for the 400–500 layer generation and for the industry's stated goal of 1,000 layers.

> **Worked example: channel-hole etch time and why it dominates NAND tool count.**
> Take a 300-pair stack etched in two decks of 5 µm each. Conventional rate: 120 nm/min average over the hole (fast at the top, slow at the bottom). Time per deck: 5,000 nm / 120 nm/min ≈ 42 min, plus ~5 min for mask open, breakthrough, chamber pumpdown, wafer exchange and in-situ clean: ~47 min per wafer per deck. Chamber throughput: 60/47 ≈ 1.3 wafers per hour. With 85% utilization over a 720-hour month, one chamber etches ~780 deck-passes per month. A 100,000 wafer-start-per-month NAND fab needs 200,000 deck-passes per month, so it needs ~260 chambers dedicated to the channel-hole etch alone. Add the equally deep slit (word-line cut) etch and the staircase contact etches and the count is well over 400 chambers, on the order of 100 platforms, at a cost on the order of $3–6 million per chamber. Now switch to a cryogenic process at 2.5x the rate: 5,000 / 300 ≈ 17 min plus 5 min overhead = 22 min, 2.7 wafers/hour, ~1,650 deck-passes per chamber per month, ~120 chambers. The rate gain is worth roughly 140 chambers, about half a billion dollars of capital, in one fab. This is why a 2.5x etch rate improvement is a headline product launch, and why NAND makers are the single largest customers of dielectric etch.

A note on the numbers: a single 10 µm hole at 300 nm/min is ~33 minutes, matching TEL's public claim, and the conventional comparison at ~120 nm/min (the "2.5 times faster" claim) lands at ~80 minutes. Both companies' actual production rates are proprietary and depend on the hole CD, the stack, and the mask budget.

## Atomic Layer Etching and Selective Removal

As dimensions drop to a few nanometers, the question is no longer "can we etch this fast" but "can we remove exactly 1.5 nm, everywhere, and stop." Continuous plasma etches, whose rate depends on the local ion and radical flux, cannot answer that with sub-nanometer certainty. **Atomic layer etching (ALE)** can, by the same logic as ALD (Module 06): make each step self-limiting so the amount removed per cycle is set by surface chemistry rather than by time or flux.

### The Cl2/Ar silicon ALE cycle

The archetypal ALE, demonstrated in the lab in the 1990s (Sakaue and Horiike's "digital etching" around 1990, Athavale and Economou in 1995–96, and earlier concepts patented by Yoder in 1988) and brought to production by Lam in 2016, has four steps:

1. **Modification (adsorption)**: expose the silicon surface to Cl2 (or a Cl2 plasma at zero bias). Chlorine chemisorbs and chlorinates the top monolayer to SiClx, but at room temperature it does not spontaneously etch silicon, so the reaction saturates once the surface is covered. Doubling the Cl2 dose changes nothing; this is the self-limiting step.
2. **Purge**: pump out the Cl2 so that no chlorine is present during the next step.
3. **Removal (desorption)**: bombard with Ar⁺ ions at a carefully chosen energy. The chlorinated SiClx layer is weakly bound and is removed at ion energies of ~40–70 eV; the bare silicon underneath has a sputter threshold of ~50–100 eV (depending on the reference and the measure). In the **ALE window** between those two thresholds, ions remove exactly the chlorinated layer and then stop, because unchlorinated silicon does not sputter at that energy. The dose is again self-limiting: more ions do nothing.
4. **Purge**, then repeat.

The removal per cycle, the **etch per cycle (EPC)**, is on the order of 0.5–1 Å for Si/Cl2/Ar in practice (a bit less than one atomic layer of Si(100), whose interplanar spacing is 1.36 Å, because chlorination does not perfectly saturate one full layer and desorption is not perfectly complete), and the total removal is EPC × number of cycles, independent of local flux. That independence is the payoff: ALE is intrinsically free of ARDE and microloading (every feature gets the same number of cycles), it produces atomically smooth surfaces (it preferentially removes protrusions, because they are more chlorinated), it operates at ion energies too low to amorphize the substrate, and its selectivity is governed by which materials chlorinate. Its cost is throughput: a cycle takes 1–10 seconds on a production tool (Lam's Kiyo with ALE was designed with fast gas switching for exactly this), so 3 nm of removal is a minute or more of process, ten to a hundred times slower than a continuous etch. For that reason ALE is used as the last few nanometers of a bulk etch (a continuous main etch followed by an ALE finish), not as a replacement for it.

> **Worked example: atomic layers removed in an ALE recipe.**
> A nanosheet inner-spacer recipe needs to trim 2.5 nm of silicon from the exposed sheet tips. The chamber is characterized at an EPC of 0.6 Å ± 0.05 Å per cycle for Cl2/Ar ALE. Cycles needed: 25 Å / 0.6 Å per cycle ≈ 42 cycles. Atomic layers removed: 25 Å / 1.36 Å per Si(100) layer ≈ 18 monolayers, so the recipe removes about 0.44 monolayers per cycle. Uncertainty in total removal from EPC uncertainty: 42 × 0.05 Å ≈ 2 Å, i.e. 2.5 ± 0.2 nm, and because the EPC error is systematic it can be calibrated out with a monitor wafer; the random per-cycle variation averages down as √42. At 4 seconds per cycle the step takes ~170 seconds per wafer, compared with ~5 seconds for a continuous etch of 2.5 nm at 30 nm/min, whose ± 10% flux-dependent variation would be ± 0.25 nm and would differ between dense and isolated features by another few Å. The ALE recipe is 30x slower and ~3x more precise, with zero microloading, which is the trade the inner spacer requires.

### Thermal ALE and isotropic ALE

Plasma ALE is directional (ions supply the removal step). **Thermal ALE**, developed largely by Steven George's group at Colorado, is its isotropic cousin: for Al2O3, alternating HF (which fluorinates the surface to AlF3, self-limiting) with trimethylaluminum or Sn(acac)2 (which ligand-exchanges AlF3 into volatile products) at 250–300 °C removes roughly 0.2–0.8 Å per cycle (rising steeply with temperature) with no ions at all. Similar cycles exist for HfO2, ZrO2, SiO2, TiN, W and others. Thermal and radical-based isotropic ALE are what you want for recesses in 3D structures where directional removal is useless: lateral recess of a film under an overhang, conformal thinning of a liner in a hole, and the SiGe recesses in a gate-all-around transistor.

### Nanosheet release and the SiGe:Si selective etch

The gate-all-around nanosheet transistor (TSMC N2, Samsung SF3, Intel 18A RibbonFET; Module 11) is built from an epitaxial superlattice of alternating Si and Si(1−x)Ge(x) layers (x ≈ 0.25–0.35, each ~5–10 nm thick, three or four periods). Two etches are the heart of the process, and both need to remove SiGe while leaving silicon untouched, with selectivity above 100:1 and near-zero damage to the 5 nm sheets that become the channel:

**Inner spacer recess.** After the dummy gate and spacer are formed and the S/D region is opened, the SiGe layers must be recessed laterally by a controlled amount (~5–8 nm) under the spacer, so that a dielectric inner spacer can be deposited in the cavity to isolate the future gate from the S/D. This is an isotropic, selective, precisely metered lateral etch on a 5 nm thick film, the textbook ALE application. Production approaches are cyclic radical-based processes (for example remote plasma fluorine-based chemistries with temperature control, or oxidation/removal cycles) on tools like Lam's and TEL's ICP chambers, and AMAT's Selectra radical-etch chamber.

**Channel release.** After the dummy gate is removed, the SiGe between the sheets is etched away entirely, leaving suspended silicon sheets that the high-k/metal gate then wraps around. This is a bulk selective isotropic etch. Three chemistries are used or reported: vapor-phase HCl at elevated temperature (~500–700 °C, in an epitaxy-type chamber, exploiting the much faster HCl etching of SiGe than of Si, with selectivity rising with Ge fraction); remote-plasma or thermal fluorine chemistry (CF4/O2/N2 or NF3-based downstream plasma, where the Ge–F products desorb more readily than Si–F at the chosen temperature, and which is what the dry "chemical etch" chambers from AMAT, TEL and Lam target); and wet chemistries (mixtures based on H2O2 with HF or acetic acid, or ammonia-peroxide, which oxidize Ge faster than Si). The failure modes are sheet bending and stiction (suspended 5 nm sheets stuck together by capillary force in a wet process, a reason to prefer dry release), sheet thinning (which shifts the threshold voltage), and residual SiGe. Exact recipes are among the most closely held secrets in the industry.

### Chemical dry etch and isotropic dry etch

A family of tools does "wet-like" isotropic, selective removal with dry chemistry. **Chemical dry etch (CDE)** uses a remote (downstream) plasma so that only long-lived radicals reach the wafer and no ions. The best-known example is the SiCoNi pre-clean (Applied's Producer/Centura SiCoNi, and TEL's Certas): a downstream NF3/NH3 plasma generates NH4F that reacts with SiO2 at ~30 °C to form solid ammonium hexafluorosilicate, (NH4)2SiF6, which is self-limiting because the product layer blocks further reaction; the wafer is then heated above ~100 °C to sublime it. Removal is a few nanometers per cycle, selective to Si and nitride, damage-free, and used before every silicide, epi and contact fill to remove native oxide without the queue-time problems of an HF-last wet clean. AMAT's Selectra and the equivalent chambers from Lam (its Selis and Prevos lines target selective removal) and TEL extend the concept to selective SiN, Si, SiGe, and TiN removal. Together with ALE, these are the fastest-growing etch categories, because the transistor is now made by removing things from inside 3D structures rather than by cutting patterns from the top down.

## Hardmasks, Mask Erosion, and Strip

An EUV resist is 20–35 nm thick. A single-exposure ArF immersion resist is ~90–120 nm. Neither survives an etch of 100 nm of silicon or 200 nm of oxide at 3–5:1 selectivity, and neither has the etch resistance or the mechanical stiffness at 20 nm line widths to stand up (resist lines with aspect ratios above ~3 collapse under capillary forces during develop). So essentially every leading-edge etch is a **hardmask** transfer: the thin resist pattern is transferred into a thin, hard intermediate layer, which then serves as the mask for the real etch. The stack is chosen so each layer has high selectivity to the one below it.

The standard hardmask materials are:

- **Spin-on carbon (SOC)** and **spin-on glass / Si-ARC**: the trilayer resist stack. Resist on 20–30 nm of a silicon-containing anti-reflective layer (etched in fluorocarbon with the resist as mask), on 100–300 nm of spin-on carbon (etched in O2/N2 or H2/N2 or COS/O2 plasma with the Si-ARC as mask, at very high selectivity because the carbon etch does not touch silicon oxide). This is a planarizing stack, so it is also the choice over topography.
- **Amorphous carbon** by PECVD (Applied's APF is the classic; every deposition vendor has one), which is denser and more etch-resistant than spin-on carbon and can be made microns thick for deep etches. **Boron-doped carbon** (higher density, higher modulus, developed for 3D NAND) raises the oxide:mask selectivity from ~5:1 toward ~10:1 and is what allows single-deck channel holes to reach 5–6 µm. Carbon masks are transparent to alignment wavelengths only when thin, hence the interest in **tungsten-doped carbon** and other metal-containing masks for the deepest etches.
- **Silicon nitride** and **silicon oxide**, the workhorse inorganic masks for silicon etches (HBr/Cl2 has excellent selectivity to both).
- **Titanium nitride** for BEOL dual-damascene trench etch (a metal hardmask that survives the low-k etch and is later removed by CMP) and for some gate stacks. TiN masks introduce their own problem: they are conductive, so they change the charging behavior, and TiN residues can cause metal contamination.

**Mask erosion** is the mask's own etch. Even at 10:1 selectivity, a 2 µm mask loses 1 µm during a 10 µm NAND etch. Erosion is not uniform: the corners of the mask facet from sputtering, the faceted corners reflect ions into the feature (bowing), the mask CD opens up (CD bias), and the mask surface roughens and transfers **line-edge roughness** into the etched feature. In EUV patterning, resist-level stochastic roughness is partially smoothed by the multi-step transfer (each etch step with a polymerizing chemistry rounds off high-frequency roughness), which is one reason etch is an active part of the EUV stochastics discussion (Module 08).

**Strip (ash)** removes the resist and carbon masks when their work is done. **O2 plasma ashing** in a downstream microwave or ICP source at 200–300 °C oxidizes organic material to CO, CO2 and H2O at several µm/min, with N2 or forming gas additions; H2/N2 chemistry is used instead of O2 on low-k dielectrics, because oxygen radicals strip the methyl groups from SiOCH and raise its dielectric constant. Post-implant resist (Module 10) has a carbonized crust that pops if heated quickly, so a low-temperature first step is used. **Wet strip** in **SPM** (sulfuric-peroxide mix, "piranha," 4:1 H2SO4:H2O2 at 120–150 °C) or ozonated water finishes the job and removes the inorganic residues an ash cannot. Strippers: Mattson (now Chinese-owned), PSK (Korea), Lam GxT, ULVAC, and the wet benches above. A leading-edge flow has roughly as many strip steps as lithography steps.

## Endpoint, Chamber Matching, Seasoning, and Consumables

### Endpoint detection

An etch must stop when the film is gone, not before (residue, opens) and not too long after (loss of the etch stop, profile degradation). Timed etches drift with chamber condition, so production etches use **endpoint detection** plus a defined **over-etch** (typically 20–50% of the main etch time, or a fixed number of seconds after endpoint) to clear the last of the film in the slowest features.

**Optical emission spectroscopy (OES)** watches the plasma glow through a window with a spectrometer. As the etched film clears and the underlying material is exposed, the concentrations of etch products and of the consumed reactants change, and their emission lines change intensity: the CO line at 483.5 nm and 519.8 nm falls as an oxide etch reaches silicon (the oxide was the oxygen source); the SiF line at 440 nm and the F line at 703.7 nm shift when silicon clears; the CN line at 387 nm appears when a nitride is reached in a nitrogen-free chemistry; the Cl line at 837.6 nm rises when a chlorine etch stops consuming chlorine; AlCl at 261 nm tracks an aluminum etch. Because a contact-hole etch may expose only 1–3% of the wafer area, the endpoint signal can be tiny, so tools use full-spectrum multivariate analysis (principal component analysis over hundreds of wavelengths) rather than single lines.

**Interferometry** (laser or broadband reflectometry) monitors the thickness of the film being etched in real time. Light reflected from the film's top surface and from the film/substrate interface interferes; as the film thins, the reflected intensity oscillates with a period equal to λ/(2n) in film thickness. For a 670 nm laser on polysilicon (n ≈ 4) one fringe is ~84 nm; on oxide (n = 1.46) it is ~230 nm. Counting fringes gives the remaining thickness, and the oscillation stops when the film clears, so interferometry can end an etch at a target *remaining* thickness (a partial recess) where OES cannot. RF impedance monitoring (the plasma's electrical load changes as the surface changes) and, on the newest tools, in-situ hyperspectral or ellipsometric sensors round out the set.

### Chamber matching, seasoning, and in-situ clean

A fab's 100 chambers of a given type must produce interchangeable results, and a chamber's results drift over its own life. The **first-wafer effect** (the first wafer after a clean etches differently, because a clean chamber wall adsorbs radicals and shifts the radical/ion ratio) is handled by **seasoning**: after every in-situ clean, the chamber runs the process on a few dummy wafers, or runs a dedicated deposition step, to coat the walls with a controlled thin polymer or oxide layer so the wall chemistry is the same for every production wafer. The wall coating then thickens with each wafer, flakes at some point into particles, and is stripped by a **waferless auto clean (WAC)** (an O2 or SF6/O2 plasma with no wafer, tens of seconds, every wafer or every few wafers), after which the cycle restarts. **Chamber matching** is the tuning of every chamber's knobs (and the qualification of its consumables) so that its etch rate, uniformity, profile and CD sit within a fleet distribution, verified by regular monitor wafers. Modern tools use sensor data (OES fingerprints, RF harmonics, chuck temperatures) and machine-learning models to keep chambers on target between physical qualifications, and both Lam ("Equipment Intelligence") and Applied sell software for this.

### Chamber materials and consumables

The chamber's own surfaces participate in the plasma. Aluminum walls are anodized or coated with yttria (Y2O3) or yttrium fluoride, which resist fluorine attack and shed fewer particles than bare Al2O3 (which fluorinates to AlF3 particles). The showerhead of a dielectric CCP is high-purity silicon (which scavenges fluorine and is a controllable, contamination-free consumable), and the upper electrode in oxide etchers is silicon or silicon carbide. Quartz and ceramic liners protect the ICP window.

The **edge ring** (**focus ring**), a consumable ring of silicon or SiC surrounding the wafer on the chuck, exists because the wafer edge is an electrical discontinuity: without it, the sheath would bend around the wafer edge and ions would arrive tilted over the outer 10–20 mm, wrecking uniformity and, in a NAND hole, tilting the holes. The ring extends the electrical surface of the wafer so the sheath is flat past the wafer edge. But the ring is etched too, ~50 µm per few hundred RF-hours, and as it thins the sheath at the wafer edge sags and the edge dies drift out of spec. Fabs replace edge rings on a schedule of a few hundred RF-hours, each replacement costing a vacuum break and hours of pump-down and re-qualification. The 2018–2022 generation of tools (Lam's and TEL's) added mechanisms to raise the ring as it erodes (compensating the sheath) and to swap rings through the wafer load port without breaking vacuum. Edge rings, showerheads, ESC surfaces and liners together are an etch consumables business measured in billions of dollars a year, shared between the tool OEMs and third parties (Greene Tweed, Hana Materials, SK Enpulse / SKC Solmics, Mitsubishi Materials, CoorsTek, and Ferrotec are typical suppliers).

## Plasma Damage and Its Mitigation

Everything the ions and photons do to the wafer that the recipe did not intend is **plasma damage**, and it is a yield and reliability line item:

- **Charging damage**: gates connected to large metal antennas collect current from a non-uniform plasma and the current tunnels through the thin gate oxide, degrading or breaking it. Design rules ("antenna rules") limit the area of metal tied to a gate before a protective diode is inserted, and etch engineers minimize plasma non-uniformity and use pulsing. In deep features, charging inside the feature causes notching and twisting.
- **Ion bombardment damage**: ions of a few hundred eV amorphize and implant the top 1–5 nm of silicon, leaving a defective, sometimes fluorine- or bromine-contaminated layer. The exposed S/D silicon after a spacer etch, and the fin sidewall after fin etch, carry this layer; it degrades epi growth and mobility. Mitigation: a low-energy final step, ALE, or a sacrificial oxidation and strip.
- **Photon damage**: plasmas emit vacuum-ultraviolet photons (Ar at 104.8 and 106.7 nm, ~12 eV, and hydrogen Lyman-α at 121.6 nm) that create traps in SiO2 and high-k gate dielectrics, shifting threshold voltage. Low-electron-temperature sources (TEL's RLSA microwave source) and pulsing reduce the VUV dose; a post-etch anneal heals some of it.
- **Low-k damage**: O2, N2 or H-containing plasmas strip the carbon from porous SiOCH, leaving a hydrophilic, higher-k, HF-soluble skin that raises capacitance and lets moisture in. Mitigation: non-oxidizing strip chemistries, ALE-like low-energy trench etch, and post-etch silylation repair.
- **Residues and contamination**: fluorocarbon polymer left on sidewalls, chlorine on aluminum (corrosion), metal sputtered from chamber parts. Post-etch cleans (Module 03's wet chemistries) handle these.

The broad direction of the last decade, driven by all of the above, is toward lower ion energy, pulsed plasmas, ALE endpoints, and selective radical-based removals: doing the last few nanometers gently, because that is where the transistor lives.

## Tools, Vendors, and the Market

### The product lines

**Lam Research** (Fremont, California) is the largest etch company. Its **Kiyo** series (Kiyo F, G, GX) is the ICP conductor etch platform used for gate, fin, spacer, hardmask and metal etches; **Flex** (Flex F, G, H and successors) is the dual-frequency CCP dielectric platform for contact, via, trench and 3D NAND holes; **Sense.i**, launched in 2020, is a new-architecture, high-density platform (initially conductor etch) built for a smaller footprint and machine-learning-based control; **Syndion** does TSV and deep silicon; **Coronus** is a bevel (wafer-edge) etcher; and Lam Cryo 3.0 is the cryogenic dielectric etch technology on its dielectric platforms. Lam is generally credited as the leader in conductor etch and in high-aspect-ratio dielectric etch for memory, and it introduced the first production ALE.

**Tokyo Electron** (Tokyo) sells etch on the **Tactras** platform, with **Vigus** dual-frequency CCP chambers for dielectric etch and RLSA microwave and ICP chambers for conductor etch, plus the **Episode UL** platform (launched 2020, configurable up to 12 chambers) on which the cryogenic channel-hole etch is offered. TEL is historically strongest in dielectric etch, particularly at Japanese and Korean memory makers and in DRAM, and its 2023 cryogenic process was the first announced.

**Applied Materials** (Santa Clara) sells the **Centris Sym3** and **Sym3 Y** dielectric etch chambers (a symmetric CCP design introduced in 2016 that Applied used to re-enter the dielectric market it had lost) and conductor etch on the **Centura** platform (its silicon etch chambers, including the Silvia line), plus the **Producer Selectra** and SiCoNi radical-based selective and pre-clean chambers. Applied's etch position has strengthened since Sym3, especially in DRAM and in selective removal; it remains third overall in etch by most estimates.

**Hitachi High-Tech** (Japan) holds a niche in ECR/microwave conductor etch (gate, fin), a few percent of the market.

**AMEC** (Advanced Micro-Fabrication Equipment, Shanghai) makes CCP dielectric etchers (Primo D-RIE, Primo AD-RIE, Primo SSC AD-RIE) and ICP conductor etchers (Primo nanova, Primo Twin-Star) and is a qualified supplier of some dielectric etch at TSMC as well as at every Chinese fab. **Naura** (Beijing) makes ICP silicon etch and CCP tools and is the largest Chinese equipment vendor overall. Together the two took roughly 20% of etch purchases in China by 2024–2025 and a mid-single-digit share globally, concentrated in mature nodes and in memory (YMTC, CXMT) where export controls have shut out or limited the US vendors. Etch is the segment where China's domestic equipment industry is furthest along, because etch chambers, unlike EUV scanners, are buildable with commercially available RF, vacuum and gas-delivery subsystems.

### Market shares and economics

The etch equipment market was roughly $20–25 billion in 2024 (about 20% of wafer fab equipment), rising in 2025–2026 with the NAND layer-count race and the GAA ramp. Market-share estimates depend on how the segment is defined (whether strip, CDE, bevel, and packaging etch are included), and sources disagree noticeably: the most-cited analyst rankings put Lam first at roughly 40–45%, TEL second at ~25%, and Applied third at ~18–20%, while at least one 2024–2025 report ranks Applied first at ~30% by including its selective and ALD-adjacent removal tools. Treat any single number as ± 5 points. The top three hold ~85–90% between them.

A leading-edge etch platform carries 4–6 process chambers on a vacuum transfer module with two or more load locks and an atmospheric front end with FOUP load ports (Module 05); the platform costs on the order of $15–25 million and a single chamber on the order of $3–6 million, with high-aspect-ratio cryogenic dielectric chambers at the top of that range. Chamber throughput ranges from ~1–3 wafers per hour (NAND channel hole) to 30–60 wafers per hour for a shallow conductor etch.

> **Worked example: etch chambers in a logic gigafab.**
> A 100,000 wafer-start-per-month N3-class logic fab, ~250 plasma etch and strip chamber visits per wafer, average chamber time 3 minutes per wafer including exchange (a blend of 1-minute strips and 8-minute contact etches), and 80% availability. Wafer-etch-steps per month: 100,000 × 250 = 25 million. Capacity per chamber: (60/3) × 720 h × 0.8 ≈ 11,500 per month. Chambers: 25,000,000 / 11,500 ≈ 2,200 etch and strip chambers, on ~500 platforms, representing on the order of $6–8 billion of the fab's ~$25–30 billion equipment bill. This is why etch and deposition each rival lithography as the largest equipment categories by dollars in a logic fab (industry-wide, lithography, deposition and etch are each roughly a fifth to a quarter of WFE, and the ranking among them shifts with the memory/logic mix), even though a single EUV scanner is the most expensive machine on the floor.

### The trend: more steps, each removing less

The etch step count has grown faster than the total step count: SAQP turned one fin-patterning etch into ~6 at N7, EUV removed some at N5–N3, and the 3D transistor (spacer, inner spacer, release, recess, cut) and every self-aligned scheme added more. At N2 and A16 with backside power delivery (Module 11), the wafer is additionally thinned, flipped and etched from the back to reach buried power rails. The direction is more steps, each removing less, with atomic precision: ALE and selective isotropic removal have grown from research curiosities in 2014 into a multi-billion-dollar sub-segment and are the fastest-growing part of etch.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Plasma etching is hot."** → The electrons are at tens of thousands of kelvin, but the gas, ions and wafer are near room temperature (and in cryogenic etch, at −70 °C). Etch is a cold process; the energy is delivered by directed ions and reactive radicals, not by heat.
- **"Ions do the etching."** → Coburn and Winters showed that ions alone sputter at ~2 Å/min and radicals alone etch at ~5 Å/min, but together they etch at ~55 Å/min. Ions do not remove most of the atoms; they enable the chemistry to do so, and only where they land. The chemistry provides the rate and selectivity, the ions provide the direction.
- **"Anisotropy comes from the ions being vertical."** → That is necessary but not sufficient. Most etch chemistries attack sidewalls spontaneously; vertical profiles come from a deliberately engineered sidewall passivation layer (CFx polymer, SiOxBry, or a condensed cryogenic layer) that ions clear from the bottom but not from the walls.
- **"Wet etch is obsolete."** → Wet etch is the only way to get 100:1 to 1,000:1 selectivity cheaply, and the 3D NAND word-line replacement, hardmask strips, nanosheet release (in some flows), and every pre-clean depend on it. What is obsolete is wet *patterning*, defeated by isotropic undercut once features became smaller than about twice the film thickness.
- **"Etch rate is a property of the recipe."** → It is a property of the recipe *and* the wafer *and* the chamber. Loading, microloading and ARDE make the same recipe etch a dense narrow hole at half the rate of a wide open area, and chamber wall condition (seasoning) changes the radical/ion ratio from wafer to wafer. This is why endpoint detection, over-etch, seasoning and chamber matching exist, and why ALE, which is flux-independent, is spreading.
- **"Copper interconnects are etched."** → Copper has no volatile etch product at practical temperatures, which is exactly why the BEOL switched to damascene (etch the dielectric, fill with copper, polish) in 1997. Aluminum and tungsten are etched; copper, cobalt and ruthenium lines are formed by fill.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Etch consumes the patterned resist and hardmask stacks produced by lithography (Modules 07 and 08), the films deposited by oxidation, CVD, ALD and PVD (Module 06), the specialty gases (NF3, SF6, C4F8, C4F6, HBr, Cl2, BCl3, HF) and wet chemicals (HF, NH4F, H3PO4, KOH, TMAH, H2SO4, H2O2) from Module 04, and the silicon, SiC and ceramic consumable parts made by a supply chain of its own. It produces the physical features of the device: isolation trenches, fins, nanosheet stacks, gates, spacers, contact and via holes, damascene trenches, NAND channel holes and TSVs. Its outputs go next to ion implantation and anneal (Module 10), where the etched silicon is doped through the openings etch created, and then round and round through the deposition-litho-etch loop several hundred times until the transistor (Module 11) and the interconnect stack (Module 12) are complete. Each etch step is followed by metrology (Module 13): CD-SEM and OCD to measure the etched CD and profile, and inspection to catch the opens, shorts, residues and particles that etch is the single largest source of. Tool-wise, etch is one of the three largest equipment categories alongside deposition and lithography, bought from Lam, TEL and Applied by every fab in the world, and it is the segment where the Chinese equipment industry (AMEC, Naura) has made the deepest inroads.
