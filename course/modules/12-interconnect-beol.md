# Module 12: Interconnect: The Back End of Line

A leading-edge logic die contains on the order of 10¹⁰ transistors, and every one of them is useless until it is wired to the others. The wiring is the **back end of line (BEOL)**: roughly 15 to 18 stacked levels (as of 2025) of metal lines and vertical **vias** (a via is a vertical metal plug that joins a wire on one level to the wire directly above it), embedded in insulating **dielectric** (an electrical insulator; here a glass-like silicon oxide), built on top of the transistors after the **front end of line (FEOL)**, the transistor-making half of the flow, is finished. Added up, a large die carries tens of kilometers of wire. The tightest lines are ~12 nm wide, narrower than the **mean free path** of an electron in copper, the average distance it flies between collisions (~39 nm). The thickest are microns wide and carry amps of supply current.

The hard problem is this: transistors got faster with every node, but wires got slower. To switch a wire, the transistor driving it must push charge onto it (the wire's **capacitance**, C, is the charge it stores per volt) through the wire's **resistance**, R; the product R × C is a time, the time to fill the bucket (C) through the straw (R). When you shrink a wire's width and pitch together, resistance per unit length rises roughly as the inverse square of the width while capacitance per unit length barely changes, so the delay of a wire of given length goes up with every node. Since the late 1990s the industry has fought this with a new conductor (copper), new insulators (**low-k**: dielectrics that store less charge, so less C), a completely different way of patterning metal (**damascene**: inlaying metal into grooves carved in the insulator, explained below), and now a third generation of conductors (cobalt, ruthenium, molybdenum) and a relocation of the entire power grid to the back of the wafer. The BEOL is now about half of all process steps in a logic flow, roughly a third of wafer cost, and the dominant term in the delay of most signal paths.

This module walks through how the stack is built, layer by layer, and why. By the end you will be able to explain why wires got slower, how one damascene level is built, why copper is being pushed out of the bottom layers, and why the power grid is moving to the back of the wafer.

## Before you start

- **Resistance and resistivity** (school physics). A conductor's resistance is R = ρL/A: length L, cross-sectional area A, and ρ the **resistivity**, a property of the material rather than the shape (the resistance of a 1 m cube of it). Resistivity is quoted in microohm-centimetres, µΩ·cm; 1 µΩ·cm = 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Copper is 1.68 µΩ·cm, aluminum 2.65.
- **Capacitance and dielectric constant** (Module 06). Two conductors separated by an insulator store charge Q = CV. The insulator's **dielectric constant k** multiplies C: vacuum is 1, silicon dioxide 3.9. R × C has units of seconds: it is a charging time.
- **The transistor you are wiring** (Module 11). A transistor has three terminals: source, drain and gate. When Module 11 ends, the source/drain **epitaxy** (crystal-grown, heavily doped raised source and drain regions) and the replacement metal gate are finished and the wafer is ready for contacts. Logic is built from **standard cells**, pre-designed gates laid out in fixed-height strips.
- **Deposition families** (Module 06). **PVD** (sputtering) knocks atoms off a target and is line-of-sight: it coats what it can see. **CVD/PECVD** lets gases react on the surface, plasma-assisted at ~300 to 400 °C, and is roughly conformal. **ALD** lays down one atomic layer per gas cycle and coats every surface, however deep, equally.
- **Plasma etch** (Module 09). Ions plus a reactive gas remove material downward, and only when the reaction product is volatile enough to be pumped away. **Selectivity** is how much faster the target etches than the mask or the layer below; a **hard mask** is a tough film that takes the pattern from the resist.
- **Lithography vocabulary** (Modules 07, 08). **Pitch** = line width + space, the centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring lines. **EUV** (extreme ultraviolet, 13.5 nm light) versus **193 nm immersion (193i)** versus the older **KrF** (248 nm) and **i-line** (365 nm) scanners; **double patterning** (SADP, LELE) splits one tight pattern across two exposures; **overlay** is how precisely one layer lands on the one below; **depth of focus** is the height range over which the image stays sharp; **stochastic defects** are random missing or merged features from photon shot noise in EUV.
- **CMP** (Modules 03, 11). Chemical mechanical planarization: a spinning pad and a chemical slurry flatten the wafer. You met it at shallow trench isolation and at the gate.
- **Diffusion and activation energy** (Modules 02, 10). Atoms hop between lattice sites at a rate proportional to exp(−E_a/kT), where E_a is the energy hill of the hop in **electron-volts** (eV, the energy an electron gains falling through 1 V), k Boltzmann's constant (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K) and T the temperature in kelvin. Deposited metals are not single crystals but mosaics of small crystals called **grains**, separated by **grain boundaries**.
- **Node names** (Module 00). TSMC N5, N3, N2 and A16; Intel 4, 3 and 18A. In this module "1× pitch" means the tightest metal pitch of the node; a "2× level" has twice that pitch.

## Why Interconnect Became the Bottleneck

Picture the designer's problem. A signal leaves a transistor on one side of a 5 mm die and must reach a transistor on the other side. The transistor itself switches in a few picoseconds; the wire, if it were a single unbroken 5 mm line on a tight level, would take longer than a whole clock cycle to settle. Every generation makes the transistor a little faster and, if nothing else is done, the wire a little slower, because the wire is shrunk in width and height along with everything else while the die is not getting smaller. This section puts numbers on why, and on what it forces.

> **Intuition: a chain of buckets and straws.** A long wire is not one bucket filled through one straw; it is a chain of many tiny buckets, each fed through its own tiny straw from the one before it. The far end fills last, and it fills slowly because the charge has to pass through every straw on the way. Double the length and you double the number of buckets (C) and the number of straws in series (R), so the fill time goes up four times: delay grows as L². And here is the trap of scaling: shrink every dimension by half and each plate of the capacitor is half as big but half as close to its neighbour, so the capacitance per unit length does not change. Only the straw gets narrower.

### RC delay

Treat the whole wire as one resistor feeding one capacitor (a **lumped** model) and the far end charges exponentially with the school time constant RC (63 percent of the way after 1.0 RC), reaching half its final voltage after 0.69 RC (ln 2 × RC). Spread the R and C evenly along the wire (a **distributed** model) and the far end reaches half its final voltage after only ~0.38 × R_total × C_total, because the charge that fills the near end of the wire never has to pass through the whole resistance; that is the 50 percent delay of a uniform distributed RC line, a like-for-like comparison with the 0.69 RC above. (The **Elmore estimate**, a common design shortcut that adds up, for each segment of the wire, its resistance times all the capacitance beyond it, gives 0.5 RC for the same line: deliberately a little pessimistic, but easy to compute for any branching net.) The constant matters less than the scaling: both R and C are proportional to L, so wire delay grows as L². An **unbuffered** wire is one with no amplifying stage inserted along its length.

Resistance per unit length is:

r = ρ / (w × h)

where ρ is the resistivity, w the width and h the height (thickness) of the line. For a 20 nm wide, 40 nm tall copper line at an effective resistivity of 7 µΩ·cm (why it is not 1.68 comes next), r = 7 × 10⁻⁸ / (20 × 10⁻⁹ × 40 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 90 Ω/µm before the barrier is subtracted; the worked example below does the full accounting and gets ~147 Ω/µm.

Capacitance per unit length for a line with neighbours at spacing s on the same level and dielectric of thickness t above and below is dominated by the lateral (line-to-line) term plus vertical terms; a workable estimate is:

c ≈ 2 ε₀ k (h/s) + 2 ε₀ k (w/t)

Each term is the school parallel-plate formula, C = ε₀ k × (plate area) / (gap), written per unit length: the first term counts the two side neighbours (the facing plates are the line sidewalls, height h, across the gap s; twice because there is a neighbour on each side), the second the levels above and below (plate width w across the vertical gap t; twice again). Here ε₀ is the permittivity of free space, 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m, and k is the dielectric constant of the insulator (how many times more charge it lets a capacitor store than vacuum: 1 for air, 3.9 for SiO₂, ~2.7 for a porous low-k). Put in the geometry of a tight level. h/s = 2, because tight lines are built with an **aspect ratio** (height divided by width) of h/w ≈ 2 and the space between lines equals their width, s ≈ w. w/t ≈ 0.33, because the dielectric between one level's lines and the next level's is the via-level thickness, ~60 nm, about three line widths. With k = 2.7 the two terms give ≈0.11 fF/µm, where 1 fF (femtofarad) is 10⁻¹⁵ farad. **Fringing fields**, the field lines that bulge out past the edges of each plate instead of running straight across the gap (so every line "sees" more of its neighbours than the parallel-plate count allows), roughly double it to the ~0.2 fF/µm (200 pF/m) that real stacks show. Because h/s and w/t are geometric ratios that stay roughly constant as everything shrinks together, capacitance per unit length has sat at about 0.2 fF/µm for two decades, almost independent of node. That number has a physical meaning: a 1 mm wire holds 0.2 pF, which at 1 V is 2 × 10⁻¹³ C, about a million electrons that the driving transistor must supply every time the wire toggles. Resistance per unit length, by contrast, doubles every time width and height are each shrunk by 0.7×, and that is before the resistivity itself starts rising.

### The resistivity size effect

> **Intuition: a corridor narrower than your stride.** A deposited metal is a mosaic of tiny crystals (**grains**) whose mismatched joints (**grain boundaries**) deflect electrons. In bulk copper a conduction electron flies ~39 nm between deflections by lattice vibrations (**phonons**, the thermal jiggling of the atoms), and that distance is its mean free path. A wire narrower than 39 nm is a corridor narrower than your stride: you hit the walls far more often than you would in a hall, and every wall hit that scrambles your direction is a lost step. A **specular** bounce is mirror-like and keeps the forward momentum (a smooth wall); a **diffuse** bounce sends the electron off in a random direction (a rough wall) and adds to the resistance. Copper against its barrier is, unfortunately, a rough wall.

Bulk copper has ρ = 1.68 µΩ·cm at 20 °C and a mean free path of about 39 nm at room temperature. When any dimension of the conductor approaches or drops below this, electrons scatter off the surfaces and the grain boundaries in addition to the phonons that limit bulk conductivity, and the effective resistivity climbs. Two classical models describe this:

- **Fuchs-Sondheimer** surface scattering: a fraction (1 − p) of electrons hitting the surface scatter diffusely rather than specularly. For copper against its tantalum nitride (TaN) barrier (the ceramic skin described just below), p is close to 0: nearly every surface collision is a resistive one.
- **Mayadas-Shatzkes** grain boundary scattering: each grain boundary reflects a fraction R_gb of incident electrons (a reflection coefficient, not a resistance; plain R stays reserved for resistance in this module), with R_gb ≈ 0.3 to 0.5 for copper. Because damascene lines are narrow trenches, grains cannot grow larger than the line width, so the grain boundary density scales inversely with width.

A reusable rule of thumb for the surface term alone is ρ_eff ≈ ρ₀ × (1 + ~0.4 × λ/w), with λ the mean free path and w the width. At w = 13 nm (the copper left in a 20 nm trench, see below) that is 1.68 × (1 + 0.4 × 3) ≈ 3.7 µΩ·cm from surfaces; grain boundaries roughly double it, to the measured 5 to 8 µΩ·cm for a 20 nm wide, 40 nm tall line, three to five times the bulk value. At 12 nm width it approaches 10 µΩ·cm.

There is a second penalty. Every copper line is wrapped in a **barrier** (tantalum nitride, TaN: a thin ceramic skin that stops copper atoms from wandering into the insulator) and a **liner** (a cobalt film that copper will stick to and spread on); both are explained in step 6 of the damascene flow. The 1.5 to 2 nm of TaN and 1 to 2 nm of cobalt needed on each sidewall and on the trench bottom carry almost no current (TaN is ~200 µΩ·cm, more than a hundred times copper) yet take up a fixed slice of the cross section. At a 20 nm trench width, ~6 to 7 nm of the 20 nm is barrier and liner: 35 percent of the width and ~40 percent of the cross-sectional area. The copper in that line is effectively a 13 × 36 nm conductor.

### The crossover

Process generations used to be named by their smallest drawn feature, so "0.25 µm" meant 250 nm lines. The famous chart from the 1997 National Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors showed gate delay falling monotonically with scaling while the delay of an aluminum/SiO₂ wire rose, with the two curves crossing around the 0.25 to 0.18 µm generations. That chart motivated the copper and low-k transitions; the curves have been re-crossing ever since. The yardstick of a node's speed is the **fan-out-of-4 (FO4) delay**: the time for an **inverter** (the simplest logic gate, whose output is the opposite of its input) to switch four copies of itself. At N3 it is under 10 ps (single-digit picoseconds), while an unbuffered 1 mm intermediate-level wire has a delay in the nanoseconds. Designers cope by inserting **repeaters** (buffers: an inverter pair placed mid-wire to re-sharpen the signal, which restarts the L² clock from zero) every 100 to 300 µm, by routing long signals on upper, wider layers, and by keeping most connections short. Nevertheless a substantial fraction of the standard cells on a modern **system-on-chip (SoC)**, a die that carries processor cores, memory and accelerators together, are repeaters and clock buffers whose only job is to fight wire delay, and interconnect is responsible for roughly half of **dynamic power**, the energy spent charging capacitances every cycle (proportional to C × V² × f), so wire capacitance is directly power. Both figures come from published mid-2000s studies, which put repeaters and clock buffers at ~10 to 30+ percent of cells and interconnect at roughly half of dynamic power; newer public numbers are scarce, because foundries and chipmakers treat them as proprietary.

Two naming conventions are needed for the worked example. **Pitch** is the centre-to-centre spacing of neighbouring lines, width plus space, and is the number lithography controls; at the tightest levels width and space are about equal, so a 40 nm pitch means ~20 nm lines with ~20 nm gaps. Metal levels are named **M1, M2, M3** and so on from the bottom up (with **M0**, the local interconnect, below M1), and the via between Mn and Mn+1 is **Vn**.

> **Worked example: RC delay of a 1 mm wire on a tight-pitch level versus M8.**
> Take a tight-pitch line at 40 nm pitch (N7-class, chosen for round numbers; at N3's 26 nm pitch every term below is worse): 20 nm drawn width, 40 nm height, spacing 20 nm. After 2 nm TaN and 1.5 nm Co on each sidewall and the bottom, the copper cross section is about 13 nm × 36.5 nm = 475 nm² = 4.75 × 10⁻¹⁶ m². Use ρ_eff = 7 µΩ·cm = 7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Then R = ρL/A = (7 × 10⁻⁸ × 10⁻³) / 4.75 × 10⁻¹⁶ ≈ 147 kΩ for 1 mm, i.e. ~147 Ω/µm. Capacitance at 0.2 fF/µm gives C = 200 fF. Distributed delay = 0.38 × 147 × 10³ × 200 × 10⁻¹⁵ ≈ 11 ns. That is a thousand FO4 delays; nobody routes a millimetre on M2.
> Now take an M8 line at a 4× pitch of 160 nm: 80 nm wide, 160 nm tall, copper cross section ~73 × 156 nm ≈ 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁴ m², ρ_eff ≈ 2.5 µΩ·cm. R ≈ 2.2 kΩ per mm, C ≈ 200 fF, delay ≈ 0.38 × 2.2 × 10³ × 2 × 10⁻¹³ ≈ 0.17 ns unbuffered. Split it into five 200 µm segments with a repeater between each: each segment's delay drops by 25× (L²) to ~7 ps, plus ~15 to 20 ps per repeater, giving ~0.1 to 0.13 ns total, and now the signal edge stays sharp. This is why the metal stack is hierarchical.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Wire capacitance sets dynamic power, and wire delay sets the number of repeaters; repeaters cost die area and power of their own. When Module 19 adds up the power budget of a GPU (graphics processing unit), a large share of the watts is wires being charged and the buffers that drive them, not the arithmetic units, and the interconnect stack chosen here is what fixes that share.

## Anatomy of the Metal Stack

Because delay grows as L², one size of wire cannot serve every connection: a short wire can afford to be thin and slow per unit length, a long wire must be fat. Modern logic therefore uses a **hierarchical** stack: many thin, tight-pitch layers at the bottom for local wiring inside standard cells and between neighbours, progressively wider and thicker layers above for longer signals, and very thick layers at the top for power distribution and clock trees. Pitch multiples are quoted relative to the minimum pitch: 1×, 1.5×, 2×, 4×, and so on.

> **Intuition: streets, arterials and highways.** A city does not connect every house by highway. Houses sit on narrow streets (M0 to M2: slow per kilometre, but the trips are short), streets feed arterial roads (M3 to M10), and long trips ride highways (the global levels) with on-ramps (vias) at each change of scale. Power is the water main: it enters from the top through the widest pipes and steps down through every level to reach each cell. Backside power delivery, at the end of this module, is the decision to run the water mains under the city instead of over it.

A representative stack for a TSMC N3-class process (the exact numbers are proprietary and vary between N3B, N3E, N3P and their design rule variants; treat these as typical rather than exact) looks like this:

| Level | Count | Pitch | Line thickness | Litho | Conductor | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contact / M0 (MOL) | ~1 to 2 | ~23 to 26 nm | ~30 to 40 nm | EUV, double-patterned | Cu (Co-lined) or Ru; Co/W plugs | Connects S/D and gate; local cell routing |
| M1 to M2 (1×) | ~2 | ~26 to 30 nm | ~40 to 50 nm | EUV single or double | Cu with Co (or, per TSMC's N3 disclosure, an "innovative", reportedly Ru, liner) and Co cap | Cell-internal and cell-to-cell routing |
| M3 to M6 (1.5× to 2×) | ~4 | ~40 to 60 nm | ~60 to 100 nm | EUV or 193i SADP | Cu | Block-level signal routing |
| M7 to M10 (2× to 4×) | ~4 | ~80 to 130 nm | ~130 to 250 nm | 193i | Cu | Long signals, local power straps |
| M11 to M14 (semi-global) | ~3 to 4 | ~200 to 400 nm | ~300 to 600 nm | 193i / KrF | Cu | Clock trunks, power mesh |
| M15 to M17 (global) | ~2 to 3 | ~0.8 to 2 µm | ~1 to 3 µm | KrF / i-line | Thick Cu | Power and ground grid, I/O |
| AP / RDL | ~1 | ~10 to 30 µm | ~1.5 to 3 µm | i-line | Al-Cu or thick Cu | Pads, bump redistribution |

Key to the table: **193i** is the 193 nm immersion **deep ultraviolet (DUV)** scanner (Module 07); **SADP** is self-aligned double patterning, a spacer trick that halves the pitch of one exposure (Module 07); **LELE** (used below) is litho-etch-litho-etch, two exposures interleaved on the same layer (Module 08); **KrF** (248 nm) and **i-line** (365 nm) are older, cheaper scanners for the coarse layers; **S/D** is source/drain; **AP** is the aluminum pad layer and **RDL** the redistribution layer, both described in The Top of the Stack; **MOL** is the middle of line, next.

A few landmarks anchor the table (all as of 2025). TSMC N5 has a minimum metal pitch of 28 nm on M0; N3 tightened this to ~23 to 25 nm, and N2 is reported to be in the same range, with EUV double patterning (LELE) used on M0 and via layers. Intel 4 runs M0 at 30 nm, relaxed to 36 nm in the PowerVia variant (PowerVia is Intel's scheme for moving the power grid to the back of the wafer, explained in the Backside Power Delivery section) because power rails are no longer competing for space on the front side, and Intel 18A also sits around 32 nm. In total, TSMC N5 uses about 14 to 15 copper levels plus the aluminum pad layer; TSMC's N3 paper at **IEDM** (the International Electron Devices Meeting, the annual conference where foundries disclose their nodes) describes a 15-level stack, and the tallest N3/N2-class product stacks are reported at up to ~17 to 18 levels. Intel's PowerVia test vehicle on Intel 4 used 14 front-side layers plus 4 backside layers and an RDL, and Intel 18A is reported in the same range. Above ~80 nm pitch the layers are still patterned with 193 nm immersion because the cost per layer is a fraction of EUV, and the litho tool mix in a fab reflects this: a handful of EUV layers at the bottom, a dozen DUV layers above them.

### Middle of line (MOL)

Here is the problem the **middle of line (MOL)** solves. Every transistor has three terminals (source, drain and gate), there are ~10¹⁰ transistors, and each terminal must be reached through a contact whose footprint is on the order of 20 × 40 nm. Metal touching silicon does not conduct freely. At the junction the electrons face an energy step, the **Schottky barrier**, whose height (a few tenths of an eV) is fixed by which metal meets which semiconductor and cannot be doped away. An electron at room temperature cannot climb a step that tall, but if the step is thin enough, a few nanometres, quantum mechanics lets a fraction of the electrons pass straight through it: **tunnelling**, a purely quantum effect with no school-physics analogue, whose probability falls exponentially with the thickness of the step. The thickness of the step is the width of the **depletion region**, the slice of silicon at the junction that has been emptied of mobile carriers to balance the charge on the metal, and that width shrinks as the doping rises, because more dopant atoms per volume balance the same charge in a thinner layer. Degenerate doping of the silicon surface (~10²⁰ dopant atoms per cm³ or more) therefore squeezes the step to a few nanometres and makes it tunnel-transparent, and forming a **silicide** (a metal-silicon compound, here TiSi_x, grown by reacting a thin titanium film with the silicon) gives an intimate, oxide-free interface for the tunnelling to happen across. The quality of that interface is quoted as **contact resistivity**, resistance × area in Ω·cm², so dividing by the contact area gives the ohms of one contact. The MOL is built after the replacement metal gate (Module 11) and before the first damascene copper, and it is distinct from the BEOL in that it uses different metals (tungsten, cobalt, ruthenium rather than copper) and tolerates a different **thermal budget** (the total time-at-temperature a wafer can take before something already built on it degrades). The MOL can still see silicide anneals at ~500 °C; once copper and porous low-k are down, the ceiling drops to ~400 °C, above which copper diffuses through its barrier and the low-k loses its carbon.

> **Worked example: contact resistance.** The contact resistivity target is below ~1 × 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm². Over a 20 × 40 nm contact, area = 20 × 10⁻⁷ cm × 40 × 10⁻⁷ cm = 8 × 10⁻¹² cm², so R = 10⁻⁹ / 8 × 10⁻¹² ≈ 125 Ω per contact. That is comparable to the **on-resistance** of the transistor it feeds (the resistance between source and drain when the gate is fully on: of order a few hundred ohms up to ~1 kΩ for a small logic transistor, depending on how many fins or sheets wide it is), and there is a contact at each end of the channel, so the pair can rival the transistor itself. That is why the silicide interface is one of the biggest parasitic resistances in the whole device, and why every 2× in contact resistivity shows up directly in **drive current** (the current the transistor delivers when fully on, which sets how fast it can charge the wire hanging on its drain).

The pieces, in the order they are built:

- **Contact hole etch (self-aligned contact, SAC)**: the ~20 × 40 nm holes are cut through the ~50 to 100 nm of oxide that fills the space between neighbouring gates, an aspect ratio of ~3 to 5, at a gate pitch of only ~50 nm, by fluorocarbon plasma in a plasma etcher (Lam, TEL and Applied supply them; the reactor type is described in step 4 of the damascene flow below). At that pitch no lithography can place a hole between two gates with margin to spare, so the etch is made **self-aligned** (the hole's landing position is fixed by a structure already on the wafer rather than by how well the mask was aligned): the gate wears a silicon nitride cap and nitride spacers on its flanks, and the oxide etch is tuned to be ~10:1 or more selective to nitride, so a hole that overlaps a gate by a few nanometres stops on the nitride and lands only on the epitaxial source/drain between the spacers. The hole must reach the epi without gouging it and without leaving fluorocarbon polymer on its floor, because everything that follows sits on that surface; this is part of why the contact and V0 layers are among the most defect-sensitive features on the die (below).
- **Trench silicide contact**: after opening the contact to the epitaxial source/drain, a thin Ti layer (~3 to 5 nm) is deposited (PVD or ALD), capped with **titanium nitride (TiN)** (a hard, conductive ceramic used throughout the BEOL as a liner and mask), and annealed to form TiSi_x at the interface. Reaching ~10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² requires very high active dopant concentration at the epi surface.
- **Source/drain contact plug**: foundries call this layer CA (Intel-style) or MD (TSMC-style); you only need "source/drain contact". The hole is filled with a metal. For decades this was CVD tungsten from WF₆ (tungsten hexafluoride) + H₂ with a Ti/TiN adhesion layer that doubles as a **fluorine barrier** (WF₆ releases fluorine as it reacts, and free fluorine attacks the titanium silicide and the silicon beneath it, so a few nanometres of TiN must seal them off), on tools such as Lam's ALTUS. CVD tungsten grows inward from every wall and meets in the middle, leaving a **seam** (a weak, higher-resistance line down the centre). Starting around 7 nm class nodes, cobalt plugs (CVD Co from a cobalt carbonyl precursor on an Applied Endura/Volta chamber, with a thin TiN liner from ALD tools such as ASM's or Applied's, and a **reflow** anneal in which the cobalt is heated until it flows like solder into the hole) replaced tungsten in many flows because Co fills narrow holes without a seam and needs a thinner liner. The current direction is fluorine-free tungsten, ruthenium and molybdenum, discussed below.
- **Gate contact** (CB or MP in the same naming schemes): contacts to the gate metal, historically placed over isolation to avoid shorting to the source/drain contact. Intel's 10 nm process introduced **contact over active gate (COAG)** to reclaim that area. The picture: the gate wears a silicon nitride (SiN) hat and the source/drain contact a carbon-doped oxide (SiOC) hat, and an etch chemistry that removes one hat leaves the other, so a gate contact can land partly over the neighbouring contact without shorting to it. The cost is a self-aligned contact scheme with two cap materials that must etch selectively against each other.
- **M0 local interconnect**: the first level of horizontal wire, at the tightest pitch on the die (~23 to 30 nm), running perpendicular to the gates and used to strap source/drain contacts and gates inside the standard cell. At several foundries M0 is still copper with a cobalt liner; Intel 10 nm made M0 and M1 pure cobalt, and ruthenium is the leading candidate to replace both at N2/A16-class pitches (as of 2025).

Everything below M1 is patterned with EUV, often double-patterned, and the vias between M0 and M1 (V0) are among the most defect-sensitive features in the process because they are sub-20 nm holes that must land on sub-20 nm lines with a few nanometres of overlay margin.

> **What can go wrong.** *V0 opens*: an EUV stochastic defect leaves a via hole unprinted, and one **net** (one electrically connected set of terminals in the design) is simply not connected. *Contact-resistance tails*: an incomplete silicide reaction or a whisper of residual oxide under the titanium leaves a minority of contacts at several times the target resistance, which shows up as slow or leaky transistors. *COAG shorts*: if the two hats do not etch selectively enough, the gate contact touches the source/drain contact. All three are caught before any die is tested, by contact and via chains in the **scribe line** (the test strips between dies that the saw later cuts through; a chain is thousands of contacts in series, read as one resistance) and by e-beam voltage-contrast inspection, where an unconnected contact charges up under the electron beam and looks bright.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The M0 pitch and the number of M0 tracks that fit across a standard cell set the cell height (Module 11), and cell height times gate pitch is the area of every logic gate on the die. A tighter M0 is a denser chip; that is why M0 gets the most expensive lithography on the wafer, and why relaxing it (as backside power allows) is a big deal.

## From Aluminum to Copper

### The aluminum era

From the 1960s to the late 1990s interconnect was aluminum (usually Al with 0.5 to 1 percent Cu added to suppress **electromigration (EM)**, the slow drift of metal atoms pushed along by the current, which gets its own section below, and sometimes 1 percent Si to prevent **spiking**, in which aluminum dissolves silicon and punches a metal spike through the shallow junction beneath the contact). It was patterned **subtractively**: blanket-sputter a Ti/TiN/Al-Cu/TiN stack, lithographically define the lines, plasma-etch the metal with Cl₂/BCl₃ (aluminum chlorides are volatile), strip resist, deposit SiO₂ over and between the lines by PECVD or **high-density-plasma CVD** (HDP-CVD, a deposition that sputters as it deposits so it can fill narrow gaps; Module 06), planarize by oxide CMP, etch via holes, and fill them with CVD tungsten plugs (blanket W deposition followed by **etch-back**, a blanket plasma etch that removes the film from the field and leaves it only in the holes, or by W CMP). W plugs were needed because sputtered Al cannot fill a narrow hole; W from WF₆ deposits conformally. The flow is intuitive: deposit the metal everywhere, then etch away everything except the lines you want, as you would with any FEOL film.

Aluminum's problems were its resistivity (2.65 µΩ·cm bulk, 60 percent higher than Cu) and its electromigration resistance. Recall the **activation energy E_a**: the energy hill an atom must climb to hop to a neighbouring site, with the hop rate proportional to exp(−E_a/kT), so that every extra 0.1 eV of hill slows diffusion at 100 °C by ~20×. Aluminum's activation energy for diffusion is low, ~0.5 to 0.7 eV, so it fails at current densities of a few × 10⁵ A/cm². For scale (the Electromigration section does the arithmetic): 1 MA/cm² = 10⁶ A/cm² is only 8 µA in a 20 × 40 nm line, so a few × 10⁵ A/cm² is a couple of microamps in a tight wire, a current that ordinary signals and any power rail reach without trying. Its atoms also move under thermal stress to form **hillocks** (bumps that grow out of the line and can short to the level above). By the 0.25 µm generation both resistivity and electromigration had become limiting.

### Why copper, and why it cannot be etched

Copper has ρ = 1.68 µΩ·cm, and its electromigration activation energy in a damascene line is around 0.8 to 1 eV (interface-limited; lattice diffusion in bulk copper is ~2 eV), versus ~0.5 to 0.7 eV for aluminum, giving orders of magnitude longer life at a given current density. IBM announced the first production copper process (CMOS 7S, 0.22 µm; CMOS, complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, is the logic family of Module 11, and 7S was IBM's process name) in September 1997, with Motorola close behind; TSMC and others followed within two to three years.

But copper cannot be dry-etched at practical temperatures. The plasma etch products that make aluminum etchable (AlCl₃ boils at ~180 °C) have no copper equivalent: CuCl and CuCl₂ have negligible vapor pressure below roughly 200 to 250 °C, and photoresist cannot survive those wafer temperatures, nor could the sidewalls be controlled. Copper also diffuses rapidly through silicon dioxide and silicon, where it creates **deep-level traps**, energy states in the middle of the band gap (the energy range that electrons in a semiconductor cannot normally occupy) that capture carriers and make junctions leak (Module 10), so it must be sealed on every side by a diffusion barrier. Both constraints pushed the industry to invert the process: instead of depositing metal and etching it away, etch the pattern into the dielectric first, fill with metal, and polish off the excess. This is the **damascene** process, named after the Damascus craft of inlaying metal into a carved surface. The **dual damascene** version patterns a via level and the line level above it into one dielectric stack, fills both in one metallization, and polishes once per level, halving the number of fill and CMP steps compared to a single-damascene via then single-damascene line.

## The Dual Damascene Flow, Step by Step

What follows is one metal level, roughly M2 through M6 on a current node. Repeat it ~15 times with different dimensions and you have a BEOL. Each level involves on the order of 30 to 40 individual process operations, which is why the BEOL accounts for roughly half of the ~1,000+ steps and ~a third of the cycle time of a leading-edge logic wafer. ### 1. Etch-stop / capping layer

The previous level's copper, freshly polished, is covered with a dielectric **cap**, typically 10 to 25 nm of PECVD SiCN (silicon carbonitride, deposited from trimethylsilane and NH₃ at ~350 to 400 °C) or an aluminum nitride / aluminum oxide (AlN/AlO_x) bilayer at the most advanced nodes. This layer does three things: it seals the copper against oxidation and out-diffusion, it acts as an **etch stop** (a layer the next level's via etch removes much more slowly than the low-k, so the etch halts on it) and its interface with the copper is the dominant path for electromigration (below). Its k value (~4.5 to 5.5 for SiCN) is much higher than the low-k bulk, and it hurts more than its thickness suggests because it sits exactly between the top corners of neighbouring lines, where the lateral field is strongest; so it is kept as thin as possible, around 10 nm at N5-class nodes. Ten nanometres is about the floor: thinner films develop pinholes through which copper escapes, and a cap that does not adhere well to the copper makes the electromigration path faster.

### 2. Low-k dielectric

> **Intuition: what k actually measures.** Put an insulator in an electric field and its electrons shift a little toward the positive side and its atoms and bonds stretch and rotate; every such shift stores extra charge on the plates. The dielectric constant k is the sum of all that shifting. A **polarizable** bond is one whose charge moves easily in a field; lighter, stiffer, less polar bonds shift less; and an empty pore shifts nothing at all (k = 1). So there are only two ways down from SiO₂'s 3.9: swap polar Si-O bonds for stiffer, non-polar ones, or replace some of the solid with nothing.

The **inter-level dielectric (ILD)**, the insulator that fills the space between and around this level's wires, is deposited next: **low-k** material, meaning a dielectric constant below the 3.9 to 4.2 of PECVD SiO₂. The workhorse is **SiOC:H** (carbon-doped oxide, organosilicate glass, OSG), deposited by PECVD from organosilicon precursors such as diethoxymethylsilane (DEMS) or octamethylcyclotetrasiloxane (OMCTS) with oxygen or CO₂ at ~250 to 400 °C. Replacing Si-O bonds with less polarizable Si-CH₃ groups drops k to ~2.9 to 3.0 for "dense" SiOC:H. To go lower, the film is made porous: a sacrificial organic **porogen** (a pore-generator, e.g. alpha-terpinene or a cyclic hydrocarbon) is co-deposited, then driven out by a UV cure (broadband UV at ~350 to 400 °C for a few minutes), leaving pores of 1 to 3 nm and 20 to 30 percent porosity. Since air has k = 1, porous SiOC:H reaches k ≈ 2.4 to 2.7. Applied Materials' Black Diamond series on the Producer platform has dominated this segment since the early 2000s; Lam (Vector) and ASM offer alternatives, and spin-on organosilicates from JSR, Dow and others have been used at some fabs. The full ILD stack for one level is roughly 80 to 150 nm at 1× levels and several hundred nm at upper levels.

Porosity is bought with pain. Porous low-k has low **modulus** (stiffness: how hard the material resists being squeezed), ~5 to 10 GPa versus 70 GPa for SiO₂ (gigapascals, a pressure unit), which makes it about as stiff as a hard plastic, and CMP will press on it at ~1 psi; it has low fracture toughness, low thermal conductivity, and it soaks up moisture and plasma damage. The industry spent a decade at k ≈ 2.5 to 2.7 and has largely stopped pushing lower; the practical lever now is thinner caps and airgaps (deliberately leaving an empty gap between neighbouring lines instead of dielectric, k = 1; see Airgaps under The New Metals) rather than a lower bulk k. The **effective k** of a real stack, including caps, hard mask residue and damaged sidewalls, is typically 0.3 to 0.5 higher than the bulk film value. The payoff of every step down is easy to state: both wire delay (RC) and dynamic power (C × V² × f) are proportional to C, and C is proportional to k, so every 0.1 taken off k is ~3 percent off both (0.1 divided by an effective k of ~3; nearer 4 percent against the bulk 2.7). That is the rule of thumb in the Key Numbers table.

> **What can go wrong.** *Moisture uptake*: an open-pore film left exposed drinks water from the air, and water has k ≈ 80, so a few percent by volume undoes the whole porosity gain; the fixes are pore sealing and short queue times. *Plasma damage*: every etch and strip burns carbon out of the sidewalls (step 5). *Cracking and delamination*: a film at 5 GPa modulus with low fracture toughness cracks under the stress the package applies when the chip is soldered and cycled, the chip-package interaction problem picked up in Other Reliability Mechanisms; it is why the upper levels return to denser dielectrics. Film k and porosity are tracked by ellipsometry (a polarized-light film measurement, Module 13) and by capacitance test structures on every lot.

### 3. Hard masks

The problem: EUV resist is only ~30 nm thick, but the via-plus-trench etch has to remove well over 100 nm of low-k, and a fluorocarbon etch consumes resist almost as fast as it consumes oxide. The resist would be gone before the etch finished. So the pattern is first transferred into a tougher **hard mask** stacked on top of the low-k: typically a thin **TEOS oxide** (SiO₂ deposited from tetraethyl orthosilicate, ~10 to 30 nm), then a **TiN metal hard mask** (~15 to 35 nm, by PVD), then sometimes a second oxide. The TiN gives high etch selectivity against the low-k in the fluorocarbon trench etch, defines the trench with better **line-edge roughness** (the nanometre-scale wobble of a line's edge) than resist, and allows the trench pattern to be "stored" in the hard mask while the via is patterned separately. Its drawbacks are stress and the risk of TiN residue and metal contamination; several fabs have moved to **amorphous carbon** (a hard, etch-resistant carbon film without crystal order) or SiN masks at specific layers.

### 4. Lithography and etch: via-first or trench-first

The goal is a T-shaped cavity: a narrow via at the bottom and a wider trench on top, cut into one block of dielectric, so that one fill makes both the plug and the wire. Two lithography steps define it, one for the vias and one for the trenches, and the art is in the order. In the common **trench-first metal hard mask (TFMHM)** integration:

1. **Trench lithography and TiN open.** The trench pattern is exposed (EUV at the tight layers, 193i above) and etched into the TiN hard mask only, stopping on the oxide beneath; the resist is stripped. The trench pattern is now stored in the TiN.
2. **Via lithography.** A planarizing organic layer fills the TiN openings so that the via resist is spun onto a flat surface (the TiN now has 15 to 35 nm of steps in it, and resist spun over steps is uneven in thickness and prints out of focus), fresh resist goes on, and the via pattern is exposed over the TiN-patterned stack. Where a via image overlaps a TiN opening the etch can proceed; where it spills over the TiN, the TiN blocks it. The via is thus **self-aligned** (its position is fixed by an existing structure, the trench opening, rather than by mask alignment) in the direction perpendicular to the line: this is the **self-aligned via (SAV)** scheme, and it means a via can only exist where a trench also exists.
3. **Partial via etch.** Fluorocarbon plasma etches the via hole part-way down through the low-k; the resist and planarizing layer are stripped.
4. **Trench etch.** With only the TiN mask exposed, the trench is etched into the top portion of the low-k everywhere the TiN is open, while the via hole, already ahead, deepens simultaneously until it reaches the SiCN etch stop. Note the asymmetry: the via has a stop layer, the trench does not. The trench depth is set by time alone (there is nothing inside the low-k to halt on), which is why the low-k thickness and the etch-rate uniformity across the wafer are specified so tightly, since any drift in either becomes a drift in line height and so in resistance; some integrations bury a thin etch-stop film at the trench-floor depth to take the timing out of it, at the cost of a little extra k.
5. **Etch-stop open.** A short, chemically different step punches through the SiCN at the via bottom to expose the copper below, without sputtering copper onto the sidewalls.

The **via-first** sequence reverses the order and was common through the 45 nm era. Either way the etch is fluorocarbon plasma (C₄F₈ or C₄F₆ with Ar, N₂, O₂) in a **capacitively coupled plasma (CCP)** reactor (the plasma is driven between two parallel electrodes, one carrying the wafer; Module 09) such as Lam Flex or TEL Vigus. The recipe balances polymer deposition on the sidewalls, which gives **anisotropy** (etching down but not sideways, because the polymer protects the walls) and selectivity to the TiN, against etch rate, and it uses low ion energy to limit low-k damage. Representative numbers, all approximate: a low-k etch rate of order ~100 to 300 nm/min, selectivity to the TiN mask of ~10:1 or better, ion energies of ~tens to a few hundred eV, and a minute or two of plasma time per level. **Overlay**, how well this layer's via lands on the line below, has a budget of a few nanometres at the tight levels: the via must land inside the width of the line beneath it, and misalignment steals cross section from the via and can create a short to the neighbouring line, which is what SAV guards against. For EUV-patterned levels, via lithography **stochastics** (random missing or merged holes from photon shot noise, Module 08) are one of the most important yield limiters on the die.

> **What can go wrong.** *Via-to-neighbour short*: an overlay error pushes the via onto the gap and it touches the adjacent line, which SAV prevents in one direction only. *Missing or merged vias* from EUV stochastics: an open, or two nets bridged. *Trench kinks and micro-trenching*: ions reflected off the sidewall dig a groove at the trench foot, so the trench bottom is not flat and the barrier is thin there. *Low-k sidewall damage*: too much ion energy or oxygen strips the carbon and raises k. Detection: **CD-SEM** images (critical-dimension scanning electron microscope: an electron microscope used to measure feature widths) of the etched pattern for shape and width, **comb-serpentine** leakage structures (a long folded line interleaved with neighbouring lines; any short between them reads as leakage) for shorts, via chains for opens, all in the scribe line and read at **parametric test** (the electrical measurement of scribe-line structures on every wafer, Module 13).

### 5. Clean and low-k damage

After etch the wafer carries fluorocarbon polymer residue, TiN etch by-products, and a damaged layer on the exposed low-k sidewalls: the plasma has stripped the methyl groups from the surface SiOC:H, leaving a **hydrophilic** (water-loving) SiO_x skin that adsorbs water and raises k. The wet clean (dilute HF, hydrofluoric acid, is too aggressive for porous material; dilute organic acid or amine-based strippers from Qnity (formerly DuPont), EMD and Entegris are used, in single-wafer spin tools from SCREEN or TEL) must remove residue and any copper oxide at the via bottom without etching the damaged low-k. Some flows add a **silylation** repair step (exposure to a silicon-bearing vapor such as **hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS)** or **dimethyldimethoxysilane (DMDMS)**, small molecules that carry methyl groups on a silicon atom and re-attach them to the dangling Si-OH sites) or a pore-sealing ALD skin. Resist strip is done with H₂/N₂ or CO₂ plasma rather than oxygen plasma, because O₂ **ashing** (burning the resist off in an oxygen plasma) also burns the carbon out of the low-k and is devastating to it.

### 6. Barrier and liner

Two problems, two films. First, copper atoms wander through oxide (the deep-level trap problem above), so the cavity must be sealed with a film copper cannot cross. TaN is chosen because it is **amorphous** (glass-like, with no crystal grains) and so has no grain boundaries for copper atoms to diffuse along, and because it is chemically stable against both copper and the dielectric. Second, copper does not wet TaN: a thin copper film on bare TaN beads up into islands like water on wax. So a **liner** of cobalt or ruthenium, which copper does wet, goes on top of the barrier.

The wafer goes into a vacuum cluster tool, almost universally an Applied Materials **Endura**, which holds well over half of the PVD market and a dominant position in copper barrier/seed. Inside, without breaking vacuum:

- A **degas** (a bake to drive moisture out of the porous low-k) and a pre-clean (argon sputter or reactive H₂ plasma) remove moisture and the copper oxide at the via bottom. Etching too hard here re-sputters copper onto the low-k sidewall; too soft leaves an oxide that raises via resistance.
- **Barrier**: 1.5 to 2 nm of TaN, deposited by PVD (ionized, with a **resputter** step, an argon bombardment that knocks barrier off the via bottom and onto the sidewalls, thinning the film that sits in series with the current) or at the tightest levels by ALD (from a tantalum **amide** precursor, a molecule in which tantalum is bonded to nitrogen-carbon groups, with NH₃/H₂ plasma) for conformality. The historical Ta/TaN bilayer (Ta for adhesion of the Cu seed) has thinned to TaN plus a separate liner.
- **Liner**: 1 to 2 nm of cobalt (CVD from dicobalt octacarbonyl derivatives, on an Applied Volta chamber) or ruthenium (CVD or ALD). On Co or Ru the copper seed spreads into a continuous film and, at elevated temperature, reflows into the via.

The total barrier + liner budget per sidewall is ~3 to 4 nm and does not scale with pitch, which is the root of the "barrier share" problem from earlier. The arithmetic shows why tenths of a nanometre are fought over: in the 20 nm line of the worked example the copper core is 13 nm wide, so shaving 0.5 nm off the barrier on each of the two sidewalls widens the core by 1 nm, ~8 percent more copper cross section and ~8 percent less line resistance from the same trench, before counting the further gain from the wider core scattering fewer electrons off its walls.

> **What can go wrong.** *Discontinuous barrier*: a gap in the TaN on a rough or damaged sidewall lets copper drift into the low-k under the operating field, and the dielectric between neighbouring lines eventually breaks down (time-dependent dielectric breakdown, TDDB, treated in Other Reliability Mechanisms); found by comb-serpentine leakage and by **transmission electron microscopy (TEM)** of the sidewall (an electron beam shone through a slice thin enough to be transparent, resolving individual atomic layers). *Too-thick bottom barrier*: without enough resputter the TaN on the via floor is the single largest resistance in the via (see Vias). *Over-aggressive pre-clean*: the argon sputter throws copper from the via bottom onto the low-k sidewall, where no barrier will ever cover it; seen as leakage in the same comb structures.

### 7. Copper seed

Electroplating needs a conductive surface, so a thin **seed layer** of copper is sputtered in a PVD chamber on the same Endura: a few nanometres to ~20 nm depending on level. The problem with ordinary sputtering is that neutral atoms fly off the target in all directions and pile up at the trench mouth, the easiest place to hit, forming an **overhang** that shadows the lower sidewalls before they are coated. The solution is a high-ionization (self-ionized plasma) source: most of the sputtered copper is ionized in a dense plasma, and a negative bias on the wafer pulls the ions in at near-normal incidence so they reach the bottom and, glancing off, the sidewalls. Even so the seed is never truly conformal: thick at the top, thin low on the walls. At 1× levels a **copper reflow** step (heating the wafer to 200 to 400 °C in the tool for tens of seconds) lets the seed diffuse along the cobalt liner into the bottom of the via, partially filling it before plating even begins; on Co and Ru liners copper can also be plated directly onto the liner with a very thin or no seed.

> **What can go wrong.** If the overhang pinches the mouth shut before the plating has filled the feature, a void is sealed inside: an open via, or a via with a fraction of its cross section that fails early by electromigration. Seed voids are found by e-beam voltage contrast (a floating via looks different from a connected one) and by via-chain resistance, and the process fix is more ionization, more reflow, or a thinner seed on a better liner.

### 8. Electrochemical deposition (ECD)

> **Intuition: a battery run backwards.** In a battery, a metal dissolves at one electrode, gives up electrons, and those electrons do work on their way to the other electrode. An electroplating cell is the same cell with a power supply forcing the current the other way. The supply pushes electrons into the wafer; every Cu²⁺ ion in the liquid that touches the wafer's surface takes two of them and becomes a copper atom stuck to the surface; and at the other electrode, a slab of copper dissolves to replace the ions the liquid lost. Count the electrons and you have counted the atoms, which is what Faraday's law says.

The wafer moves to a plating tool: Lam Research's **SABRE** family (SABRE Extreme/Max/Excel for damascene; the SABRE 3D variant serves packaging plating). Lam holds a large majority of the copper damascene plating market; Applied's Raider is the main alternative. Each plating cell is a cup in which the wafer is held face-down on a rotating head, its edge contacted by a sealed ring of electrical contacts, and immersed in acidic copper sulfate electrolyte (CuSO₄ giving ~40 g/L Cu²⁺, sulfuric acid H₂SO₄ ~10 to 100 g/L, Cl⁻ ~50 ppm, parts per million) with a copper anode below. Current flows from anode to wafer (the cathode); at the wafer surface Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.

Plating alone would coat every surface at the same rate, pinch off the top of a narrow trench (where the seed is thickest) and leave a void inside. What makes damascene possible is **superfill** (bottom-up fill), engineered with three organic additives at ppm concentrations. Two words first: the **overpotential** is the extra voltage, beyond the equilibrium value, needed to make copper deposit at a given rate, and a film adsorbed on the surface that blocks the reaction raises it; a **diffusion-limited** molecule is one so large and scarce that it arrives at the surface slowly and runs out inside narrow holes.

- **Suppressor**: a polyether such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) or a PEG/PPG (polypropylene glycol) copolymer, molecular weight in the thousands, which together with chloride ions adsorbs on the copper surface and forms a blocking film that raises the overpotential needed to deposit. Because it is large and diffusion-limited, it reaches flat surfaces and trench mouths readily but cannot penetrate the bottom of a narrow feature quickly.
- **Accelerator** (brightener): a small sulfur-containing molecule, typically bis(3-sulfopropyl) disulfide (SPS) or its monomer MPS, which displaces the suppressor and catalyzes copper deposition where it sits. It adsorbs everywhere, but here is the trick: as the bottom of a trench fills, its surface area shrinks and the accelerator molecules already there are concentrated (they do not desorb), so the deposition rate at the bottom accelerates while the suppressed field remains slow. This is the **curvature-enhanced accelerator coverage (CEAC)** mechanism (Moffat and coworkers at NIST, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, ~2000). The fill proceeds from the bottom up and, in a via, sweeps upward like a piston.
- **Leveler**: a nitrogen-containing polymer or dye (e.g. Janus Green B, or proprietary quaternary ammonium polymers), strongly adsorbing and present at very low concentration, which is consumed under diffusion control preferentially at protruding areas and high-current regions. It quenches the accelerated bump that would otherwise form over each filled feature, giving a flatter overburden for CMP.

Chemistry vendors are Atotech (now MKS), Qnity Electronics (the DuPont electronics business, separated in November 2025; the former Rohm and Haas / Dow Electronic Materials line), MacDermid Alpha (Element Solutions; the former Enthone ViaForm line) and Moses Lake Industries; the exact additive structures are trade secrets, and the bath is continuously analyzed by **cyclic voltammetric stripping (CVS)**, an electrochemical assay that measures each additive's concentration from how much it changes the plating rate on a small test electrode, and dosed. The current density is stepped: an initial low-current stage (~2 to 5 mA/cm²) to protect the thin seed and fill the features, then higher current (~20 to 40 mA/cm²) to build the **overburden**, the 200 to 800 nm blanket of copper above the trenches that CMP will remove. Wafer rotation and shaped anodes/shields manage the **terminal effect** (the voltage drop across the thin seed from the edge contact to the centre, which would otherwise plate the edge faster). Plating is at room temperature and takes one to three minutes per wafer.

**Faraday's law** is the accounting rule for plating: the charge passed equals the number of electrons per atom (2 for Cu²⁺) × 96,485 coulombs per mole of electrons × the moles of metal deposited.

> **Worked example: charge, current and time to plate one wafer.**
> Suppose a 300 mm wafer at a 2× level needs 600 nm of copper (feature fill plus overburden). Plated area ≈ π (15 cm)² ≈ 707 cm² (edge exclusion ignored). Volume = 707 cm² × 6 × 10⁻⁵ cm = 0.0424 cm³. Mass = 0.0424 × 8.96 g/cm³ = 0.380 g. Moles Cu = 0.380 / 63.55 = 5.98 × 10⁻³ mol. Faraday's law with n = 2 electrons per atom: Q = 2 × 96,485 C/mol × 5.98 × 10⁻³ = 1,154 C. At a bulk current density of 20 mA/cm², I = 0.02 × 707 = 14.1 A, so t = 1,154 / 14.1 ≈ 82 s for the bulk stage. Add a 20 to 30 s low-current fill stage at 3 mA/cm² (2.1 A, depositing ~20 nm) and the entire plate takes under two minutes, which is why a multi-cell SABRE with one robot can sustain on the order of 100 or more wafers per hour (vendor throughput figures are configuration-dependent). Plating efficiency is close to 100 percent for acid copper, so the Faraday estimate is nearly exact. Note the numbers: half a gram of copper per wafer per level, ~7 g of copper on a finished wafer, almost all of it polished off again.

### 9. Anneal

The problem the anneal solves is that the copper coming out of the plating cell is not yet the copper you want. Plated at room temperature in a minute or two, it is a mosaic of fine grains, tens of nanometres across, laced with traces of the plating additives, and it is **metastable** (stable for the moment, but sitting above a lower-energy state it will fall into given a nudge). A film in that state changes on its own: it **self-anneals** at room temperature over hours, **recrystallizing** (its atoms rearranging into fewer, larger grains) with a resistance drop of ~20 percent. A wafer whose resistance is still drifting cannot be polished or measured reproducibly, so the fab forces the change to completion deliberately, all at once, before CMP.

> **Intuition: why grains grow.** Every grain boundary is a sheet of misfitting atoms, and misfit costs energy, so a film with many small grains stores more energy than the same film as a few big ones. At room temperature the atoms lack the energy to fix this quickly; warm the film and atoms at each boundary hop across it, the larger grains swallow their smaller neighbours, boundary area shrinks, and the film settles into its lower-energy state. Fewer boundaries means fewer of the grain-boundary reflections from the size-effect section, which is where the ~20 percent comes from.

The recipe is a **forming-gas** anneal (N₂ with a few percent H₂; the hydrogen keeps the bare copper surface reduced, since any oxygen at these temperatures would grow a copper oxide that CMP then removes unevenly) at 150 to 400 °C for minutes to an hour, on either a hotplate station attached to the plating tool or a batch furnace (Applied and TEL supply both types). The upper limit is set by the porous low-k and the barrier, neither of which tolerates more than ~400 °C, and the lower levels are annealed near the top of that range because their narrow lines need the most help. The result is grains as large as the line geometry permits: in the blanket overburden they grow to ~1 µm or more, but inside a 20 nm trench a grain cannot be wider than the trench, so the copper there becomes a **bamboo structure**: grains stacked like the segments of a bamboo cane, each spanning the full wire width, so that an atom migrating along the wire must cross each boundary head-on instead of sliding along it, which is good for electromigration. Cooling from the anneal also leaves the copper in tension, because copper shrinks about seven times more than the silicon it is clamped to (17 versus ~2.6 ppm/K), and that residual stress is the seed of the stress-voiding mechanism treated in Other Reliability Mechanisms. The anneal must happen before CMP, since polishing a film that is still recrystallizing gives an unstable removal rate, and its completeness is checked on the spot by measuring **sheet resistance** (the resistance of a square of film, whatever its size, read with a four-point probe: four needles in a row, two to force a current and two to sense the voltage) on monitor wafers: a film that has finished recrystallizing reads ~20 percent below its as-plated value and stops moving.

> **What can go wrong.** *Stress voids*: the tensile stress left after cooling pulls vacancies toward the stress concentration under a via, most of all a small via sitting on a wide line, and over months of operation at 150 to 200 °C they collect into a void that opens the via (stress-induced voiding, below); the process levers are the anneal temperature and ramp rate, the cap adhesion and the copper purity, and the design lever is the redundant-via rule. *Under-anneal*: too cool or too short, and the film keeps recrystallizing through CMP, so the removal rate wanders and line resistance drifts for days after the level is finished; caught by sheet resistance that has not settled and by parametric line resistance that differs between lots. *Oxidation*: a leak or a poor purge lets oxygen into the anneal ambient, and the copper surface grows Cu₂O that CMP must then clear; seen as colour on the wafer and as a CMP endpoint that arrives late. *Over-temperature*: above ~400 °C the low-k loses carbon and the copper can extrude out of the trench as a hillock, so the anneal tools interlock on temperature and the recipe stays well inside the limit.

### 10. Copper CMP

**Chemical mechanical planarization (CMP)** removes the overburden and the barrier from the field, leaving copper only inside the trenches and vias, with a surface flat enough that the next level's lithography is in focus (depth of focus at EUV is on the order of 100 nm). The tool is a rotary polisher: Applied Materials **Reflexion LK Prime** (Applied holds around 70 percent of the CMP tool market, estimated, as of 2025), with Ebara (F-REX) as the main alternative, strong in Japan and Korea. The wafer is held face-down in a carrier head with a flexible membrane and several concentric pressure zones, pressed against a rotating polyurethane **pad** (IC1000 and successors from Qnity, formerly DuPont; grooved, ~1 to 2 mm thick) on a platen ~750 mm in diameter, with **slurry** dripped onto the pad at 150 to 300 mL/min. The head and platen spin at ~50 to 120 rpm, and a diamond-grit **conditioner** disk (3M, Kinik, Entegris) sweeps the pad to keep its surface rough and open.

The mechanism for copper is chemical first, mechanical second. Copper is too ductile to abrade cleanly, so the slurry contains an oxidizer (hydrogen peroxide, ~1 to 3 percent) that converts the surface to copper oxide (CuO/Cu₂O), a **complexing agent** (glycine, citric acid or another amino or organic acid: a molecule that grabs a metal ion and wraps around it, forming a soluble **complex**, so the copper oxide dissolves into the slurry instead of staying put on the surface), and a **corrosion inhibitor**, benzotriazole (BTA), that forms a passivating Cu-BTA film on the copper. On the high points of the wafer the pad and abrasive particles (colloidal silica or alumina, ~50 to 100 nm, at ~0.5 to 3 percent by weight) mechanically strip the BTA/oxide film and expose fresh copper, which is immediately oxidized and dissolved; in recessed areas the inhibitor film stays intact and removal nearly stops. This is what makes the process **planarizing** rather than merely thinning. Removal rate follows the Preston relation, RR = K_p × P × v, where P is the pressure, v the relative velocity of pad and wafer, and K_p the **Preston coefficient**, an empirical constant for a given pad, slurry and material; for bulk copper the rate is around 300 to 800 nm/min at 1 to 2 psi (1 **psi**, pound per square inch, is ≈6.9 kPa, roughly a fingertip resting on a table, though across a 300 mm wafer it adds up to ~50 kg of force). At 500 nm/min, the 600 nm of overburden from the plating example takes ~1.2 min on the first platen. Porous low-k with its low modulus forced pressures down from ~5 psi in the oxide era to ~1 psi or less, which is part of why removal rates and throughput are limited.

Copper CMP is a multi-platen sequence, one platen per step:

1. **Bulk copper removal** at high rate on platen 1, stopped a few tens of nanometres above the barrier using an **eddy-current endpoint** sensor embedded in the platen (a coil under the pad induces circulating currents in the copper film; the thinner the film, the weaker the response, so the sensor reads the remaining copper thickness in situ) combined with optical reflectance.
2. **Soft landing / copper clearing** on platen 2 at lower rate and pressure, with a slurry selective to copper over TaN, until the barrier is exposed everywhere; the endpoint here is the change in optical reflectance or motor torque as TaN appears.
3. **Barrier removal and buff** on platen 3 with a different slurry (often with a different **pH**, the acidity scale, and abrasive) that removes TaN and the Co liner from the field, removes a controlled ~5 to 20 nm of the hard mask or low-k to clear residual metal, and smooths the surface.

Two defects define the process window. **Dishing** is the recess of the copper surface below the surrounding dielectric within a wide line (the pad flexes into the soft copper); **erosion** is the thinning of the dielectric in a dense array of narrow lines, where the local pattern density is high and the dielectric between lines wears faster. Both increase line resistance (less copper cross section) and add topography that accumulates layer over layer. Specs at tight levels are on the order of 10 to 30 nm. Both scale with pattern density, which is why design rules require metal density fill (below).

> **Worked example: what 20 nm of dishing costs.** On a 160 nm-thick M8 power line, 20 nm of dishing leaves 140 nm of copper: resistance rises by 160/140, +14 percent, tolerable on a power rail with margin. On a 40 nm-tall M2 line the same 20 nm would remove half the copper and double the resistance, which is why the tight-level dishing spec is far tighter, why the bulk removal must end well above the barrier, and why density rules exist to keep every region of the die polishing at the same rate.

**Post-CMP clean** happens immediately in a clean module attached to the polisher: double-sided **PVA** brush scrubbing (soft polyvinyl alcohol sponge rollers) with dilute chemistry (BTA-containing or citric acid/**TMAH** based, TMAH being tetramethylammonium hydroxide, an alkaline cleaning base; tuned to remove slurry particles and copper ions without corroding the exposed copper), a **megasonic** rinse (MHz-frequency sound waves that shake particles loose), and a **Marangoni** or spin dry (an alcohol vapor pulls the water off the wafer as it is lifted, leaving no droplets to dry into spots). Copper left exposed for too long corrodes and grows CuO, so the wafer moves to the next cap deposition within a queue time limit of hours.

> **What can go wrong.** *Dishing and erosion* beyond spec, read by **atomic force microscopy (AFM)** (a fine tip traces the surface height) and stylus profilometry on test pads and by line resistance at parametric test. *Scratches* from agglomerated abrasive, each one a trench that later fills with metal and shorts two lines; caught by optical defect inspection. *Residual copper or barrier puddles* left in low spots, which short neighbouring lines; the buff step and comb-serpentine leakage tests exist for these. *Edge corrosion* where the slurry chemistry attacks copper at the wafer bevel (the rounded wafer edge). *Queue-time oxidation* if the cap is late. The eddy-current, optical-reflectance and motor-torque endpoints are the in-tool defence: each platen stops on a physical signal, not a timer.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The 10 to 30 nm of topography one level leaves behind does not go away; the next level is built on it, and over 15 levels the errors accumulate unless each CMP is nearly perfect. Against that stands an EUV depth of focus of ~100 nm: a level whose surface wanders by a fraction of that prints its lines out of focus, blurred and misshapen, and yield falls. CMP planarity is therefore a lithography specification as much as a metal one.

### 11. Cap, and repeat

The wafer goes back to the PECVD tool, receives a pre-clean (NH₃ or H₂ plasma to reduce copper oxide) and the SiCN cap for this level, and the cycle begins again for the next level. The queue time between CMP and cap is limited to hours because the bare copper surface oxidizes and adsorbs contaminants in air, and a cap deposited on a dirty surface adheres poorly and becomes a fast electromigration path. At the levels that matter for electromigration, a selective **metal cap** is added first (below). On a 15-level process the loop from step 1 to step 11 runs 15 times, with dimensions changing by an order of magnitude between the bottom and the top; the top thick-copper levels use thicker seeds, longer plating (5 to 10 minutes), thicker overburden, and a much less critical CMP.

## Vias, Resistance and the Small-Dimension Tax

A **via** is the vertical connection between two adjacent metal levels. On a modern node a 1× via is a roughly 20 nm diameter hole, ~40 to 60 nm deep, filled by the same TaN/Co/Cu as the line above it, sitting on the SiCN-capped copper of the line below (the etch stop is opened at the bottom). Its resistance has three parts: the copper column, the barrier at the bottom (TaN across the whole via footprint, in series with the current, unlike the sidewall barrier which is in parallel), and the interface to the underlying copper, which is sensitive to the pre-clean. Typical single-via resistances are ~10 to 50 Ω at 1× levels, versus a fraction of an ohm at the global levels.

> **Worked example: where a via's ohms come from.** Take the via footprint as ~20 × 20 nm = 400 nm² = 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ m². The floor barrier: 2 nm of TaN at 200 µΩ·cm = 2 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m gives R = ρt/A = 2 × 10⁻⁶ × 2 × 10⁻⁹ / 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 10 Ω, from a film four atoms thick. The copper column: after the sidewall barrier and liner it is ~13 nm across, ~50 nm tall, at ~8 to 10 µΩ·cm, so R = ρL/A ≈ 9 × 10⁻⁸ × 5 × 10⁻⁸ / 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁶ ≈ 25 to 30 Ω. The interface to the copper below adds a few ohms if the pre-clean was good and far more if it was not. Total ~40 Ω, squarely in the quoted 10 to 50 Ω, and a quarter of it is the barrier floor. That floor cannot be scaled away by geometry, which is why hybrid metallization and barrierless ruthenium vias (The New Metals) exist: they remove the one resistor that does not shrink.

Chips have billions of vias, and the statistical tail dominates both yield and reliability; designers place redundant double vias wherever there is space.

> **What can go wrong.** *Partially landed via*: overlay error sets the via half on the line and half on the gap, so it has a fraction of its cross section (high resistance, early electromigration failure) and may touch the neighbour (a short). *Residual oxide at the interface* from a weak pre-clean: tens of extra ohms, and a tail in the distribution. *Seed void* (step 7): an open, or a via with a hidden cavity. *Missing via* from EUV stochastics: an open net. Detection is by via chains in the scribe line (below) and by e-beam voltage-contrast inspection on product wafers, where a floating via charges up and images differently from a connected one. The design response is the redundant via: two vias in parallel wherever the layout allows, so that one tail event does not kill the net.

## Electromigration

> **Intuition: the electron wind.** At a million amps per square centimetre the conduction electrons are a wind blowing along the wire, and each collision with a metal atom gives it a tiny push in the direction the electrons flow. Atoms in the interior are held by their neighbours, but atoms at a fast-diffusing interface can hop, and over months the wind moves them toward the end of the wire the electrons flow into. A void opens wherever atoms leave faster than they arrive. The commonest such place is just past a via, because the barrier under the via is a wall that atoms cannot cross: the wind carries atoms away from it and nothing comes through the wall to replace them.

**Electromigration (EM)** is the drift of metal atoms under the momentum transfer ("electron wind") from the conduction electrons at high current density. Two words borrowed from electrochemistry: the **cathode** end of a wire is where the electrons enter and the **anode** end is where they leave. Atoms move toward the anode (in the direction of electron flow), depleting the cathode end of a line and piling up at the anode end. In copper, the fast diffusion path is not the bulk (activation energy ~2 eV) but the interface between the copper and the dielectric cap, with an activation energy of ~0.8 to 0.9 eV for a SiCN cap; grain boundaries are intermediate. A **vacancy** is a missing atom in the lattice; atoms move by hopping into vacancies, so a flow of atoms one way is a flow of vacancies the other. Voids nucleate where the atomic flux **diverges**, in plain words where more atoms leave a spot than enter it, typically just under a via at the cathode end (the barrier at the via bottom blocks atomic flux, so atoms leave and are not replenished), and an open circuit results.

Lifetime is characterized empirically by **Black's equation**:

MTTF = A × J^(−n) × exp(E_a / kT)

where MTTF is the mean time to failure, A is a fitted constant for the line geometry and process, J is current density, n is the current exponent (≈1 for void growth limited failure, ≈2 for nucleation limited, and fabs fit values in between), E_a the activation energy of the dominant diffusion path, k Boltzmann's constant and T absolute temperature. The exponential in temperature is what makes testing possible: with E_a = 0.9 eV, going from 110 °C in use (383 K) to a 300 °C test (573 K) speeds failure by exp[(E_a/k) × (1/383 − 1/573)] = exp(9.0) ≈ 8,000×, so hours of test stand in for years of use. The current density unit **MA/cm²** (megaamps per square centimetre) sounds enormous, but in a 20 × 40 nm line (8 × 10⁻¹² cm²) 1 MA/cm² is only 8 µA. Tests are run at 300 to 350 °C and 1 to 3 MA/cm² for hours and extrapolated to operating conditions (105 to 125 °C, ~10 years). Design rules then set the maximum allowed current per wire width and per via, and the tools check every net.

Two mechanisms extend the limit. First, the **Blech length**: as atoms pile up at the anode, they build a compressive stress; at the cathode, tensile stress. The resulting stress gradient drives a back-flux of atoms opposite to the electron wind. For a line short enough that the stress gradient balances the wind before a void can nucleate, EM stops. This happens when the product of current density and length is below a critical value, (J × L)_crit ≈ 2,000 to 4,000 A/cm for copper in a rigid dielectric (lower in soft low-k). At J = 1 MA/cm² that is a critical length of ~20 to 40 µm: short lines that are bounded by barriers at both ends are effectively immortal, and design rules exploit this.

Second, the **metal cap**. Since the copper/SiCN interface is the weak path, replacing it with a copper/metal interface raises E_a. IBM and others introduced **electroless** CoWP (cobalt tungsten phosphide) caps around 2005 (electroless plating is driven by a chemical reducing agent in the bath rather than an applied current, so metal grows only on the catalytic copper surface), deposited selectively on exposed copper after CMP; the industry converged instead on a selective CVD cobalt cap a few nanometres thick, applied before the SiCN. Together with the Co liner on the sidewalls, the copper is fully clad in cobalt, E_a rises to ~1.0 to 1.2 eV, and the EM lifetime improves by an order of magnitude or more. This is what Intel calls "enhanced copper" (eCu) at Intel 4/3 and what TSMC is reported to have used since ~N7 (TSMC does not disclose its cap chemistry). It is also why purity matters: the plating bath is continuously purified and the seed target is 6N (99.9999 percent) copper, because sulfur, chlorine or carbon incorporated from additives segregate to grain boundaries and interfaces and become both EM and stress-migration weak points. Copper sputter targets come from Honeywell, JX Nippon Mining & Metals and Tosoh.

> **What can go wrong.** *Cathode void under a via*: the classic failure, a resistance rise followed by an open. *Extrusion at the anode*: the piled-up atoms burst through the cap or sidewall and short to the next line. *Additive impurities*: sulfur or chlorine from the plating bath segregated to grain boundaries, lowering E_a locally. Fabs catch these with dedicated EM test lines: package-level tests, where a few hundred bonded structures are stressed at 300 °C for weeks and the failure-time distribution is fitted to Black's equation, and faster wafer-level tests at higher current that screen each lot for drift in the cap or the plating chemistry.

## Other Reliability Mechanisms

This section is a shorter survey; each mechanism has its own design rule and its own test, and Module 13 returns to how they are measured.

**Stress-induced voiding (SIV / stress migration)** is the migration of vacancies (missing atoms in the lattice), driven by the tensile stress the copper is left in after cooling from anneal (its thermal expansion is 17 ppm/K versus ~2.6 ppm/K for the silicon wafer that everything is clamped to), toward the stress concentration under a via. It is worst when a small via sits on a wide line (a large reservoir of vacancies) and peaks around 150 to 200 °C, where vacancy mobility is high but stress has not yet relaxed. It is tested by storage bakes at ~150 to 200 °C for hundreds of hours with via-on-wide-line structures read before and after (numbers approximate). The design rule fix is to require multiple vias on wide lines, and the process fix is to control the anneal and cap adhesion.

**Time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB)** in the low-k is the slow degradation of the dielectric between two adjacent lines at different potentials. With a 20 nm spacing and 1 V, the field is 0.5 MV/cm (megavolts per centimetre), in a material that is porous, plasma-damaged at its sidewalls, and in contact with copper, which under the field can drift as Cu⁺ ions into the dielectric and build a conductive path. Lifetime is measured at accelerated fields on comb-serpentine structures and extrapolated; there are several competing empirical formulas for how lifetime shortens with field, and the choice moves the 10-year extrapolation by orders of magnitude, so it is argued over. The target is 10 years at operating voltage with 95 to 99.9 percent survival. TDDB is a major reason the barrier must be continuous, the CMP must not leave copper on the surface, and the post-CMP clean must not leave copper ions in the porous film.

**Thermomechanical failure**: porous low-k delaminates or cracks under packaging stress, the **chip-package interaction (CPI)** problem, in which the mismatch between the chip's and the package's expansion tears at the weakest layer in the stack, particularly under the **C4** solder bumps (the balls that join a flip-chip die to its package, defined in The Top of the Stack). This is why the upper levels return to denser dielectrics and why the pad structures have crack-stop rings and specific mechanical design rules.

> **What can go wrong.** *SIV under a single via on a wide line*: the via reads fine at **wafer sort** (the electrical test of every die while it is still on the wafer, Module 14) and opens after months at operating temperature; caught by the storage bakes above, prevented by the redundant-via rule. *TDDB from CMP copper residue*: a puddle of copper or a scratch full of it between two lines seeds a leakage path that grows under bias; comb-serpentine leakage at parametric test is the screen, and the buff and post-CMP clean are the fix.

## The New Metals

Copper damascene runs into a wall at line widths below about 15 nm: the barrier/liner take a large share of the width, the copper resistivity is several times bulk, and the total line resistance grows faster than the geometry alone would predict. The alternatives are metals with a shorter electron mean free path (so they lose less to surface scattering) and no need for a thick barrier, even if their bulk resistivity is worse. Why the figure of merit is ρ × λ: when the width w is much smaller than the mean free path λ, the size-effect rule of thumb becomes ρ_eff ≈ ρ₀ × (constant × λ/w), so the material term that survives is ρ₀ × λ, and the metal with the smallest product wins at small w even if its ρ₀ is larger. Lower is better at small dimensions:

| Metal | Bulk ρ (µΩ·cm) | Mean free path λ (nm) | ρ × λ (10⁻¹⁶ Ω·m²) | Melting point (°C) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cu | 1.68 | ~39 | 6.7 | 1,085 | Needs TaN barrier + Co liner + cap |
| Al | 2.65 | ~19 | 5.0 | 660 | Subtractive etch; poor EM |
| Co | 6.2 | ~12 | 7.3 | 1,495 | Thin TiN liner; good EM; fill by CVD/ECD |
| Ru | 7.1 (7.8 in Gall's dataset) | ~6.6 | ~4.7 to 5.1 | 2,334 | Near-barrierless; subtractive etch possible |
| Mo | 5.3 | ~11 | 6.0 | 2,623 | Barrierless on oxide; CVD from MoO₂Cl₂ |
| W | 5.3 | ~15.5 | 8.2 | 3,422 | CVD from WF₆ (needs F barrier) or fluorine-free |

(Values after Gall, J. Appl. Phys. 2016; mean free paths are first-principles estimates and other sources quote somewhat different numbers, e.g. ~10 to 11 nm for Ru.)

> **Worked example: copper versus ruthenium in a 12 × 24 nm line.** Copper: after 3.5 nm of barrier plus liner on each sidewall and the bottom, the copper core is ~5 nm wide by ~20 nm tall, ~100 nm² = 10⁻¹⁶ m², and at that width it runs at ~10 µΩ·cm = 10⁻⁷ Ω·m; R per µm = 10⁻⁷ × 10⁻⁶ / 10⁻¹⁶ ≈ ~1,000 Ω/µm. Ruthenium with no barrier uses the full 12 × 24 nm = 288 nm² at a thin-film resistivity of ~12 µΩ·cm: R per µm = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ × 10⁻⁶ / 2.88 × 10⁻¹⁶ ≈ ~400 Ω/µm. A ~2× win from a metal whose bulk resistivity is four times worse, entirely because it keeps the whole trench for itself. (Both figures are estimates; the Ru thin-film resistivity depends on grain size and deposition.)

**Cobalt**: Intel's 10 nm process (2017 to 2019) made M0 and M1 (36 nm pitch) entirely of cobalt with a thin TiN liner, and used cobalt contact plugs. Cobalt wins on EM (activation energy well above copper) and its liner is thinner, but its bulk resistivity is 3.7× copper's, so at 36 nm pitch the line resistance was in fact somewhat higher than copper's and Intel had reliability and yield trouble with the fill. Intel 4 retreated to cobalt-clad copper (eCu) for M0 to M4. As of 2025, cobalt survives broadly as the liner and cap around copper and, at several foundries, as the source/drain contact plug.

**Ruthenium**: Ru has the lowest ρ × λ of the practical candidates, is thermally and chemically robust, and copper-style barriers are unnecessary because it does not diffuse into oxide; a sub-nanometre TiN or TaN adhesion layer or none at all suffices. Its bulk resistivity of 7.1 µΩ·cm means it only beats copper when the line is narrower than roughly 12 to 15 nm including barrier, which is exactly where M0/M1 sit at N2/A16 class pitches (TSMC's **Axx** names, A16 and A14, are its ångström-era nodes, following N2). Ru can be deposited by CVD/ALD or plated, and, uniquely, it can be dry-etched: Ru + O₂ plasma forms volatile RuO₄, so a **subtractive** ruthenium process (deposit blanket Ru, pattern with EUV, etch the lines, then fill the gaps with dielectric or leave an airgap) is feasible. **imec** (the Belgian semiconductor research institute, which styles its name in lowercase; its consortium with the major foundries and toolmakers prototypes interconnect schemes several nodes ahead of production, and it sells nothing) has demonstrated subtractive Ru at 18 nm pitch and below, and TSMC and Samsung have discussed Ru for the bottom levels of N2-class and beyond nodes. As of 2025, Ru is confirmed in production only as a liner (Intel 10 nm; TSMC's N3 paper cites an "innovative", reportedly Ru, liner); no foundry has publicly confirmed Ru lines or vias in volume production, and the first such use is expected at A16/A14-class nodes, with details proprietary. Deposition tools: Applied offers Ru PVD and CVD chambers on the Endura platform, and ASM offers ALD Ru.

**Molybdenum**: Mo has a resistivity close to tungsten's with a shorter mean free path and, critically, does not need the fluorine barrier that WF₆-based tungsten requires, because the newer precursors (MoO₂Cl₂, MoCl₅) are fluorine-free; the TiN barrier can go from ~3 to 4 nm to under 1 nm or zero. Mo entered production first in the **wordlines** (the row-select lines of a memory array) of **3D NAND** flash (the vertically stacked storage memory inside solid-state drives, Module 15), Micron first, per Lam, replacing tungsten across 200+ layers, where the barrier occupied a large share of the wordline thickness; as of 2025 it has early adoption in advanced logic contacts and vias, with development under way for **DRAM** (dynamic random-access memory, the main working memory of Module 15). Lam (ALTUS Halo, announced February 2025) and Applied both sell Mo CVD/ALD tools.

**Tungsten** is a side note in this section, because it is leaving the tight levels rather than entering them, but it has not gone: it remains the source/drain contact plug at many nodes and in some flows the V0 fill. What changed is the chemistry. The classic WF₆ process needs its ~3 to 4 nm TiN fluorine barrier (the MOL section explains why), which in a ~20 nm contact is a large share of the hole. **Fluorine-free tungsten (FFW)**, grown by CVD or ALD from tungsten pentachloride (WCl₅) with H₂ at roughly ~400 to 500 °C, hotter than the fluoride route, releases chlorine rather than fluorine, which does not attack the silicide, so the liner can shrink to ~1 nm or be dropped, and the plug gains cross section and drops resistance. Lam's ALTUS line (the tool named for the WF₆ process above) and Applied both offer it. Its costs are a slower, hotter deposition and corrosive, moisture-sensitive chloride precursors that need the same heated, purged delivery lines as molybdenum's.

> **What can go wrong.** *Cobalt*: fill voids in narrow trenches and, at Intel 10 nm's 36 nm pitch, line resistance that came out higher than copper's, both of which pushed Intel back to eCu. *Ruthenium*: the subtractive etch must hold line-edge roughness at sub-20 nm pitch, and the etch product RuO₄ is toxic and a strong oxidizer, so exhaust and abatement are designed around it. *Molybdenum*: the metal oxidizes readily in air, so queue times and caps matter, and its chloride precursors are corrosive and moisture-sensitive, which the delivery lines must be built for. All show up first as resistance and leakage at parametric test and as voids in cross-section TEM.

### What "subtractive metal" means and why it may return

Damascene was adopted because copper cannot be etched, and it has three structural costs: a barrier that cannot scale, a fill that becomes voidy in high-aspect-ratio (tall and narrow) trenches, and CMP dishing/erosion that couples the metal thickness to pattern density. If a conductor can be dry-etched (Ru, Mo, W, Al all can), the flow inverts back to the Al-era style: deposit a blanket metal of exactly the intended thickness (so no CMP dishing), pattern it with EUV, etch it (so the aspect ratio is set by the etch, not by the fill), then deposit dielectric between the lines. Advantages: no barrier on the sidewalls, larger grains (the film is annealed as a blanket before patterning, so grains are not confined by the trench), lower line resistance at the same pitch, freedom to make lines tall, and the option of leaving an **airgap** between lines simply by depositing a **non-conformal** dielectric (one that deposits faster at the mouth of a gap than inside it, so it bridges over) that pinches off. Disadvantages: metal etch at 20 nm pitch with acceptable line-edge roughness, the via must then be filled separately (usually still a damascene via), and the tooling ecosystem (Lam, TEL and Applied are all developing Ru/Mo etch chambers) is younger. As of 2025, imec's roadmap and several foundries' N2/A16-era disclosures point to subtractive Ru or Mo for M0/M1, with damascene copper retained above.

### Airgaps

The lowest k is vacuum. Intel's 14 nm process (2014) introduced **airgaps** at two intermediate layers (80 nm and 160 nm pitch), reducing the capacitance of those layers by about 17 percent. The method: after the copper level is finished, a lithography step opens the areas where airgaps are allowed, the low-k between selected lines is etched away, and a non-conformal PECVD oxide is deposited that seals over the top of the gap before it can fill it. The gap must be kept away from vias (a via landing in an airgap would be an open or a short), which is why an extra mask is needed, and mechanical strength suffers, which is why it is used only where the RC benefit justifies the cost. Airgaps are a natural companion of subtractive metal, and, as of 2025, several roadmap proposals for M1 through M3 in the ångström nodes include them.

### Hybrid metallization

The problem hybrid metallization solves is the one the via worked example ended on: a quarter of a 1× via's resistance is a 2 nm TaN floor that no amount of geometry can shrink, and another large share is a copper column squeezed to 13 nm by its own sidewall barrier. Replacing copper wholesale with ruthenium fixes the via but costs the line, since above ~15 nm width copper still wins. **Hybrid metallization** keeps both: a barrierless metal via (Ru or Co) is prefilled into the via hole before the copper line is plated over it, so that the via and the line in the same dual damascene level are different conductors. Prefilling removes the barrier floor from the current path and lets the via metal use the whole hole, and the copper line above then only needs a barrier on its own sidewalls and floor, which now sits on metal rather than on the via.

The enabling process is **bottom-up selective deposition**: a CVD or ALD chemistry (Ru from a ruthenium carbonyl or organometallic precursor, Co from the same cobalt carbonyl family as the cap) tuned so that the metal nucleates on the exposed copper at the via bottom but not on the dielectric sidewalls, and grows upward until it reaches the trench floor. It is the same selective chemistry that underlies the cobalt cap, run for longer, on the same Applied Endura and Volta chambers that deposit the cap and liner (ASM's ALD Ru is the alternative), followed by the ordinary TaN/Co/Cu sequence for the line. As of 2025 the scheme has been demonstrated by imec at N2/A16-class via dimensions and appears in roadmap presentations from foundries and toolmakers; which foundry inserts it at which node is proprietary.

> **Worked example: what the prefill buys.** Reuse the via from the worked example above: a 20 × 20 nm footprint, ~50 nm tall. Copper damascene gave ~10 Ω of TaN floor plus ~25 to 30 Ω of 13 nm copper column plus a few ohms of interface, ~40 Ω. A ruthenium prefill with no barrier uses the full 20 × 20 nm = 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ m² at the ~12 µΩ·cm thin-film resistivity from the copper-versus-ruthenium example: R = ρL/A = 1.2 × 10⁻⁷ × 5 × 10⁻⁸ / 4 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 15 Ω for the column, plus a few ohms at the Ru/Cu interfaces top and bottom, ~15 to 20 Ω in all. The via resistance roughly halves, from a metal whose bulk resistivity is four times worse than copper's, because it keeps the whole hole for itself and needs no floor. Multiply by the billions of vias in series with the signals on a die and the gain is a few percent of path delay at the tight levels (estimate; the exact share depends on how many vias a path crosses).

> **What can go wrong.** *Selectivity loss*: after a certain thickness, or on a sidewall that the clean left slightly damaged, the metal begins to nucleate on the dielectric too, and the stray nuclei bridge to the neighbouring line; seen as leakage on comb-serpentine structures, and the fix is a shorter deposition, a better clean or a periodic selective etch-back. *Prefill height*: stop too low and the copper line's barrier lines the top of the via hole and gives back part of the resistance; grow too high and a mushroom of Ru stands above the trench floor, where the barrier must cover it and CMP must not catch it; controlled by timed deposition and checked by cross-section TEM. *Oxidized interface*: ruthenium that sits in air before the line's barrier and seed go on grows a thin oxide at the Ru/Cu joint, and the via reads tens of ohms high; the answer is queue-time limits and an in-situ pre-clean, and the via chain catches the drift.

## The Top of the Stack

The problem at the top is one of scale. Hundreds of amps and thousands of signals must leave a die whose finest wires are 12 nm and land on a package whose connection pitch is 100 µm or more: a 10,000× change of scale across a few layers. Above the thick global copper levels the process therefore changes character; the features are microns, the lithography is i-line, and the goal is to get the signals and power off the die cheaply and robustly.

Also living here are the **metal-insulator-metal (MIM) decoupling capacitors**. When millions of transistors switch in the same nanosecond they draw a current spike, and the supply cannot respond that fast through the package; a capacitor next to the switching transistors is a local charge reservoir that keeps the supply voltage from sagging, which is what "decoupling" means. The MIM is a thin **high-k** dielectric (an insulator that stores more charge per area than SiO₂; here ZrO₂ or HfO₂ based, a few nm thick; Module 11) sandwiched between TiN plates over large areas between the global layers, giving reported densities on the order of tens of fF/µm² (TSMC's "super high density MIM", SHDMIM, is stated to be 4× its earlier HDMIM, and the N2-era SHPMIM another 2× above that; these are marketing tiers for high density, super high density and super high performance, and absolute values are proprietary).

The MIM is built as a short subtractive interlude between two damascene copper levels, and it is the one place in the BEOL where the FEOL's gate-stack tools reappear. After a global copper level is polished and capped, a bottom plate of TiN (a few tens of nanometres, by PVD on an Endura-class tool or by ALD) is deposited over the whole wafer, then the high-k by ALD (ZrO₂, HfO₂ or a ZrO₂/Al₂O₃/ZrO₂ laminate, one atomic layer per cycle on ALD platforms from ASM, Applied or TEL, the same tools and chemistries as the high-k gate dielectric of Module 11), then the top TiN plate. Lithography (i-line or KrF, since the plates are tens of microns across) and a chlorine-based metal etch define the top plate, a second pass defines the bottom plate, the stack is buried in the next level's dielectric, and vias from the copper level above land on each plate. Because the plates are huge by BEOL standards, the film must be free of pinholes over square millimetres, and the 1 V across a few nanometres of dielectric is a field of ~2 MV/cm, so the capacitor's own leakage and time-dependent breakdown are qualified like the low-k's, and the layout is split into segments so that one defective patch does not short the whole supply.

> **Worked example: what "tens of fF/µm²" means.** Use the parallel-plate formula from the RC section. Take k ≈ 25 for ZrO₂ (a representative value; the k of these films depends on their crystal phase) and a 5 nm thickness: C/A = ε₀ k / d = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 25 / (5 × 10⁻⁹) = 0.044 F/m² = 44 fF/µm², in the quoted range. A 100 × 100 µm patch of it, 10⁴ µm², holds ~0.44 nF, more than two thousand times the 0.2 pF of a 1 mm signal wire, sitting a few microns above the transistors that need it. Halving the thickness or doubling k doubles the density, which is what the SHDMIM and SHPMIM tiers are doing.

**Pad metal (AP)**: the last copper level is capped and covered with a **passivation** dielectric, the sealing glass that keeps moisture and contamination out of the stack for the life of the chip (typically ~0.5 to 1 µm of PECVD SiO₂ and SiN). Openings are etched, and the **aluminum pad** layer is deposited: ~1 to 3 µm of PVD Al-0.5%Cu with Ti/TiN under and over (sputtered on an Endura-class PVD tool), patterned by the old subtractive Cl₂/BCl₃ etch in a metal etch chamber. Aluminum is retained here because it forms a thin, self-limiting oxide and so stays a stable surface for **probe needles** (the fine metal tips that touch the pads at wafer sort, Module 14) and **wire bonds** (thin gold or copper wires welded to the pad to connect it to the package), and because it is cheap. A final passivation (SiN, then usually a **polyimide** or **PBO** polymer, 5 to 10 µm, tough plastics spun on as a stress buffer between the brittle stack and the solder) is patterned to open the pad.

**Redistribution layer (RDL)**: for **flip-chip** parts (the die is turned face-down onto the package and joined through bumps rather than wires) the pads are re-routed into an **area array** (bumps spread over the whole die face rather than along its edges) with one or two RDL levels: thick copper (2 to 5 µm) patterned by a **semi-additive process** (sputter Ti/Cu seed, thick resist, electroplate copper only in the openings, strip resist, etch the seed), which is a third style of patterning, neither damascene (no trench is etched and nothing is polished) nor subtractive (no metal is etched beyond the thin seed): the resist itself is the mould, and that works well at these coarse dimensions where a thick resist can be patterned with cheap i-line lithography. The plating is done on packaging-class tools such as Lam's SABRE 3D and Applied's equivalents.

**Under-bump metallization (UBM)** and bumps: on each pad opening a UBM stack (e.g. sputtered Ti or titanium-tungsten (TiW) as barrier/adhesion, Cu as the wettable layer, sometimes Ni as a solder diffusion barrier) is deposited, and the bump is electroplated through a thick resist: a **C4** solder bump (**controlled collapse chip connection**, IBM's 1960s name for a solder ball that melts on its pad and self-centres by surface tension, "collapsing" to a height set by the pad size; tin-silver, SnAg, ~80 to 100 µm diameter on a 130 to 150 µm pitch) for conventional flip-chip, or a **copper pillar** (~20 to 50 µm diameter, with a thin SnAg cap) for fine pitch, or ~10 to 25 µm microbumps for chip-on-wafer stacking (Module 17). **Reflow**, heating the wafer until the solder melts and surface tension pulls it into a ball, forms the bump. This is the point where the wafer, still whole, leaves the fab's BEOL and, after sort test, heads to packaging (Modules 16 and 17).

> **What can go wrong.** *Pad corrosion*: moisture reaching an aluminum pad through a passivation crack, or probe-mark damage from thousands of needle touches at sort (Module 14), leaves a pad that will not bond. *Bump voids and non-wets*: a void inside the plated solder or a UBM surface that the solder will not wet, both found by X-ray and shear tests in packaging. *Low-k cracking under the C4* (the CPI problem): the package pulls on the bump as it cools and the crack runs into the porous dielectric; the defence is the crack-stop ring around the die edge, dense dielectrics at the top levels, and the polyimide buffer.

## Backside Power Delivery

In a conventional stack, the power and ground grid shares the same levels as the signals, descending from the thick top layers through every via level to the M0 power rails that run alongside each standard cell row. At N3-class pitches those M0 power rails are a large fraction of the **cell height** (the fixed height of a standard-cell row, set by how many wiring tracks fit across it), the ~15 via levels between the C4 bump and the transistor cost **IR drop** (the voltage lost to resistance on the way, V = I × R), and the power mesh steals **routing tracks** (the fixed-pitch lanes on each level along which wires may run) on every level.

> **Worked example: IR drop.** A 100 W chip at 0.75 V draws 100 / 0.75 ≈ 133 A. Through ~0.5 mΩ of total grid resistance from bump to transistor that is 133 × 0.5 × 10⁻³ ≈ 70 mV, nearly 10 percent of the supply, which the transistors then do not see; they run slower, or the designer raises the supply and pays for it in power (both numbers are illustrative orders of magnitude).

**Backside power delivery (BSPDN)** moves the entire power network to the back of the wafer. The front side keeps only signal wiring; after the front-side BEOL is complete the wafer is flipped, bonded to a carrier, and thinned from 775 µm (the standard thickness of a 300 mm wafer as it arrives from the wafer maker; the transistors occupy only the top fraction of a micron of it, the rest is a handle) down to a few hundred nanometres of silicon above the transistors, and a new, thick, coarse metal stack (~3 to 5 levels at pitches from a few hundred nanometres to a few microns, comparable to the front side's semi-global and global levels) is built on the back. The thinning and rebuild, in order:

1. **Carrier bonding.** The finished front side is bonded face-down to a carrier wafer (wafer bonders from EV Group or SUSS MicroTec), so that the device wafer can be thinned without breaking.
2. **Grinding.** A grinder (Disco is the dominant supplier) removes most of the 775 µm of silicon from the back in minutes.
3. **CMP.** Polishing takes the surface toward the target and removes the grinding damage.
4. **Selective etch to the stop layer.** A wet or plasma etch that removes silicon much faster than silicon-germanium (SiGe) stops on a SiGe layer buried in the starting wafer just below the transistors, leaving a few hundred nanometres of silicon with uniformity that grinding alone could never deliver.
5. **Backside contact lithography.** The backside connections are patterned with backside-to-frontside overlay, aligning through the wafer to structures built hundreds of process steps earlier on the other face. How a scanner sees an alignment mark on the far side: silicon is opaque to visible light but transparent to infrared beyond ~1.1 µm wavelength (a photon with less energy than silicon's 1.12 eV band gap cannot be absorbed by it), so the scanner's alignment sensor uses infrared wavelengths to image the front-side marks through the remaining silicon, and once that silicon is down to a few hundred nanometres the marks are nearly visible outright. The harder part is that the bonded, thinned wafer is no longer the shape it was when the front side was printed: bonding and grinding stretch and twist it by tens of nanometres across the field, so the scanner measures a dense grid of marks and fits a distortion map (a higher-order overlay correction) before exposing. Demonstration flows report through-wafer overlay of order ~10 nm (imec, Intel; exact values vary by flow and are improving), a few times looser than a front-side via layer's few nanometres, which is why the backside vias are given larger landing pads than a front-side via would get.
6. **Backside metal.** A damascene stack at coarse pitch, built with the same steps as the front side but with far more margin.

Three connection schemes exist. (The production-status notes in this list carry an "as of 2026" stamp because they were updated after the rest of the module was written; everywhere else the "as of 2025" stamps stand.)

- **Buried power rail (BPR)**: a rail of Ru or W is placed in the isolation trench below the fins or nanosheets (the transistor channels of Module 11) before the transistors are built, and nano-TSVs from the backside contact it (imec's original scheme; Intel evaluated it). A **through-silicon via (TSV)** is a metal-filled hole through the silicon itself; a nano-TSV is one at sub-100 nm dimensions.
- **PowerVia** (Intel, in production on Intel 18A as of 2026, after a test-chip demonstration on Intel 4 in 2023): nano-TSVs through the thinned silicon connect the backside metal to the front-side contacts directly, without a buried rail. Intel reported that PowerVia allowed M0 pitch to relax from 30 to 36 nm (fewer tracks needed for power), improved **cell utilization** (the fraction of die area actually filled with standard cells rather than left empty for routing) by 5 to 10 percent, and cut IR drop substantially.
- **Direct backside contact / Super Power Rail (TSMC A16, ramping 2026 to 2027 as of 2026)**: the backside metal contacts the source/drain epitaxy directly from below, with no TSV through the cell, giving the smallest area penalty.

The BEOL implications are significant. Demonstrated so far (Intel 4 test chip, Intel 18A): the M0 relaxation, the utilization gain and the IR-drop reduction above, with a 14-level front-side stack plus 4 backside levels. Expected rather than yet demonstrated in a published product: the front-side stack losing its thick power layers and dropping from 15 to 18 levels to 12 to 14, all signal; the C4 bumps and RDL and the MIM capacitors moving to the backside. What is certain is that the thermal path changes (the transistors are now sandwiched between two metal stacks, with the heat sink on the backside metal) and that the wafer thinning to sub-micron silicon is a new precision process with no margin for error. The tight-pitch signal layers, however, are built exactly as described above.

> **What can go wrong.** *Silicon thickness non-uniformity*: at a sub-micron target, a few hundred nanometres of variation across 300 mm is the whole budget; it is why the process ends on an etch stop rather than a grinder, and it is measured by infrared interferometry across the wafer. *Nano-TSV overlay*: the backside via must hit a front-side contact a few tens of nanometres wide through the wafer, and overlay marks on the far face are seen through silicon. *Heat trapped between two metal stacks*: the transistors' hot spots no longer sit next to the bulk silicon that used to spread heat, and the thermal design must be redone. *Wafer bow after thinning*: a wafer reduced to a film on a carrier warps with the stresses of its own metal stacks, and handling and lithography must cope.

## Interconnect Metrology and Test Structures

BEOL cannot be inspected transistor by transistor, so it is monitored through in-line film metrology and electrical test structures in the scribe lines. Module 13 gives each technique its full treatment; here is what each one does in a few words.

- **Film thickness and CMP**: copper thickness by in-situ eddy current on the polisher and by four-point probe (the sheet-resistance measurement introduced at the anneal step) or **X-ray fluorescence (XRF)** (X-rays make each element glow at its own wavelength; the intensity gives mass per area) on monitor wafers; dielectric and cap thickness by spectroscopic ellipsometry (polarized light reflected from the film); dishing and erosion by AFM and high-resolution profilometry (a stylus dragged across the surface) on test pads; barrier/liner thickness by **X-ray reflectometry (XRR)** (interference of grazing X-rays gives thickness and density) or XRF on blankets and by TEM cross-sections on product.
- **Trench and via CD/profile**: CD-SEM and **optical scatterometry (OCD)** (the diffraction of light from a grating gives its profile) after etch; TEM lamellae cut by **focused ion beam (FIB)** (an ion beam mills out a slice for the microscope) for depth, sidewall angle, barrier continuity, and void checks. Void detection in filled vias uses **e-beam voltage-contrast** inspection (a floating via charges differently from a connected one and images bright or dark).
- **Electrical**: every wafer carries scribe-line structures measured at parametric test: **Kelvin** line resistance (R per unit length by a 4-terminal measurement, two contacts to force current and two to sense voltage, on a **serpentine**, a long line folded back and forth, of known length), **via chains** (10³ to 10⁶ vias in series; the resistance per via and the count of opens quantify via yield), **comb-serpentine** structures (a serpentine interleaved with a comb of neighbouring lines) for line-to-line shorts and leakage (which also feed TDDB), and EM and SIV test lines that are stressed at wafer level or in package. Line resistance distributions across the wafer are the most direct readout of CMP uniformity and barrier thickness.

> **Worked example: what a via chain tells you.** A chain of 10⁴ vias at ~30 Ω each reads ~300 kΩ. A single open anywhere in the chain reads infinite, so one number on one structure detects one bad via in ten thousand. A 10 percent shift in the chain resistance across the wafer, with no opens, flags a barrier that got thicker on the via floor or a pre-clean that is leaving oxide, days before any die reaches sort. That is why a fab watches the via-chain trend chart more closely than almost any other BEOL number (Module 13).

## Design Rules the BEOL Imposes

The BEOL hands the designer a rulebook, enforced by the **EDA** tools (electronic design automation: the software that places the cells and routes the wires), and each rule exists because of a step above:

- **Minimum pitch and width** per layer, with the tight layers restricted to **unidirectional** lines (all wires on a layer run one way; the layer above runs the other way) at a fixed pitch (a **gridded** style, in which every wire sits on one of a fixed set of tracks) and vias allowed only at grid intersections; because SADP and EUV double patterning cannot make corners or arbitrary spacings.
- **Via enclosure**: the metal must extend beyond the via by a specified distance so misalignment does not reduce the contact area; at self-aligned levels this is relaxed in one direction; because overlay has a budget of a few nanometres. Representative values (foundry rulebooks are proprietary; these are the right order): at a 1× level the via is nearly as wide as the line, so the enclosure across the line is ~0 to a few nm, which is exactly why the self-aligned via exists, while along the line it is ~10 to 20 nm; at the 4× levels enclosures of ~20 to 40 nm are affordable.
- **Minimum area and end-of-line extension** rules; because tiny shapes do not print reliably and because a short stub past a via acts as a copper reservoir that delays EM voiding. Representative values: the shortest wire allowed on a 1× level is ~2 to 3 pitches long (~50 to 100 nm at a 26 to 40 nm pitch), and a line must run on ~1 via width, ~20 to 40 nm, past the last via on it.
- **Metal density**: each layer must have a minimum (typically ~20 to 30 percent) and maximum (~70 to 80 percent) metal density in every window of, say, 50 × 50 µm, so that CMP dishing and erosion, which depend on local density, stay within spec. Where the design is sparse, the EDA tools insert **dummy fill** (floating metal shapes that carry no signal) automatically; where it is dense, slots are cut into wide lines. Dummy fill adds capacitance and is a standing negotiation between process and design.
- **Antenna rules**: a long metal line connected to a gate but not yet to a source/drain (a **diffusion** in layout jargon, the doped region that would give the charge a path into the substrate) collects plasma charge during etch, because the partly built stack leaves the line and the gate it touches electrically floating, and the accumulated voltage can blow the gate oxide; the rule limits the ratio of metal area to gate area at each level, and the fix is an **antenna diode** (a small diode to the substrate that bleeds the charge away) or a jumper to a higher level.
- **Current density limits** per width and per via for EM, and **redundant via** requirements on wide lines for SIV.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Two of these rules are visible in every layout you will ever open. Dummy fill covers most of the empty space on every metal layer and adds a few percent to the capacitance of the signals it sits next to, which the timing tools must model. Antenna diodes are sprinkled through every block, taking area and adding leakage, purely because of a plasma etch step in this module. The BEOL is not a passive substrate for the design; its process physics is written into the chip.

## Summary

- Transistors got faster with scaling but wires got slower: resistance per length ∝ 1/(w × h) and rises further once the width drops below copper's ~39 nm mean free path, while capacitance per length stays at ~0.2 fF/µm; delay ∝ RC ∝ L².
- The answer is a hierarchy of ~15 to 18 levels (as of 2025): tight EUV-patterned layers (~23 to 30 nm pitch) at the bottom for short wires, micron-pitch i-line layers at the top for power, and repeaters every 100 to 300 µm on long signals.
- Copper cannot be plasma-etched (no volatile chloride below ~200 °C) and poisons silicon, so it is inlaid by dual damascene: etch via and trench into low-k, line with ~2 nm TaN and ~1.5 nm Co, sputter a seed, electroplate, anneal, CMP, cap; ~30 to 40 operations per level.
- Low-k SiOC:H sits at k ~2.9 to 3.0 dense and ~2.5 porous, and has stayed there for a decade because porosity destroys strength; caps and damage push the effective k 0.3 to 0.5 higher.
- Superfill works because a large suppressor blocks the field while a small accelerator concentrates on the shrinking trench bottom; Faraday's law gives ~0.4 g of copper per wafer per level in one to two minutes.
- CMP planarizes because the slurry chemistry attacks copper everywhere but BTA passivates recesses and the pad strips passivation only from high points; dishing and erosion of 10 to 30 nm drive metal-density rules and dummy fill.
- The 3 to 4 nm barrier-plus-liner per sidewall does not scale: at 20 nm width it takes ~40 percent of the cross section, and the barrier floor of a via alone is ~10 Ω.
- Electromigration moves copper along the Cu/cap interface (E_a ~0.8 to 0.9 eV); Black's equation extrapolates hot, high-current tests to 10 years (~8,000× acceleration from 110 to 300 °C); lines shorter than the Blech length (~20 to 40 µm at 1 MA/cm²) are immortal; cobalt cladding raises E_a to ~1.0 to 1.2 eV.
- Stress-induced voiding, low-k TDDB and chip-package cracking are the other reliability limits, each with its own design rule.
- Below ~15 nm width the figure of merit becomes ρ × λ plus barrier share: ruthenium and molybdenum are replacing copper at M0/M1 and in contacts (expected at A16/A14-class, as of 2025), cobalt has retreated to liner, cap and plug, and subtractive etch with airgaps is returning.
- The top of the stack returns to coarse, cheap processes: subtractive aluminum pads, semi-additive copper RDL, UBM and C4 or pillar bumps, plus MIM decoupling capacitors.
- Backside power delivery (Intel PowerVia on 18A, TSMC Super Power Rail on A16) moves the power grid to the wafer back, relaxing M0 pitch, cutting IR drop, and demanding sub-micron wafer thinning.
- The BEOL is monitored through scribe-line Kelvin lines, via chains and comb-serpentines plus eddy-current, XRR and TEM metrology; its tools come from an oligopoly (Applied, Lam, TEL, Ebara) and its chemistry from a handful of suppliers.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Copper interconnect is etched like everything else in the fab."** → Copper has no volatile etch products at resist-compatible temperatures; it is inlaid by damascene (etch the dielectric, plate the metal, polish it back) and has been since 1997. The only front-side metal that is still subtractively etched on a leading-edge logic wafer is the aluminum pad layer; ruthenium and molybdenum may bring subtractive metal back at the bottom of the stack.
- **"Smaller wires mean faster chips."** → Transistors speed up when scaled; wires slow down. Resistance per unit length rises as 1/(w × h) and then faster still once the width is below copper's 39 nm mean free path, while capacitance per unit length stays at ~0.2 fF/µm. Interconnect, not the transistor, sets the delay of most paths on a modern die, and a substantial fraction of the transistors exist only to repeat signals along wires.
- **"Low-k is the main lever for reducing RC."** → Bulk k has been stuck at ~2.5 to 3.0 for over a decade because more porous films are mechanically and chemically unworkable. Recent RC gains have come from thinner caps, selective metal caps, better liners, airgaps and, most of all, from fighting resistance with new conductors and backside power.
- **"Electromigration is a bulk copper property."** → In damascene copper the atoms move along the copper/cap interface, with an activation energy of ~0.85 eV rather than the ~2 eV of bulk diffusion. Cladding the copper in cobalt raises the barrier; short lines below the Blech length do not fail at all.
- **"Cobalt replaced copper at 10 nm."** → Only at Intel, only for M0/M1, and Intel went back to cobalt-clad copper at Intel 4 because cobalt's 3.7× higher bulk resistivity outweighed its thinner barrier at 36 nm pitch. Cobalt is now the liner, cap and contact plug; ruthenium and molybdenum are the metals actually replacing copper at the tightest levels.
- **"CMP is just sanding the wafer flat."** → Copper CMP is a chemical process gated by mechanical action: the slurry oxidizes and complexes copper everywhere, an inhibitor (BTA) passivates recessed areas, and the pad and abrasive only strip the passivation on the high points. That selectivity is what planarizes rather than simply thins.
- **"A via is just a hole full of copper."** → A large share of a 1× via's resistance is the 2 nm TaN floor (~10 Ω of a ~40 Ω via) and the interface beneath it, not the copper; that floor does not shrink with geometry, which is why barrierless Ru vias and hybrid metallization exist.
- **"Backside power is just moving some wires to the back."** → It changes the standard cell (M0 pitch relaxes, cell utilization rises), the IR drop, the thermal path (transistors sandwiched between two metal stacks) and the wafer itself, which must be thinned to sub-micron silicon on an etch stop and patterned through its back with overlay to the front.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module takes the finished transistor from Module 11 (replacement metal gate complete, source/drain epitaxy exposed, wafer still 775 µm thick) and wires it: MOL contacts, M0, then ~15 levels of dual damascene copper with cobalt, ruthenium and molybdenum at the bottom, thick copper for power at the top, aluminum pads, and the UBM and bumps that packaging will attach to. Its inputs are the wafer itself plus a long list of consumables (low-k precursors, TaN/Cu/Co targets and precursors, plating chemistry, CMP pads, slurries and conditioners, hard mask materials, EUV and DUV photomasks for each of the ~30 metal and via levels) supplied by the vendors in the Key Players table, and it consumes roughly half of the fab's process steps and Applied, Lam and Ebara's largest installed base. Its output is a completed, bumped wafer that goes first to the inline metrology and inspection tools of Module 13 (where via chains, line resistance and defect inspection determine whether the BEOL yields), then to wafer sort in Module 14 where probe needles land on the aluminum pads, and finally to the dicing, flip-chip and advanced packaging flows of Modules 16 and 17, where the C4 bumps and RDL built here meet the package substrate or interposer.
