# Module 14: Wafer Sort and Test

A finished 300 mm wafer leaving the **back end of line (BEOL)**, the wiring layers built on top of the transistors (Module 12), has somewhere between 60 and 60,000 candidate dies on it, and the fab has no idea which of them work. Module 13 gave you the statistical picture: a defect density **D0**, the number of killer defects per square centimetre, of a few hundredths of a defect per cm², a yield model, an expected fraction of good dies. But a yield model is a forecast, not a sort. Somewhere in the chain, every single die has to be electrically exercised, judged, and either passed forward or thrown away, and it has to happen before the die is bonded, stacked, molded, and soldered into a $40,000 GPU module where it can no longer be separated from its neighbors.

That is what wafer sort does, and it is hard because the thing being measured is a naked piece of silicon with thousands of **bond pads**, squares of exposed aluminum (or small solder bumps) on the die's top surface, 40 to 60 µm across, that are the only electrical doors into the die. They must be contacted through a mechanical assembly with micron-level **planarity** (how flat the array of contact tips is, edge to edge), driven at multi-gigabit data rates, fed hundreds of amps at sub-volt levels, held anywhere from −40 °C to +150 °C, and judged in seconds to minutes on tests that must catch a defect the designers never saw. The equipment costs millions per tool, the consumable probe cards cost as much as a house, and every second of test time is a direct charge against the margin of the chip.

By the end of this module you will be able to explain what a prober and a probe card do and why they cost what they cost; what a tester actually is (a rack of precision instruments, not a fast computer); what scan, built-in self-test, IDDQ and part average testing each look for, and why running the chip's own software is not a test; why some defective dies always escape and how the escape rate in defective parts per million is computed; why known-good-die is the economic hinge of CoWoS packaging; and who does the testing and who makes the equipment.

## Before you start

- **Dies, wafers and scribe lines** (Modules 03, 13). A 300 mm wafer carries from ~60 **reticle-sized** GPU dies (as large as the lithography scanner can print in one exposure, Module 07) to tens of thousands of small ones, separated by ~60 to 80 µm streets called **scribe lines** that are cut away at dicing; the scribe lines also hold the fab's own test structures.
- **CMOS logic** (Module 11). Every logic gate is a stack of n-type and p-type transistors of which one is always off once the gate has settled, so an idle CMOS circuit draws only leakage. This is why excess idle current signals a defect.
- **Pads, bumps and the top of the wiring stack** (Module 12). The BEOL ends in aluminum bond pads or copper-pillar solder bumps under openings in the **passivation**, the glass-like protective top layer. They are the only electrical access to the die, and the fragile, porous **low-k** insulators (low-permittivity dielectrics chosen to cut wire-to-wire capacitance) sit just beneath them.
- **Defect density, yield models and clustering** (Module 13). D0, the Poisson and negative-binomial yield formulas, and the fact that defects cluster on the wafer. A yield model predicts; test measures.
- **Wafer acceptance test (WAT) and process control monitor (PCM) structures** (Module 13, section 7). The scribe-line transistors, via chains and ring oscillators that are measured before any product die is probed.
- **Ohm's law and inductance** (school physics). V = I·R: 500 A through 1 mΩ drops 0.5 V. V = L·di/dt: a fast change of current through an **inductance** L makes a voltage spike proportional to how fast the current changes. Both govern how power reaches a die through a probe card.
- **Thermal expansion** (school physics). A solid lengthens in proportion to its length and to the temperature rise; silicon does so at 2.6 parts per million per kelvin.
- **Clocked digital logic** (programming background). A **flip-flop** is a one-bit memory that copies its input on each clock edge; between flip-flops sit gates that compute the next values. A chip is an enormous state machine of these, which is what scan testing exploits.
- **Mean and standard deviation σ** (school statistics). σ is the typical spread of a distribution; for a normal distribution, a value ±6σ from the mean is an extreme outlier. Needed for part average testing.

Forward pointers: **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)** (Module 15) is a stack of **dynamic random-access memory (DRAM)** dies, the main-memory chip type, on a base die; **CoWoS** (Module 17) places a GPU and its HBM stacks side by side on a silicon interposer; once they are bonded, nothing can be removed.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## Why Test Exists: The Rule of Ten

The economic argument for test is the **rule of ten**: the cost of finding and removing a defective part rises by roughly an order of magnitude at each stage it slips through. A bad die caught at wafer sort costs the sort test time, a few cents to a few dollars, and the die itself. The same die caught at **final test** (the test of the packaged part in its socket, Module 18) has also consumed a package, a substrate, an assembly slot, and (in a multi-die package) the good dies packaged alongside it. Caught at **board test** (the test of the circuit board it was soldered onto), it has consumed a printed circuit board and its rework; caught at **system test** (the test of the assembled server), a server integration slot; caught in the field, a warranty return, a technician visit, and in automotive or data-center contexts, a potential recall or an outage on a cluster billing thousands of dollars an hour. The multipliers are folklore, not physics, but the shape is right, and for advanced packaging the first step is far steeper than 10×, as we will see under known-good-die.

The other reason test exists is that manufacturing defects are random and cannot be designed away. Design verification establishes that the design is correct; it says nothing about whether this particular copy was built correctly. Verification checks the recipe; test tastes every cake. Only measurement does that, and the measurement must be structured so that a defect anywhere among 200 billion transistors produces an observable difference at a **pin**, the external contact of the chip: a bond pad at sort, a solder ball on the package at final test.

> **Intuition: why running software is not a test.** A GPU running a real workload toggles only a small fraction of its transistors in any given second, and a defect in a transistor that happens to sit idle stays invisible. Worse, a defect that does get exercised may change an internal value that never propagates to any pin before it is overwritten. Test therefore has to be a structured search: deliberately drive every internal node to both values, and deliberately route the result of each to a pin where the tester can see it. That is what scan and built-in self-test do, and it is why a test program looks nothing like a program.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Put dollars on the chain for a Blackwell-class module (**Blackwell** is NVIDIA's 2024-generation data-center GPU: two large dies and eight HBM stacks on one interposer, built in full in Module 19). A bad GPU die caught at sort costs a few dollars of tester time (the worked example under multi-site testing gets ~$26 for a whole single-site sort). The same die caught at the chip-on-wafer test, after it has been bonded to a silicon interposer next to eight HBM stacks, scraps an assembly carrying more than $10,000 of good parts, because a bonded stack cannot be taken apart. Caught in the field, it takes down a server in a cluster billing thousands of dollars an hour and triggers a technician visit. The first step is not 10× but hundreds of times, which is why the known-good-die section below is the economic core of this module.

## The Test Flow, in Order

Every advanced chip passes through a sequence of **test insertions**: an insertion is each time a die or a packaged part is placed into a tester, and every insertion costs time and equipment and is a point where parts fall out. This module focuses on the wafer-level insertions; Module 18 covers the package-level ones in depth.

1. **Wafer acceptance test (WAT)**, also called **process control monitor (PCM)** test. What happens: before any product die is touched, the fab probes dedicated test structures in the scribe lines, the ~60 to 80 µm wide **kerf** (the strip the dicing saw consumes) between dies that will be destroyed by dicing. These are not the product; they are individual transistors, resistors, via chains, capacitors, and ring oscillators laid out by the foundry to characterize the process itself. What it sees that nothing earlier could: whether the transistors and wires the fab built are within specification, measured electrically rather than inferred from film thicknesses and line widths. Cost: seconds per site, a few sites per wafer, negligible against sort.
2. **Wafer sort**, also called **circuit probe (CP)** or wafer probe. What happens: every product die is contacted by a probe card and run through a test program while still on the wafer. What it sees: whether each individual die is structurally correct, has working memory, and meets speed and power targets. Its output is a **wafer map** of pass/fail and **bin codes** (a short numeric grade) per die. Cost: from under a dollar per small die to tens of dollars per GPU die.
3. **Dicing and packaging** (Modules 16 and 17). What happens: the wafer is cut, and the good dies are placed onto substrates or interposers and encapsulated. Not a test, but it is where sort's wafer map is consumed and where the die becomes inseparable from its package.
4. **Final test (FT)**. What happens: the packaged part is picked by a **handler** (a robot that loads packaged parts into a **socket**, a spring-contact fixture that grips a package) and tested at one or more temperatures with full-bandwidth access to its input/output pins. What it sees that sort could not: full-rate high-speed links through real package pins, full power, and package-induced defects. Cost: comparable to sort per part, often higher for multi-temperature flows.
5. **Burn-in**. What happens: extended operation at elevated temperature and voltage, hours long. What it sees: **infant-mortality** failures, parts with latent weaknesses (a thin oxide, a marginal via) that would fail in the first days of use; heat and voltage accelerate them so they fail on the shelf instead. Cost: hours of oven time per part, so it is sampled or skipped on consumer parts and standard for automotive and many data-center parts.
6. **System-level test (SLT)**. What happens: the part runs real firmware and workloads in something resembling its end-use environment. What it sees: interactions between blocks, firmware and power management that **structural tests** (tests derived from the circuit's wiring rather than from its function, of which scan is the main example) cannot model. Cost: minutes per part on cheaper equipment.

| Insertion | Where it happens | What it can see | What it cannot see |
|---|---|---|---|
| WAT / PCM | Fab, on scribe-line structures | Whether the process (transistors, vias, metal) is within spec | Anything about an individual product die |
| Wafer sort | Fab or test house, on the wafer | Structural (scan) faults, memory faults, speed and power grade, gross I/O faults | Full-rate high-speed I/O, full-load power, package-induced defects |
| Final test | Test house or **OSAT** (an outsourced semiconductor assembly and test company; see Who Does the Testing), in a socket | Full I/O rates, full power, multiple temperatures, package defects | Latent weaknesses that need hours to show |
| Burn-in | Burn-in ovens | Latent, infant-mortality defects accelerated by heat and voltage | Firmware-level interactions |
| SLT | Test house or system builder | Firmware, power management and block-to-block interactions | Anything the workload did not happen to exercise; its coverage cannot be quantified |

For a **CoWoS** product like an NVIDIA GPU (TSMC's chip-on-wafer-on-substrate packaging, which places the GPU and its HBM stacks side by side on a **silicon interposer**, a passive silicon plate carrying only wiring, Module 17) there are extra insertions in the middle: a test after the **chip-on-wafer (CoW)** step, in which the dies are bonded onto the interposer wafer, before the interposer is attached to the substrate; and a test of each **HBM stack**, 8 to 16 DRAM dies on a base die, before placement. Every insertion is a fallout point and a cost line, and the purpose of a good wafer sort is to make every downstream insertion see as few bad parts as possible.

### WAT: Testing the Process, Not the Product

Before a single product die is probed, the fab needs to know whether the transistors and wires it built on this wafer are the ones the design was simulated against. Sort time is expensive and sort results are meaningless if the process itself has drifted, so WAT is run first, on structures the foundry designed for exactly this purpose. Module 13, section 7, describes the structures in more detail; here the point is what WAT hands to sort.

What is measured, per site, is on the order of several hundred to a couple of thousand parameters. For n- and p-channel transistors (the two complementary flavours of CMOS) of several widths and lengths: the **threshold voltage V_t**, the gate voltage at which the transistor turns on; the **saturation current I_dsat**, the current it carries when fully on; the **off-state leakage I_off**, the current that leaks through when it is supposed to be off; and the **subthreshold slope**, the number of millivolts of gate voltage it takes to cut the off-state current by a factor of ten (roughly 60 to 70 mV per decade at room temperature for a good transistor; a bigger number means a leakier device that is slower to turn off). Then **gate leakage**, current tunnelling through the gate insulator; contact and via resistance from **via chains**, thousands of layer-to-layer contacts wired in series so that one open shows up as an open chain; metal **sheet resistance** (the resistance of a square of the metal film) and line-to-line leakage on **serpentine-comb structures**, a long snaking wire that catches opens interleaved with a comb that catches shorts; capacitance; and the frequency of **ring oscillators**, loops of an odd number of inverters that oscillate at a rate set by gate delay, which integrates all of it into a single number that tracks the "speed" of the wafer.

The machine is a **parametric tester**, an instrument that measures analog quantities (volts, amps, ohms, farads) rather than pass/fail logic, docked to a wafer prober and fitted with a probe card that contacts a block of a few dozen scribe-line pads at a time. A **site** is one place on the wafer where the card touches down. A typical recipe measures 5 to 13 sites per wafer (center, mid-radius, edge in several directions) on every wafer, or on every wafer in a sample of **lots** (a lot is a batch of wafers, typically 25, that travel through the fab together) depending on maturity. WAT is first run at **first metal**, the point in the flow at which the lowest wiring layer (metal 1) has just been built and the transistors can be contacted electrically for the first time; that gives the fab its feedback while the wafer still has weeks of BEOL processing ahead of it. It is run again at end of line, and often at intermediate layers too.

> **What can go wrong.** *Out of limits*: every WAT parameter has a specification window, and a wafer outside it is scrapped regardless of how its product dies would have sorted, because out-of-spec transistors cannot be trusted for reliability. *A via-chain shift*: a step change in via resistance on one layer points at a specific etch or barrier tool, which is why WAT data is joined to the fab's tool history. *First-metal versus end-of-line disagreement*: transistors that were fine at first metal but shifted by end of line implicate the BEOL thermal budget or a plasma-charging step, not the front end.

WAT is the foundry's ship/no-ship gate (foundry contracts are written against WAT limits), the feedback loop to the fab, and the calibration baseline for sort: if a wafer's ring oscillators run 4 percent slow, its sort speed bins will shift accordingly, and the test engineer expects it. The parametric testers themselves come from **Keysight** (the 4080-series and the per-pin P9001A) and **Keithley** (the S500/S530-series), docked to a TEL or Accretech prober.

## Wafer Sort: The Prober

The prober's problem is mechanical: hold a wafer flat, bring it to a chosen temperature anywhere across a 200 K range, align it to a probe card, and press it against that card with micron accuracy, thousands of times an hour, while several hundred newtons push back and the wafer itself changes size with temperature.

> **Intuition: a washing-machine-sized microscope stage.** Picture a microscope stage the size of a washing machine that positions a dinner-plate wafer to a hundredth of a hair's width, ten thousand times an hour, while a few hundred newtons (the weight of a large dog) push down on it, and while the whole plate is held at 125 °C or −40 °C. That is a production prober. The hard part is not any one of those requirements but meeting all of them at once, unattended, for years.

The **wafer prober** is the mechanical half of a **sort cell**, one prober and one tester docked together. It holds the wafer, brings it to temperature, aligns it to the probe card, and steps it from die to die, pressing each die's pads up into the probe tips. The tester (the **automatic test equipment (ATE)**, described in its own section below) is docked on top; the probe card sits in the prober's **head plate**, the rigid ring at the top of the probing chamber; the wafer sits below on the **chuck**, the vacuum-clamped moving platen described next.

### The Chuck and Positioning

The chuck is a vacuum-clamped platen that pulls the wafer's 775 µm thickness (the standard thickness of a 300 mm wafer) flat against a **lapped** surface (one ground flat with a fine abrasive slurry to micrometre-level flatness, the same finishing step wafers themselves get in Module 03) and moves it in X, Y, Z, and **theta** (rotation about the vertical axis) with positioning repeatability of roughly ±1 µm. It must be that good: a tip landing 10 µm off center on a 45 µm pad risks cracking the edge of the **passivation** (the glass-like protective top layer, with windows over the pads; a crack at the window edge lets moisture in), and on a 40 µm **pitch** (centre-to-centre spacing) **microbump** array (solder bumps tens of micrometres across used to stack one die on another) 10 µm is a quarter of the pitch. The stage uses linear motors on air bearings (the moving parts float on a thin film of air, so there is no friction to fight) or precision ball screws, read by linear encoders (fine optical rulers that report position) on a granite base that neither flexes nor drifts, and it must hold position while several hundred newtons of probe force push down on it (see the worked example below).

### Temperature and Thermal Expansion

Temperature control is integrated into the chuck: −40 °C to +150 °C on production probers (to +200 °C for **wide-bandgap** devices, silicon carbide and gallium nitride power transistors, which are tested hot because that is how they are used), using resistive heaters and chiller or refrigeration loops. The die surface runs hotter than the chuck when the device dissipates power, and for a GPU dissipating hundreds of watts under test the chuck must actively sink that heat, which is why GPU sort chucks are special-order. Cold testing below the **dew point** (the temperature at which moisture in the surrounding air condenses) requires a dry-air or nitrogen purge of the probing chamber to prevent frost, which would short pads and change contact resistance.

Thermal expansion is a first-order problem, not a detail. Silicon's coefficient of thermal expansion is ~2.6 ppm/K: each kelvin of warming lengthens a piece of silicon by 2.6 millionths of its length.

> **Worked example: how much a hot wafer grows.** Across a 300 mm wafer, heating from 25 °C to 125 °C (a 100 K rise) expands the wafer by 300 mm × 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 = 78 µm edge to edge. Pads near the wafer edge therefore sit ~39 µm further from the centre than they did at room temperature. That is nearly two pad widths on a 45 µm pad, so a room-temperature alignment is useless at 125 °C. The probe card, built on a ceramic space transformer and a glass-fibre epoxy (FR-4) or polyimide (a heat-resistant polymer) circuit board with their own expansion coefficients, moves too, and by a different amount.

The prober therefore **soaks** the wafer at temperature (holds it until it stops changing, typically minutes), soaks the probe card by parking the hot chuck under it, and then re-aligns at temperature before the first touchdown; hot alignment and periodic re-alignment during the wafer are standard.

### Alignment and Touchdown

Alignment happens in two halves. A downward-looking camera on the prober bridge finds the wafer's pad pattern by pattern recognition and computes its position and rotation; an upward-looking camera on the chuck images the probe tips from below. From the two, the prober derives a transformation that places each die's pads under the tips to within about ±1 to 2 µm, verified by **probe mark inspection** after the first touchdowns: the prober images the pad and measures where the scrub mark landed relative to pad center.

The **touchdown** is the moment the chuck rises to bring the pads into the tips. The problem it must solve is chemical: exposed aluminum grows a **native oxide**, a few nanometers of Al₂O₃ that forms instantly in air and is an insulator, so a tip resting gently on a pad makes no reliable electrical contact. The mechanism is spring compression plus a wipe. The chuck rises until first contact (detected by a contact sense circuit, a capacitive sensor, or simply the known Z height from the tip camera) and then continues upward by a programmed **overtravel**. Each probe is a spring, so driving it past first touch compresses it and generates a contact force set by the probe's spring constant and the overtravel. Cantilever needles also translate along the pad as they deflect, producing a **scrub** that scrapes through the oxide to reach clean metal. The numbers: overtravel is typically 50 to 100 µm for cantilever cards and 50 to 75 µm for vertical cards; force per probe lands at 2 to 8 gram-force (20 to 80 mN); cantilever scrub is 10 to 25 µm long. Vertical probes scrub less, which is why they are preferred on delicate **low-k** stacks (the porous, fragile insulator between the top wires, Module 12, which cracks under point loads) and on solder bumps, where a wiping motion would smear solder onto the tip. The **space transformer** named above is defined in the next section.

> **Worked example: total probe force on a big-die probe card.** A vertical MEMS probe card for a large GPU die carries ~12,000 probes (several thousand signal pins plus a dense grid of power and ground probes, which are needed to deliver hundreds of amps without excessive voltage drop). At 3 gf per probe, total force is 12,000 × 3 gf = 36,000 gf = 36 kgf, or about 353 N, pushing down on the chuck at every touchdown. A full-wafer DRAM card with 100,000 probes at 2 gf carries 200 kgf, about 2 kN, which is why memory probers are built with reinforced Z stages and why probe cards need a stiffener plate to keep the card from bowing under its own contact force by more than the ~10 µm of planarity budget. This force also sets the mechanical fatigue life of the card and the prober.

### Throughput

The prober then steps: chuck down, **index** (the stage move from one die or group of dies to the next), chuck up. Index-plus-touchdown time is roughly 0.3 to 0.6 s per step, small against a logic test of tens of seconds but a meaningful fraction of a short 2 s memory test step. Wafer exchange, **notch** pre-alignment (the notch is the small V-cut on the wafer edge that marks crystal orientation), and full alignment add 1 to 2 minutes per wafer, so probers carry two **FOUP** ports (the FOUP is the sealed 25-wafer carrier, the front-opening unified pod) and a pre-aligner to have the next wafer ready.

| Item | Reticle-sized GPU wafer | DRAM wafer, full-wafer-contact card |
|---|---|---|
| Candidate dies per wafer | ~65 | Tens of thousands |
| Touchdowns per wafer | ~65 for test, plus ~10 cleaning touchdowns (the tips are pressed onto an abrasive pad at the chuck edge to scrape off aluminum debris; the mechanism is under Probe Cards) | One, or a handful |
| Index plus touchdown per step | 0.3 to 0.6 s (~30 s per wafer in total) | 0.3 to 0.6 s (negligible) |
| Test time per die | ~240 s, summed over a hot and a room-temperature insertion | Set by the memory patterns, not by stepping (Module 15) |
| Load, pre-align and align | 1 to 2 min per wafer, per insertion | 1 to 2 min per wafer |
| Wafer time in the cell | 65 × 240 s plus overhead: more than 4 hours across both temperature insertions, i.e. roughly 2 hours per insertion, with a second load, soak and hot alignment between them | Dominated by test pattern time |

> **Why it matters downstream.** Wafer time multiplied by wafer volume is the number of sort cells a product needs, and a sort cell is a multi-million-dollar asset; the multi-site section below turns "more than 4 hours per GPU wafer" into a cell count and a capital budget.

> **What can go wrong.** *Probe-to-pad offset*: thermal drift after a temperature change, or a bad initial alignment, walks the tips off pad centre; the scrub runs off the pad, cracks the passivation edge, and the die fails continuity. Probe mark inspection on the first dies of every wafer catches it. *A particle under the wafer*: a single particle a few tens of micrometres across, trapped between chuck and wafer, tilts the wafer enough to break the planarity budget, so probes on one side miss and probes on the other punch through the pad. *Frost*: a purge that lags the chuck below dew point leaves water or ice on the pads, shorting neighbours and raising contact resistance; the symptom is a wave of continuity fails on the first dies after a cold ramp. *Card crash*: a Z-height error or a wrong overtravel drives the card into the wafer, bending or breaking hundreds of probes on a card that costs $100,000 to more than $1 million (next section).

### Who Makes Probers

Two companies make essentially the entire world's supply of 300 mm production probers: **Tokyo Electron (TEL)** (the Precio family: Precio XL for logic and Precio nano, succeeded since 2021 by the Prexa series, with Prexa MS for full-wafer memory contact) and **Tokyo Seimitsu**, trading as **Accretech** (the UF3000-series, UF3000EX and later). Between them they hold on the order of 80 to 90 percent of the market as of 2025; the remainder is smaller players and Chinese entrants that have been growing under the domestic-substitution push. A production 300 mm prober costs on the order of $0.7 to 1.5 million depending on temperature range and options; vendors do not publish list prices.

## Probe Cards

The probe card's problem is one of scale. The tester's electronics end in thousands of contacts spaced millimetres apart on a plate the size of a dinner tray; the die's pads are spaced a hair's width apart on a thumbnail. Something has to bridge that gap, carry hundreds of amps and multi-gigabit signals across it, and hold every tip at the same height to a fraction of the spring travel, wafer after wafer, at any temperature the chuck can reach.

> **Intuition: a funnel of wiring.** A probe card is a funnel that steps the tester's thousands of connections, spaced millimetres apart, down to tips spaced a hair's width apart, and holds every tip flat to a hundredth of a hair. Each stage of the funnel is a different material with a different thermal expansion, and the last stage is ten thousand microscopic springs that must all compress by the same amount.

The **probe card** is the consumable that translates the ATE's **pin electronics** (the tester's per-wire driver and measurement circuits, described in the next section), arranged on a ~15-inch circular **PCB** (printed circuit board) with contact pads on a millimeter-scale pitch, down to probe tips on a 40 to 150 µm pitch that touch the die. The tester's **pogo tower**, a ring of spring-loaded pins on the test head, presses down onto the card's outer pads. The pads on the die may be an **area array**, spread over the whole face of the die as on every flip-chip GPU, or **peripheral**, arranged around the edge as on wire-bonded chips; which one it is decides which card architectures can reach them. It is a mechanical, electrical, and thermal design problem simultaneously, and for advanced logic it is a custom part built for one die design, usually with lead times of 8 to 16 weeks and a cost from $100,000 for a modest device to well over $1 million for a large multi-site logic card or a full-wafer-contact DRAM card.

### Three Architectures

**Cantilever cards** are the oldest: tungsten or tungsten-rhenium needles, ~0.1 to 0.25 mm in diameter, epoxy-mounted around a ring so they fan inward and downward to the die, with 10 to 30 µm tips. They are cheap ($5,000 to $50,000) and repairable, but they scale poorly: the needles reach in from the periphery, so they cannot contact an area array; the long needles have tens of nanohenries of inductance and cannot carry signals much above a few hundred MHz; and pin counts top out in the low thousands. Inductance is a wire's resistance to changes in current, so a long needle rounds off every fast edge and the tester cannot switch the pin quickly. They remain common for analog, power, discrete, and legacy logic.

**Vertical probe cards** were pioneered by IBM in the 1970s as the **cobra** or buckling-beam probe: a straight wire, ~50 to 100 µm in diameter, held between two guide plates (ceramic, laser-drilled with tens of thousands of holes) with a slight offset between them so that under compression the wire buckles predictably in a known direction. Because the probes are vertical, they can contact a full area array, and the guide-plate approach scales to tens of thousands of probes. Modern vertical probes are often called "cobra" generically even when made by Technoprobe, MJC, or JEM.

**MEMS probe cards** replaced hand-assembled wire with lithographically defined springs. **Micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS)** are microscopic mechanical parts built with the same photolithography and electroplating used to make chips, so the springs are identical to the micrometre and can be made ten thousand at a time. FormFactor's MicroSpring (wire-bonded gold springs) started the category in the 1990s; today's advanced cards use photolithography and electroplating to build springs of nickel-cobalt or nickel-manganese alloys with rhodium, palladium-cobalt, or other hard tip platings, thousands at a time on a carrier, then transfer them to the space transformer. The alloys are chosen for their jobs: a nickel-cobalt spring holds its force over a million compressions without fatiguing, and a rhodium or palladium-cobalt tip is hard, does not oxidize, and sheds aluminum debris. MEMS tips can be made with a defined geometry (a small flat, a point, a crown for bumps), pitch down to ~40 µm on area arrays and ~20 to 30 µm on some memory cards, spring constants tuned to a few gf, and consistent electrical characteristics probe to probe. The leaders in advanced MEMS cards are **FormFactor** (US), **Technoprobe** (Italy), **Micronics Japan (MJC)**, and **Japan Electronic Materials (JEM)**, with **MPI** (Taiwan) and several Korean suppliers behind them; their market shares are given under The Equipment Market.

### Anatomy of an Advanced Card

From the ATE downward: a **PCB** of 30 to 60 layers (a PC motherboard has 6 to 12) that carries signals inward from the tester's pogo tower contact ring, with power-rail **decoupling capacitors** placed as close to the die as possible; an **interposer** of spring pins or elastomer to absorb tolerance (in probe-card jargon an interposer is a compliant spacer, not the silicon interposer of CoWoS); the **space transformer**, a multilayer ceramic or organic substrate that translates from ~1 mm pitch to the die's pad pitch in ten or more routing layers; the **probe head** with its guide plates and probes; and a **stiffener**, a steel plate that keeps the card from bowing under its own contact force, holding the assembly flat to within ~10 to 25 µm across the array. The ceramic is **LTCC or HTCC** (low- or high-temperature co-fired ceramic: stacked ceramic sheets with printed wiring, fired into one block, dimensionally stable when hot) from Kyocera, NTK, and others; the alternative is an **organic build-up** substrate, the laminated resin substrate also used under packages (Module 16). Planarity is everything: if one physical corner of the probe array sits 30 µm higher than the opposite one, probes on the low corner see 30 µm less overtravel and may not break through the oxide, while those on the high corner may punch through the pad.

The decoupling capacitors deserve a sentence. A capacitor next to the die is a local reservoir of charge: when the die suddenly draws more current, the reservoir supplies it for the first nanoseconds, because current from the tester cannot accelerate through the inductance of the delivery path fast enough. The loop inductance of the power path limits how fast the device can draw current, and the capacitors are what bridge the gap.

> **Worked example: power delivery through parallel probes (illustrative).** Suppose a GPU card gives ~1,000 of its probes to the core supply and each probe, with its contact, is ~0.3 Ω. In parallel they are ~0.3 Ω / 1,000 = ~0.3 mΩ. At 500 A the drop across them is 500 A × 0.3 mΩ = 0.15 V, a fifth of a 0.75 V core rail, and that is before the drop in the space transformer, PCB and cables. This is why a GPU card dedicates thousands of probes to power and ground, why the tester senses the voltage at the card rather than at its own output (remote sensing, next section), and why the program throttles activity during the highest-current tests.

Electrically, even a MEMS card has ~0.5 to 2 nH of inductance and a few hundred milliohms of resistance per probe, with crosstalk between neighbors. This is why full-rate **SerDes** testing at 100+ Gb/s is generally not done at sort (a **serializer/deserializer** is the circuit pair that turns parallel data into one very fast serial stream on a wire and back, as in PCI Express (PCIe, the standard expansion bus) and NVLink (NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU link)), and why sort tests high-speed I/O via internal **loopback** (connecting the chip's transmitter to its own receiver so it tests itself, explained under the test program) or at reduced rate.

### Contact Resistance, Cleaning, Lifetime, and Pad Damage

Each probe-to-pad contact has a **contact resistance (Cres)** that starts below about 1 Ω for a fresh probe on clean aluminum and rises as aluminum debris, oxide, and (on bumped wafers) solder accumulate on the tip. The program monitors Cres through the continuity test on every die, and once it exceeds a threshold of a few ohms, or the continuity fail rate ticks up, the prober performs an **online clean**: the chuck moves under an abrasive medium (a polymer sheet loaded with alumina or silicon carbide grit, or a tungsten-carbide plate) mounted at its edge and touches the probes down a programmed number of times. Cleaning intervals run from every few hundred to every few thousand touchdowns, and each clean removes a little tip material; tip wear and spring fatigue together set a MEMS card's life at roughly 0.5 to 1 million-plus touchdowns, after which it goes back to the vendor for probe-head refurbishment.

> **Worked example: card life in wafers (illustrative).** A single-site GPU card makes ~65 test touchdowns plus ~10 cleaning touchdowns per wafer, ~75 in all. One million touchdowns is therefore ~1,000,000 / 75 ≈ 13,000 wafers, which at a few wafers per cell per day is years of service. A 16-site mobile card on a wafer of ~2,000 dies makes ~2,000 / 16 = 125 touchdowns per wafer, so a million touchdowns is ~8,000 wafers. In practice GPU cards are usually retired for damage (a crash, a bent probe, a lost tip) or because the die design changed, not for wear; the high-volume mobile card is the one that actually wears out.

Probe marks matter because the same pads must later be **wire bonded** (a ~20 µm gold or copper wire welded from pad to package lead) or bumped. Assembly houses specify a maximum probe-mark area (~25 to 50 percent of the pad) and depth (a fraction of the ~1 µm aluminum thickness; punching through to the **barrier**, the thin titanium-nitride film under the aluminum that stops the metals mixing, causes bond failures). On **copper-pillar** wafers (a plated copper post with a solder cap, the flip-chip connection of Module 16) and microbump wafers the problem inverts: the tip is harder than the solder, so the risk is deforming the bump height beyond what the later **thermocompression** bond (pressing a heated die onto its substrate so the solder caps fuse) tolerates, and picking up solder that raises Cres on the next die. Low-force (1 to 2 gf) vertical MEMS probes with small flat tips are used, and some HBM and chiplet designs add sacrificial test pads to avoid touching the fine-pitch array at all.

> **What can go wrong.** *Planarity loss*: the stiffener creeps or the card warps after thousands of hot-cold cycles, so one region of probes stops making contact while another over-scrubs; caught by tip-height mapping on a card metrology station. *Bent or missing probes*: after a crash a card with a few hundred bent probes shows the same pins failing continuity on every die of every wafer, the signature that separates a card problem from a wafer problem. *Over-scrub*: a wrong overtravel drives the scrub through the ~1 µm aluminum to the barrier, and the wafer passes sort but its dies fail at wire bond weeks later. *Electrostatic discharge (ESD)*: a static shock delivered through the card punches a hole in a gate oxide on the die. *The mid-wafer continuity spike*: the classic signature of a dirty card is a continuity fail rate that climbs with touchdown count rather than with die position (a wafer problem clusters in space; a card problem grows in time); the response is an online clean, and if the rate does not fall, the card comes off for inspection.

### The Microbump Problem

For HBM base dies and **chiplets** (dies designed as one part of a multi-die package) on interposers, the I/O is on microbumps at ~40 to 55 µm pitch (about 50 µm through HBM3E, ~40 µm for HBM4's doubled interface) and pushing below, with thousands of bumps in a compact array; Module 18 quotes ~25 to 40 µm for the finest current bumps, and published values vary by vendor and generation. Probing a 1,024-bit HBM3 or 2,048-bit HBM4 interface directly (the number is the count of parallel data wires between GPU and stack, each on its own microbump) is at the edge of probe technology for two separate reasons. The first is geometric: the pitch is at the limit of guide-plate drilling and MEMS spring fabrication, and a probe that must fit inside a 40 µm pitch is a thinner, weaker spring than one built for 100 µm. The second is mechanical: the tips must land on solder caps only ~20 µm across (Module 17 gives ~10 to 15 µm of solder on top of a 20 to 30 µm copper pillar for GPU-side bumps) without deforming them beyond what the later thermocompression bond tolerates. The force budget is therefore 1 to 2 gf per probe rather than the 2 to 8 gf used on aluminum, the tips are small flats rather than points, there is no deliberate scrub, and the dent each touchdown leaves is held to a small fraction of the cap height, checked by measuring probe-mark depth on sample bumps with an optical profiler rather than by the oxide-breaking argument used for aluminum pads.

> **Worked example: contact pressure on a solder cap (illustrative).** Pressure is force divided by contact area. A vertical MEMS probe with a flat tip 10 µm on a side has a contact area of 100 µm² = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m². At 2 gf (~20 mN) the contact pressure is 20 mN / 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m² = 2 × 10⁸ Pa, or 200 MPa; at 1 gf it is 100 MPa. Tin-silver solder is soft: its hardness (the pressure at which a flat punch sinks into it) is of order ~100 MPa, so even the lowest usable force dents the cap on every touchdown, and the dent is what breaks the thin oxide on the solder and makes the contact. What limits the force is therefore not contact quality but dent depth and solder pickup, and the same arithmetic says why the aluminum-pad rules do not transfer: an aluminum pad is harder and is covered by an insulating oxide that must be wiped through, so aluminum needs a scrub and solder must avoid one. The reusable model is pressure = force / tip area; halving the side of the tip flat quarters the area and quadruples the pressure at the same force.

The industry's answer combines very fine-pitch MEMS cards (FormFactor and Technoprobe both ship below 40 µm pitch as of ~2025), the **IEEE 1500 wrapper** on the HBM base die (a standard test-access boundary around a block, more under Design for Test) that exposes the whole stack through a few hundred **direct-access pins**, bumps on the base die through which a tester can reach every DRAM die's arrays without touching the wide interface, and testing the high-speed interface only after assembly. **Hybrid-bonded** HBM, in which copper pads are pressed together with no solder at all (Module 17), is deferred beyond HBM4, which stays on microbumps; with sub-10 µm pad pitch it cannot be probed on its bond pads at all and will rely entirely on test-access pads and built-in self-test.

> **What can go wrong.** *Cap deformation*: a force or overtravel set for aluminum flattens the solder caps, the stack's bump heights no longer match within the thermocompression bonder's tolerance, and joints open or short at assembly weeks later; the fix is a dent-depth measurement on the first wafers of every card. *Solder pickup*: tin transfers to the tip, Cres climbs die after die, and the cleaning interval must be shortened, at the cost of tip life. *Missed bumps*: at 40 µm pitch the ±1 to 2 µm alignment budget is a twentieth of the pitch, and a thermal drift that would be harmless on 100 µm pads lands tips on the edge of the caps; probe-mark inspection is run more often on bumped wafers. *Untestable interface*: the wide interface that cannot be probed is the one that carries the product's bandwidth, so an interface defect is first seen at CoW-stage test, on an assembly that cannot be reworked.

This subsection is a bridge, not the full treatment: the Known-Good-Die section below gives the HBM test flow in outline, Module 15 covers the stacking process, Module 17 the microbump and hybrid bonding processes, and Module 18 the stack test through the base die in detail. The point here is what a probe card can and cannot do at these pitches, and why the answer pushed test access into the die.

## The ATE

The tester's problem is electronic: drive and sample thousands of wires with timing accurate to tens of picoseconds, deliver hundreds of amps at under a volt without letting the voltage wobble, measure currents of microamps on the same pins a moment later, and do all of it through a probe card while a prober presses a wafer onto it, for years, with results that agree from one tester to the next.

> **Intuition: a thousand synchronised oscilloscopes.** Each tester channel is a tiny programmable signal generator plus an oscilloscope for one wire: it can drive that wire to any voltage at any instant, or look at it and ask "is it above or below this threshold right now?" There are thousands of them, all synchronised to a fraction of a nanosecond, plus a bank of power supplies that delivers welding-class currents at less than a volt. A tester does not run the chip; it plays a pre-computed score to it and listens for wrong notes.

The **automatic test equipment (ATE)** is the electronic half of the cell: a rack-sized **mainframe** (the cabinet holding power, cooling and the controlling computer) with a **test head** that docks to the prober (or, at final test, to a handler) via a **manipulator**, a counterbalanced arm that positions the several-hundred-kilogram head. The test head holds the instrument cards; the **device interface board (DIB)**, called a **load board** at final test or a **probe interface board (PIB)** at sort, sits on top of it and connects to the probe card through the pogo tower. The tester is built for a class of device: **system-on-chip (SoC)** testers for processors that put everything on one die, **mixed-signal** (analog plus digital) instruments for radio and sensor parts, and memory testers with massive parallelism.

### What Is Inside

**Digital pin electronics** are the core. Each channel has a driver with programmable levels, **slew** (how fast the edge rises), and **termination** (the resistor that stops reflections on the wire); a comparator that samples the pin at a programmed time against programmable thresholds; and a **per-pin parametric measurement unit (PPMU)** that forces voltage and measures current (or the reverse) from microamps to hundreds of milliamps. Behind each channel sits deep **vector** memory (a vector is one row of the test pattern, the 0/1 value every pin must drive or expect at one clock tick; a test is millions of them, and scan patterns are enormous), a timing generator with **edge placement accuracy** of a few tens of picoseconds (V93000 Pin Scale 5000 channels run to 5 Gb/s with ~±25 to 50 ps accuracy at the **device under test (DUT)**, the chip being tested), and a fail memory that records which vector and pin mismatched so diagnosis can trace a failure back to a **scan cell** (one flip-flop in a scan chain, next section). A test head carries 1,000 to 5,000 such channels for SoC test and tens of thousands for multi-site memory.

**Device power supplies (DPS)** deliver the DUT's **rails**, its separate supply voltage nets, such as the 0.75 V core supply. A GPU sort needs hundreds of amps at roughly 0.7 to 1.0 V with millivolt regulation and microsecond transient response, because current draw changes by hundreds of amps when a scan pattern starts toggling. Advantest's DC Scale XPS-series and Teradyne's equivalents gang many channels in parallel with **remote sensing** at the probe card, a second pair of wires that measures the voltage where it matters and lets the supply raise its output to correct for the drop in the delivery path; a configured GPU supply can exceed 1,000 A. The drop across the probe card's power path is the reason for the thousands of power and ground probes noted earlier: at 500 A, even 1 mΩ is 0.5 V, which would collapse a 0.75 V rail.

> **Worked example: where the volts go between supply and die.** Take the 500 A GPU case. If the total path from supply output to die were 1 mΩ, the die would see 0.5 V less than the supply put out, and a 0.75 V rail would collapse to 0.25 V. Remote sensing fixes the part of the drop that lies between the supply and the sense point on the probe card: the supply simply raises its output until the sense wires read 0.75 V. It cannot fix the last stretch, through the probes themselves, because there is no sense point on the die; from the probe-card example that stretch is ~0.3 mΩ for ~1,000 parallel power probes, or 0.15 V at 500 A. The program compensates by setting the sense target a little high, by limiting activity during high-current tests, and by relying on on-card and on-die capacitors for the first nanoseconds of any current step (the GPU section computes why).

**Mixed-signal and RF instruments** (arbitrary waveform generators, digitizers, Advantest's Wave Scale series) test analog blocks, **PLLs** (a **phase-locked loop** is the circuit that multiplies a reference clock up to the chip's operating clock), and **PHYs** (the **physical-layer** circuits that drive and receive off-chip wires). **Serial-link instruments** generate and check **PRBS** patterns (a **pseudo-random bit sequence**, a scrambled but exactly reproducible stream used to stress a link) at tens of gigabits per second with **jitter** injection (deliberate timing wobble of the edges) to characterize SerDes **eye** margins (thousands of received bits overlaid on one screen; the open area in the middle is the timing and voltage margin). **High-voltage instruments** program e-fuses (one-time-programmable on-die fuses, defined under the test program) and run stress tests.

The tester runs a **test program** (written in Advantest's SmarTest or Teradyne's IG-XL environment, the two vendors' programming languages and runtimes; it is a flow of test suites with limits and bin assignments) that executes each test, assigns a bin, and logs everything to a datalog. A sort cell runs unattended once loaded: the prober reports each die's position over **GPIB** (an old instrument-control bus still universal in test floors) or Ethernet and the tester returns the bin.

> **What can go wrong.** *Calibration and skew drift*: the timing of each channel drifts with temperature and age, so a tester is calibrated against a reference fixture on a schedule; **skew** is the difference in arrival time between edges on different channels that were meant to be simultaneous, and a channel-to-channel skew of a few tens of picoseconds is enough to fail an at-speed test on a good die. *Worn pogo pins and DIB damage*: the pogo tower's spring pins and the interface board wear like any contact and add resistance or intermittents that look like device failures. *Supply instability*: a supply with fast feedback driving a probe card with nanohenries of inductance can oscillate, and the ringing shows up as random failures at pattern start. *Tester-caused fails and correlation*: a failure that follows which tester or test head ran the lot, rather than the wafer, is a tester problem, usually timing skew or a marginal at-speed test, and every floor tracks yield by tester for exactly this reason. Before a program is released, the same wafers are run on several testers and the results must agree within a defined margin; a program that passes on one tester and fails on another is not released.

### Who Makes Testers

Three product families dominate SoC testing worldwide: **Advantest V93000** (the EXA Scale generation since 2020, with SmarTest 8 software), **Advantest T2000** (a modular platform strong in Japanese and mixed-signal markets), and **Teradyne UltraFLEX** and **UltraFLEXplus** (with IG-XL software). Memory testing uses different platforms with massive parallelism: Advantest's T5800-series and Teradyne's Magnum. A high-end configured SoC tester lists at $2 to 10 million and more; the instrument mix is what drives the number, and a GPU sort configuration with thousands of digital channels, high-current power supplies, and serial-link instruments sits at the upper end. Market shares are given under The Equipment Market.

## What a Test Program Contains

A production sort program is a sequence of tests ordered so that the cheapest tests that catch the most failures run first, and a die that fails early is abandoned (**stop-on-fail**) rather than wasting tester seconds. The order below is typical.

**Continuity (opens/shorts).** With supplies off, the PPMU pulls a small current (say 100 µA) out of each pin and measures the voltage. Every pad carries an **electrostatic discharge (ESD) diode**, a protection diode that shunts static shocks to ground. The diode sits between ground and the pad, pointing from ground toward the pad (it conducts whenever the pad is dragged below ground, which is what a negative static spike does), so pulling current out of the pad forward-biases it: it conducts and drops ~0.5 to 0.7 V. The supplies are off so that this diode is the only path; with the chip unpowered nothing else can source or sink current at the pad and confuse the reading. A working pin therefore reads the diode drop, an open reads the **compliance** limit (the ceiling voltage the source is allowed to reach), and a short reads ~0 V. It takes milliseconds, verifies probe contact, and catches gross defects, and because it runs on every die it doubles as the program's running check on probe-card health (the dirty-card signature is under Probe Cards).

**Leakage and IDDQ.** Each rail is powered and its **quiescent** (idle) current measured with the logic in a known state. **IDDQ testing** (the name is I_DD, the supply current, with Q for quiescent) was once a powerful screen: in CMOS every logic gate is a stack of n- and p-type transistors of which one is always off in any settled state, so no path from supply to ground exists and the current is only leakage; a bridging defect from supply to ground creates such a path and adds a constant current. On modern nodes the intrinsic IDDQ of billions of leaky transistors is amperes and varies across the wafer by more than a defect adds, so fixed limits are useless. What survives is **delta-IDDQ** (compare current between vector states; a defect adds a constant, leakage does not change) and per-die outlier analysis, discussed under PAT.

**DC parametrics.** Input thresholds, output drive, **pull-up and pull-down** resistances (the weak resistors that hold an unconnected pin at a defined level), and pin leakages are measured on a sample of pins with the PPMU; on-die regulators and references are measured; internal supply monitors are read.

**Scan / ATPG structural test.** This is the workhorse of digital test and consumes most of the tester's vector memory. The vocabulary: a **flip-flop** is a one-bit memory that copies its input on each clock edge, and a GPU has hundreds of millions; **combinational logic** is the gates between flip-flops that compute the next values; a **net** is one wire in the circuit; a **multiplexer** is a two-way switch; a **clock-domain crossing** is a place where signals pass between parts of the chip running on different clocks.

> **Intuition: pausing the chip and reading every register.** Scan makes every flip-flop part of one enormous shift register, so the tester can load any state into the chip and read any result out, as if it could pause the chip at any instant and inspect every register. Loading a state exercises the logic that feeds each flip-flop; reading the state back reveals what that logic did. A defect anywhere in the combinational logic changes at least one captured bit, and that bit is shifted out to a pin where the tester compares it against the expected value.

During design, every flip-flop was given a multiplexer that, in test mode, connects it into long shift registers called **scan chains**. The tester shifts a pattern into all chains at once (thousands of shift cycles at 50 to 200 MHz), pulses the functional clock for one or two cycles to capture the combinational logic's response, and shifts the result out for comparison while the next pattern shifts in. **Automatic test pattern generation (ATPG)** tools (Synopsys TestMAX, Siemens Tessent) compute patterns from a fault model, not from the design's function: the **stuck-at model** asks whether each net could be observed if stuck at 0 or 1; the **transition-delay model** asks whether a slow-to-rise or slow-to-fall net would be caught by a launch-and-capture clock pair at rated speed (**at-speed scan**); bridging, small-delay, and cell-aware models cover defects inside standard cells. A production program targets ~99 percent-plus stuck-at and ~90 percent-plus transition coverage; the last few percent hide behind untestable or clock-domain-crossing logic. **Test compression** (Tessent TestKompress, Synopsys DFTMAX) inserts a **decompressor** at the chain inputs and a **compactor** at the outputs so a few dozen tester channels feed thousands of internal chains at 50 to 200× compression; without it, a large GPU's scan data would be many gigabits per die and scan alone would take minutes.

> **Worked example: scan time with and without compression (illustrative).** Take a die with ~200 million flip-flops, organised into 40,000 scan chains of 5,000 flops each, fed from 400 tester channels through a 100× decompressor, shifting at 100 MHz. Loading one pattern takes 5,000 shift cycles, or 5,000 / 100 MHz = 50 µs (the previous result shifts out at the same time). A set of 30,000 patterns therefore takes 30,000 × 50 µs ≈ 1.5 s, plus capture cycles and overhead. Without compression the same 400 channels would each have to feed one chain of 500,000 flops, so each pattern takes 5 ms and the same set takes ~150 s. That factor of 100 is the difference between a sort program that fits in a 240 s budget and one that does not.

**Memory BIST.** A GPU has hundreds of megabytes of **static random-access memory (SRAM)**, the fast on-die memory whose cells hold a bit for as long as power is applied, in thousands of instances (register files and the L1 and L2 **caches**, the small fast memories closest to the compute units). Testing them from the pins would be impossible, so each instance or group has a **memory built-in self-test (MBIST)** controller that runs a **March algorithm**: read and write passes over all addresses in ascending and descending order, with **data backgrounds** (the pattern written before each read pass: all 0s, all 1s, checkerboard) chosen to expose stuck cells, coupling faults between neighbors, address-decoder faults, and **read-disturb** (a read that flips a weak cell). March C- takes 10n operations for n words; variants for advanced-node SRAM add **hammering** (repeatedly accessing one row to see whether it disturbs its neighbours) and retention tests. The controller reports the failing addresses, which feed **built-in self-repair (BISR)**: the memory has spare rows and columns, the repair logic computes a substitution map, the map is programmed into **e-fuses** (electrically programmable fuses: tiny on-die links blown permanently by a current pulse to store a bit, described below), and the memory is re-tested. Without repair, a single failed bit among a few billion SRAM bits would scrap the die.

> **Worked example: how long a March test takes (illustrative).** Consider a 512 Mbit SRAM instance organised as 4 M words of 128 bits. March C- needs 10 operations per word: 10 × 4,194,304 ≈ 42 million read and write operations, or ~42 ms at one operation per cycle at 1 GHz. A GPU has thousands of instances, but each has its own BIST engine and they run in parallel, so the whole memory test takes seconds rather than minutes. What limits it is not time but power: thousands of SRAMs hammering at full rate at once draw more current than the probe card can deliver, so the program schedules them in groups.

**Functional and at-speed vectors.** Sequences that exercise the design as designed. Functional vectors have low fault coverage per tester second compared to scan, so they are kept short at sort and used for things scan cannot see: PLL lock, reset behavior, and analog-digital interaction.

**Analog and SerDes loopback.** For high-speed I/O, the transmitter is looped back to the receiver either on-die (internal loopback) or through a short trace on the probe card (external loopback), and the receiver's **eye monitor** (a circuit inside the receiver that measures its own timing and voltage margin) and error counter report the margin. This lets a sort program verify PHY functionality without a tester instrument that can run at full line rate through the probe card's **parasitics** (the card's unwanted stray inductance, capacitance and resistance: the ~0.5 to 2 nH and hundreds of milliohms per probe quoted under Probe Cards, which round off and attenuate a 100 Gb/s signal before it reaches the die). Full-rate characterization is a final-test and SLT job.

**High-voltage stress and e-fuse programming.** Where the design has a burn-in-like stress mode (elevated supply voltage for a few seconds to weaken marginal gate oxide so that it fails now rather than in the field), it is often run here. E-fuses are then programmed: repair solutions, analog trim values, the **electronic chip ID (ECID)** encoding lot, wafer, and X-Y position, feature disables for partial-good configurations, and security keys. Polysilicon or metal e-fuses are blown by driving a current of ~10 to 30 mA through a narrow link until it **electromigrates** open: at high current density the flow of electrons physically sweeps metal atoms along the link until it breaks. **Antifuses** do the opposite, punching a permanent conductive hole through a thin oxide with a high voltage. Programmed values are read back and verified in the same insertion.

> **What can go wrong.** *Droop at pattern start*: the first vectors of a heavy scan pattern draw a current step the supply cannot follow, the rail sags below V_min (the lowest supply voltage at which the die still passes, defined under binning) for a few nanoseconds, and a good die fails; the fix is a slower pattern ramp, more on-card capacitance, or a relaxed limit on the first vectors. *Limits set too tight*: a limit that was set from a few early lots rejects good dies from a later, faster process corner; a **process corner** is one extreme of the normal manufacturing spread, for example the fastest-and-leakiest or the slowest-and-tightest transistors the process is allowed to make, and a good die from a fast corner sits at the edge of every parametric distribution. Limits are re-centred as the process matures. *A repair map that does not fit*: an SRAM with more failing rows than spare rows cannot be repaired, and the die is scrapped; the BISR spare budget is set from the expected fail-bit distribution at target D0.

Where the seconds go: for a ~240 s GPU sort (both temperature insertions together), an illustrative split is scan and at-speed scan ~40 percent, MBIST and repair ~15 percent, the V_min and F_max searches described under binning ~20 percent, loopback and analog ~10 percent, e-fuse programming and read-back ~5 percent, and stepping, soak and overhead ~10 percent. Real programs vary widely, but scan is always the largest line, which is why compression and shift frequency are the first things a test engineer optimises.

## Design for Test

None of the above is possible unless the chip was built to be tested. **Design for test (DFT)** is the collection of on-chip structures inserted during **implementation**, the design stage where logic is turned into a physical layout. What is physically added: a multiplexer in front of every flip-flop so it can be chained; **on-chip clock controllers** that can issue the precise launch-and-capture clock pairs for at-speed scan; the decompressor and compactor for test compression; a BIST engine beside every memory instance and many analog blocks; a serial **test access port** through which all of it is reached; and a set of on-die sensors. What it costs: on a large SoC, a few percent of die area, a small timing penalty on every flip-flop (the multiplexer sits in the data path), and engineer-months to engineer-years of design effort. Why designers accept it: the DFT architecture sets the test cost for the product's whole life, and without it there is no known-good-die and no partial-good harvesting. Poor compression or long chains cost tester seconds on every die; strong BIST coverage allows fewer channels and more sites in parallel.

**Scan chains** and **compression** are as described above. On a big die, DFT inserts hundreds of chains, each thousands of flops long, with on-chip clock controllers for at-speed launch. The rest of the infrastructure is standardized so that tools from different vendors and IP from different suppliers fit together:

| Standard | What it gives access to | Where a GPU uses it |
|---|---|---|
| IEEE 1149.1 (**JTAG**, after the Joint Test Action Group that wrote it) | A five-pin serial test access port (TCK clock, TMS mode select, TDI data in, TDO data out, TRST reset) and a **boundary-scan** register, a shift register threaded through every I/O pad, so a tester can drive and observe every pad serially | The front door to all other on-chip test infrastructure; originally for board-level interconnect test |
| IEEE 1500 | A standardized **core wrapper**, a boundary register around an **IP block** (a pre-designed, reusable circuit block, often licensed) that isolates it so it can be tested independently of the surrounding logic | Every **hard IP** block (delivered as a fixed layout: a PCIe PHY, an HBM PHY, a licensed CPU core) comes with its 1500 wrapper; the HBM standard adopted 1500 as the test interface to the stack |
| IEEE 1687 (IJTAG) | A hierarchical network of instruments behind the JTAG port, described in a standard language so tools can automatically generate the access sequences | Hundreds of embedded instruments: BIST engines, sensors, PLL test modes |
| IEEE 1838 | Test access through 3D-stacked dies via **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, vertical copper conductors etched through a die (Module 15) | HBM stacks and TSMC **SoIC** (system-on-integrated-chips) die stacks (Module 17) |

**MBIST** and **BISR** for memories were described above. **Logic BIST (LBIST)** exercises the logic without external vectors: a pseudo-random pattern generator (a **linear-feedback shift register (LFSR)**, a shift register with feedback taps that produces a long, reproducible pseudo-random sequence) feeds the scan chains and a **signature analyzer** (a circuit that compresses a long output stream into one checksum) collects the result; it is used for in-field self-test in automotive. **Analog BIST** covers PLLs, **analog-to-digital converters (ADCs)**, and PHYs. **On-die monitors** (ring oscillators, temperature sensors, voltage droop monitors, and aging sensors, which proteanTecs and Synopsys's **Silicon Lifecycle Management (SLM)** suite, its product line for on-die monitors and the analytics that read them, sell as IP) are logged at every insertion as part of the die's lifetime record.

> **What can go wrong.** *A broken chain*: one defective flip-flop or one open in a scan chain makes every pattern through that chain unreadable, so the die is untestable rather than merely faulty; chain-integrity patterns run first to find it, and a hold-time failure in shift mode (data racing through two flops on one clock edge because the shift path is too fast) is the classic design-side cause. *Untestable logic*: redundant logic, constant nets and analog-facing paths cannot be observed and cap the achievable coverage below 100 percent. *Clock-domain crossings*: paths between clock domains cannot be tested at speed with a simple launch-capture pair and need dedicated handling or are excluded. *A DFT bug at first silicon*: if the test access port or a compression path is wrong in the first wafers, the chip cannot be characterised and the fix is a respin, which is why DFT is verified in simulation as thoroughly as the function.

The tools are **Synopsys** (TestMAX for ATPG, DFTMAX for compression, and the SLM suite for monitors and analytics) and **Siemens EDA** (Tessent for ATPG, TestKompress, MBIST and IJTAG), with **proteanTecs** supplying on-die monitor IP.

## Multi-Site Testing and the Economics of Tester Time

A tester costs a fixed amount per hour whether it tests one die or sixteen, so the industry's main lever on test cost is **multi-site testing**: contacting and testing several dies on one touchdown. Each site needs its own copy of the channels, supplies and instruments it uses (**per-site resources**), so site count is limited by tester resources, probe card complexity, and die size. Memory, with a few dozen pins per small die, goes to the extreme: full-wafer-contact cards touch every die on a 300 mm DRAM or NAND flash (the storage-memory type, Module 15) wafer at once, hundreds to more than a thousand sites, and the wafer is tested in one or a handful of touchdowns. Small logic and mixed-signal dies run 8 to 64 sites. A reticle-sized GPU with thousands of pads and a multi-hundred-amp power budget is tested one or two sites at a time; the tester has no room for more GPU-class supplies and serial-link instruments, and the wafer only carries ~60 to 70 candidate dies anyway. **Multi-site efficiency** is how close N sites come to N× throughput; it is below 100 percent because some instruments are shared and serve the sites one at a time.

The cost of a tester-second is commonly quoted at ~$0.01 to 0.05, with the high end for a fully loaded high-end SoC cell. It is derived as follows.

> **Worked example: cost per die at sort.** Take a GPU sort cell: a $6.0 million ATE configuration, a $1.0 million prober, and $0.5 million per year for probe cards (two $400k cards in rotation, each replaced roughly every two years, so $400k per year, plus ~$100k per year of probe-head refurbishment, ≈ $0.5M per year). Depreciate the capital over 5 years: ($7.0M / 5) = $1.4M per year. Add probe cards ($0.5M), maintenance and service contracts (~8 percent of capital, $0.56M), floor space, power, and labor (~$0.4M): total ~$2.9M per year. A well-run cell is in productive test about 85 percent of the 8,760 hours in a year (the rest is wafer exchange, cleaning, calibration, and program changes), so 7,450 productive hours. Cost per hour is $2.9M / 7,450 = ~$390 per hour, or ~$0.108 per second; a leaner cell with a $3M tester lands nearer $0.05 per second. Now apply it to a GPU. A single-site sort program with continuity, scan with compression, MBIST with repair, loopback, and e-fuse programming, executed at hot and at room temperature in two insertions, might run 240 s per die total. Cost: 240 s × $0.108 = ~$26 per die, before final test, burn-in, and SLT. Against a die whose manufacturing cost is on the order of $300 to 400 (a ~$18,000 **4N-class** wafer, TSMC's NVIDIA-tuned 5 nm-class process, yielding roughly 50 harvested good dies of ~800 mm²; Module 13's harvest example gives 47 to 49, and Module 17's un-harvested count is lower), sort is ~7 percent of die cost, and the whole test chain for a GPU module can approach $100 or more per unit. Now compare a 100 mm² mobile SoC tested 16 sites at once for 25 s per touchdown on a $0.05 per second cell: 25 s × $0.05 / 16 = $0.078 per die (a little more in practice, since multi-site efficiency is ~90 to 95 percent rather than 100 percent because of serialized instrument use). That die costs ~$15 to make, so sort is ~0.5 percent. Multi-site is why small-die test is cheap and single-site is why GPU test is not.

The wafer-level throughput implication is worth stating: at 65 candidate dies and 240 s each, a GPU wafer occupies a sort cell for over 4 hours. A product line consuming 100,000 wafers a year needs on the order of 400,000 to 450,000 cell-hours, or 55 to 60 sort cells running continuously, before final test and SLT capacity is counted. Test capacity at KYEC and TSMC was reported as one of the constraints on NVIDIA's Blackwell ramp, and it is why KYEC's 2025 capital expenditure set a record, almost entirely for testers and probers (the figures are under Who Does the Testing).

> **Why it matters downstream.** The 58-cell calculation is exactly how a test house sizes its capital spending. Multiply cells by ~$7 million per cell and the sort capacity for one GPU product line is ~$400 million of equipment, before final test and SLT; a test house that wins that business must order testers a year ahead of the wafers. That is the arithmetic behind KYEC's record capex and behind Advantest's share gains in the AI cycle, both described later in this module.

Test-time reduction is therefore a discipline of its own: ordering tests to fail early, removing tests that yield learning shows never fail alone, maximizing scan shift frequency, and pushing coverage into BIST engines that run inside the die while the tester waits.

## Coverage, Escapes, and DPPM

Every test program is a compromise between test time and **test escapes**: defective dies that pass. The metric is **DPPM** (defective parts per million shipped), and the customer's specification (~500 to a few thousand DPPM for consumer, below 10 DPPM targeted for automotive) sets the coverage the program must achieve. The classic relationship is the **Williams-Brown model**, which relates defect level DL (fraction of shipped parts that are defective) to yield Y and fault coverage T. The intuition first: each modelled fault the patterns never look at is a hiding place, and a defect that lands in one of them escapes.

DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T)

T is the fraction of modelled faults the patterns would catch; if the die's defect behaves like one of the modelled faults chosen at random, the chance the patterns never look at it is 1 − T. The exact form comes from three lines. Assume the die has n possible faults, each present independently with probability p; then the true yield, the chance that none of them is present, is Y = (1 − p)^n. The patterns look at nT of those faults, and the die passes if none of the nT covered faults is present, which has probability (1 − p)^(nT) = Y^T. The defect level is the fraction of passing dies that are actually bad, DL = 1 − P(good) / P(pass) = 1 − Y / Y^T = 1 − Y^(1 − T). A naive single-defect argument would give roughly (1 − Y)(1 − T) instead; at Y = 0.70 and T = 0.99 that is 3,000 DPPM against the model's 3,560, close at high yield and further apart as yield falls. The model is the form the industry quotes, and it is the one to reuse: the only inputs are the sort yield and the pattern set's fault coverage, both of which every test engineer has.

> **Worked example: DPPM versus coverage.** A large die yields Y = 0.70 at sort. With stuck-at coverage T = 0.99: DL = 1 − 0.70^0.01 = 1 − exp(0.01 × ln 0.70) = 1 − exp(−0.003567) = 0.00356, or ~3,560 DPPM. Raise coverage to T = 0.999: DL = 1 − 0.70^0.001 = 0.000357, or ~357 DPPM. At T = 0.9999, ~36 DPPM. Each additional "nine" of coverage cuts escapes tenfold, and note that a die that yields worse (Y = 0.5) at the same coverage escapes more (1 − 0.5^0.01 = 6,900 DPPM), which is why low-yield early production ships with more escapes and why final test and SLT exist to mop them up. The model overstates the effect of the last nines (real defects are not equally likely and several fault models overlap), but the direction is right and the arithmetic is what drives the demand for at-speed and cell-aware patterns on top of stuck-at.

Escapes are not uniformly distributed, and that fact is the basis for the statistical methods described under adaptive test.

## Binning at Sort

The output of sort is not just pass/fail. Each die is assigned a **hard bin** (a small number of categories: pass, fail continuity, fail scan, fail MBIST, and so on) and a **soft bin** (a fine-grained failure or grade code, hundreds of them, used for yield analysis). Passing dies are further graded.

**Speed binning.** The test program runs a **V_min search** (lower the supply voltage in steps while running at-speed scan or a functional loop until the die fails; the last passing voltage is V_min) and an **F_max search** (raise frequency at fixed voltage), sometimes visualized as a **shmoo plot** (a two-dimensional pass/fail map over the voltage-frequency plane). Ring-oscillator frequencies from the on-die monitors are read as a fast proxy. Dies are graded into bins that correspond to product **SKUs** (stock-keeping units: distinct sellable product variants) or to the voltage-frequency curves that firmware will later program; a die with a lower V_min at the target frequency will run cooler at the same performance, and in a data-center GPU, where power is the constraint, the better bins go to the flagship SKU.

> **Worked example: the V_min search and what it buys (illustrative).** A V_min search that steps the core supply down in 20 steps of 25 mV, running a ~0.2 s at-speed loop at each step, takes 20 × 0.2 s ≈ 4 s per temperature, which is where a good part of the ~20 percent "search" line in the 240 s budget goes. Now compare two dies that both pass at the target clock: one at V_min = 0.75 V, the other at 0.72 V, 30 mV lower. Dynamic power scales with the square of the supply voltage, P ∝ V², so at the same clock the second die burns (0.72 / 0.75)² ≈ 0.92, or ~8 percent less dynamic power. In a data-center product whose selling point is performance per watt inside a fixed thermal envelope, that 30 mV is the difference between the flagship bin and the one below it.

**Partial-good harvesting.** A reticle-sized die with ~140 or more **streaming multiprocessors (SMs)**, the GPU's repeated compute block, can lose sellable yield if every unit must be functional; the probability depends on the defect distribution and design, so the design includes unit-level redundancy: the GH100 die (the die inside NVIDIA's 2022 H100 GPU, the generation before Blackwell) has 144 SMs physically and the H100 SXM product (the version sold as a bare module that plugs into a server baseboard, rather than as a plug-in card) ships with 132 enabled. Sort determines exactly which SMs, **cache slices** (banks of the shared L2 cache), and **memory channels** (independent paths to HBM) are defective, and e-fuses record the disable map, so a die with a bad SM becomes a good product rather than scrap. Module 13's harvest worked example does the yield arithmetic (effective yield near 78 percent instead of ~45 percent un-harvested); what sort decides is which specific SMs to disable on each die, and that decision is written to the e-fuses before the die leaves the wafer. This can be a major lever on effective yield for large dies, and it is why the yield that matters for a GPU is not "fraction of perfect dies" but "fraction of dies meeting the harvest floor."

**Wafer maps and traceability.** Decades ago, failed dies were physically inked so the die-attach operator could skip them. Today the result is an **electronic wafer map** (SEMI E142 XML, or the older text-based SINF and vendor formats) that travels with the wafer to the assembly house, whose pick-and-place equipment picks only the bins it is told to, with speed bin and harvest configuration encoded so dies for different SKUs can be separated. The ECID burned into e-fuses at sort ties every die to its lot, wafer, and X-Y position for life; every later insertion reads it and appends results, so a field return traces back to the sort datalog, the WAT data for its wafer, and the fab tool history for its lot.

> **What can go wrong.** *Bin drift between sort and final test*: a die binned fast at sort can bin slower in the package, because the package changes the thermal path and final test runs the I/O at full rate; a sort bin that does not predict the final-test bin is corrected with a **guard-band**, a deliberate margin added to the sort limit, at the price of some good dies binned down. *E-fuse mis-programming*: a fuse that did not blow cleanly reads back correctly at sort and wrong a month later; the read-back step and a margin-read mode exist to catch marginal fuses. *ECID read-back failure*: a die whose ID cannot be read has no traceability and is scrapped even if it passes everything else. *Harvest floor mistakes*: disabling a working SM instead of the defective one, or disabling too few, ships a part that fails in the field; the disable map is verified by re-running the affected tests after the fuses are programmed.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Bin decides SKU, and SKU decides price: a die that passes at a lower V_min or a higher F_max goes into a product that sells for more. Module 13 showed that a 1 percent shift in ring-oscillator speed moves a measurable fraction of dies across a bin boundary, so a process drift the fab would call small shows up on the finance side as a change in product mix. Sort is where that translation happens.

## Known-Good-Die

A **known-good-die (KGD)** is a bare die tested to the same confidence level as a packaged, final-tested part. The concept dates to the 1990s multichip-module era, but chiplets and HBM have made it the central economic problem of advanced packaging. A Blackwell-class package carries two ~800 mm² GPU dies and eight HBM3E stacks of 8 to 12 DRAM dies plus a base die each, on a **CoWoS-L** interposer (the CoWoS variant that uses small silicon bridge chiplets embedded in an organic interposer, Module 17): roughly a hundred dies and thousands of dollars of HBM, interposer, and substrate are committed at assembly, and if any one die is bad beyond what redundancy can repair, the package is scrapped, because there is no rework for a **thermocompression**-bonded stack (dies pressed together hot so their solder caps fuse) or a **hybrid-bonded** one (copper pads pressed together with no solder at all).

> **Worked example: escapes in a multi-die stack.** Suppose each DRAM die in an HBM stack is shipped at 500 DPPM after the memory maker's wafer test and repair, and each GPU die at 300 DPPM after sort. A package with 8 stacks × 12 DRAM dies + 8 base dies + 2 GPUs = 106 dies. The probability the package contains no escaped die is (1 − 0.0005)^104 × (1 − 0.0003)^2 ≈ 0.949 × 0.9994 ≈ 0.948. So ~5 percent of packages, each carrying more than $10,000 of good parts, would fail at package test from escapes alone, before any assembly-induced defects. Counting the 96 DRAM dies alone, 1 − 0.9995^96 = 4.7 percent. Push the DRAM escape rate to 50 DPPM and the loss falls to ~0.5 percent. That difference is worth tens of millions of dollars a year on a high-volume product, and it is why HBM vendors run extended tests on every die, why NVIDIA specifies test coverage to its memory suppliers, and why the HBM stack is tested again as a **known-good-stacked-die (KGSD)** (Module 18 calls it a known-good stack, KGS) after stacking and before it is shipped to TSMC.

Achieving KGD at wafer level runs into the limits described earlier: probe cards cannot exercise high-speed I/O at full rate, cannot deliver full-load power without large voltage drops, and cannot always apply final-test thermal conditions. So KGD is approached by moving coverage into the die (BIST, loopback, wrappers), testing at multiple temperatures at sort, running voltage stress at sort where possible, and statistically screening the residual (see adaptive test). For HBM, the IEEE 1500 direct-access port and the base die's own test engines let the full stack's arrays be tested through a few hundred pins, with repair applied after stacking.

| What KGD requires | What wafer sort can do | Residual gap | Where the gap is closed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every logic net and memory cell exercised | Scan at 99 percent-plus stuck-at, MBIST with repair | Untestable logic, the last few percent of transition coverage | Final test, SLT, and PAT outlier screening |
| High-speed I/O at full rate | Loopback through the on-die eye monitor at reduced rate | The link through real package pins at 100+ Gb/s | Final test in a socket; CoW-stage test for the HBM interface |
| Full-load power and thermal conditions | A fraction of TDP through the probe card, subsets of SMs at a time | Droop and hot spots at full load | Final test, burn-in, SLT |
| Latent (infant-mortality) weaknesses screened | Voltage stress for seconds at sort | Defects that need hours of heat and voltage to surface | Burn-in (sampled or full, by product class) |

The most developed KGD recipe in the industry is the one HBM makers run, because a stack commits a dozen dies at once. In outline (Module 15 covers the stacking process and Module 18 the base-die test architecture in detail):

1. **DRAM wafer sort, front side.** Each DRAM die is probed on its bond pads before thinning, on a memory tester with a full-wafer-contact card: full-array March patterns at hot and cold, retention screens (every cell must hold its charge for the refresh interval), and row and column redundancy repair programmed into antifuses (defined under the test program). Module 18's repair worked example shows why almost every die is repairable and what the unrepairable remainder is.
2. **TSV reveal and second probe.** After the wafer is thinned to ~30 µm on a glass carrier and the TSV ends are exposed (Module 15), the die is probed again from its microbump side with the low-force bump probes of the Microbump Problem section: TSV **daisy chains** (test structures that thread a string of TSVs in series so that one open shows as an open chain) and a shortened array test confirm the die survived thinning.
3. **Stacking.** Known-good DRAM dies are thermocompression bonded onto the base die, 8 to 16 high, and the stack is molded. From this point no DRAM die can be reached by a probe.
4. **Stack test through the base die (the KGSD test proper).** The base die's IEEE 1500 wrapper and **direct-access (DA) port** take over: the DA port exposes a reduced-width test interface on a few hundred of the base die's microbumps, which a memory tester contacts with a conventional probe card on the base-die wafer, or through a socket after the stacks are cut apart. The base die's own MBIST engines run March patterns over every DRAM die's arrays while the 1500 wrapper selects which die in the stack is under test, reads results and burns fuses. Three repairs are available after stacking: **TSV repair**, in which a TSV found open or resistive is swapped for a spare through a multiplexer network; **lane repair**, in which a failing data lane of the wide interface is remapped to a spare lane; and **post-package repair**, in which spare rows held in reserve absorb cells that failed since wafer sort (a cell disturbed by thinning, TSV stress or bonding heat). The wide interface itself is exercised only at reduced rate through the DA port; its full-speed behaviour cannot be seen until the stack is on an interposer.
5. **Stack burn-in and re-test.** The stack runs hot at elevated voltage for a period of the order of a day (~) to surface infant-mortality failures, then repeats the stack test; it ships as a KGSD with its identity and test record.
6. **CoW-stage test at the packaging house.** After the stack is bonded to the interposer beside the GPU, the 1,024- or 2,048-bit interface runs at speed for the first time, driven by the GPU's own HBM PHY, before the interposer is committed to a substrate and lid.

> **What can go wrong.** *TSV opens after stacking*: a TSV that did not bond, or a microbump cracked by the stack's warpage during bonding, appears at stack test as an open; TSV repair absorbs a few per group, beyond which the stack is scrapped with every good die in it. *Repair budget exhausted*: a die that consumed most of its spares at wafer sort has little left for post-package repair, which is why the fraction of spares a die used is part of the KGD decision, not just whether it passed. *Interface escapes*: a lane that passes at the DA-port rate and fails at 8+ Gb/s is first seen at CoW-stage test, on an assembly that cannot be reworked, and who pays for that package is a standing negotiation between memory vendor, foundry and GPU vendor (Module 18). *Confidence mismatch across suppliers*: the worked example above shows that 500 DPPM per DRAM die costs ~5 percent of packages, so a GPU vendor cannot accept a memory supplier's KGD claim on trust; each supplier's stacks are qualified over months against the GPU vendor's own coverage and DPPM requirements, and a supplier whose 12-high stacks miss them is not designed in.

Who runs it: the HBM makers (SK hynix, Samsung and Micron) are **integrated device manufacturers (IDMs)**, companies that design and make their own chips, and they perform every step through stack burn-in on their own floors, on memory-class testers with high-parallelism handlers (Advantest's memory platforms are the ones named in trade descriptions; vendors do not publish their recipes, and the pattern times and burn-in durations quoted in trade and conference descriptions are order-of-magnitude figures). NVIDIA specifies the coverage and DPPM its suppliers must meet and qualifies each vendor's stacks, TSMC runs the CoW-stage test inside its CoWoS line, and for the GPU die itself NVIDIA writes the sort program and KYEC or TSMC's back-end fabs execute it. The stack test is reported at roughly 5 to 10 percent of stack cost (Module 15), which is expensive for a memory part and cheap against the package it protects.

## Test at Temperature: The Hot and Cold Split

Why test at more than one temperature at all? Because defects and marginalities move in opposite directions with temperature: some circuits fail only when hot, others only when cold, and a single room-temperature pass proves neither. Every extra temperature is an extra insertion or at least an extra soak, so the hot/cold decision is a direct cost driver and is made product by product.

> **Intuition: a crowded corridor.** Think of the electrons in a transistor channel as people crossing a corridor full of other people who are fidgeting in place. Heat makes the crowd fidget harder (the silicon atoms vibrate more), so the crossers are jostled more often and get through more slowly: that is why a hot transistor carries less current and a hot chip runs slower. Cold calms the crowd, and everything speeds up, which sounds like good news until a signal arrives at a flip-flop before the flip-flop has finished with the previous one. Hot finds paths that are too slow; cold finds paths that are too fast.

The mechanism has three parts. First, heat slows transistors: at higher temperature the silicon lattice vibrates more, those vibrations scatter the carriers, and carrier **mobility** (how fast electrons and holes move per unit of electric field) falls, so each transistor carries less current and every logic path takes longer. A path that just meets its timing at 25 °C can miss it at 125 °C, which is why hot test catches timing-marginal paths. Second, leakage is thermally activated and grows roughly exponentially: the rule of thumb for subthreshold leakage (the I_off of the WAT section) is of order a doubling for every ~10 to 15 °C, so a die's idle current at 125 °C is many times its value at 25 °C (total die leakage rises less steeply than the rule implies because gate leakage is nearly temperature independent), and a bridging defect that added a detectable delta at room temperature is a smaller fraction of a much larger background when hot, while thermally activated bridges that are open at room temperature conduct when hot. Third, copper resistance rises by ~0.4 percent per kelvin, so the wires slow too. The result is that hot (85 to 125 °C at the chuck) catches timing-marginal paths, IDDQ outliers, and thermally activated bridging.

Cold (−40 °C, or 0 °C for many commercial products) speeds up transistors and exposes **hold-time violations**: a hold time is the requirement that a signal not change too soon after the clock edge, and when transistors speed up in the cold, data can race through a short path and arrive before the receiving flip-flop has finished capturing the previous value. Cold also exposes certain analog and I/O failures, since **bias circuits** (the small circuits that set the steady currents and voltages an analog block needs in order to operate) and reference voltages drift with temperature. There is an honest caveat: on advanced nodes at low supply voltage, transistors can get faster rather than slower when hot, because the threshold voltage falls with temperature and, near threshold, that matters more than the mobility loss. This **temperature inversion** means cold is not always the slow corner and hot is not always the fast one (a corner here is one extreme of the temperature and voltage range the part must work over, by analogy with the process corners defined under the test program); the test engineer has to characterise which corner is worst for each path class at each supply voltage rather than assume it.

The machine is the thermal chuck already described under the prober; what this section adds is the recipe. Ramp and soak take minutes per setpoint, and the program reads the on-die temperature sensors to record the **junction temperature** (the temperature of the transistors themselves) the die actually reached, which under a heavy pattern can sit tens of degrees above the chuck. The recipe by product class: automotive parts are tested at three temperatures at final test and often two at sort; consumer SoCs typically get one hot sort; data-center GPUs are sorted hot, with cold characterization on samples. On a mature product a standard cost reduction is to prove statistically, over many lots, that the cold test never uniquely catches a failure that the hot test and final test miss, and then remove it from the production flow while keeping it on a sample.

> **Worked example: what 100 K does to a speed bin (illustrative).** Suppose a die's ring oscillator reads ~10 percent slower at 125 °C than at 25 °C at nominal supply voltage, a typical order of magnitude for an advanced node at full voltage. If the same die's at-speed scan passes up to ~2.0 GHz at 25 °C, it will pass only to ~1.8 GHz at 125 °C. With a bin boundary at 1.9 GHz, the die is in the upper bin at room temperature and the lower bin hot. Because the product will run hot, the hot number is the one that counts, and a program that binned at room temperature would over-grade the die. Under temperature inversion at a low-voltage operating point the sign can flip, which is why the search is run at the temperature and voltage the product will actually use.

> **What can go wrong.** (Frost and alignment drift after a temperature change are covered in the prober section; the failures below are the ones the temperature recipe itself introduces.) *Insufficient soak*: a wafer or card tested before it reaches thermal equilibrium is still drifting, so the alignment walks off during the wafer and the speed bins are wrong by whatever the temperature error was. *Chuck-versus-junction offset*: the chuck reads 125 °C but a GPU under a heavy pattern is at 150 °C; without the on-die sensor the program records the wrong temperature and mis-bins the die. *The wrong worst corner*: a flow built on the assumption that hot is slow misses a path class that, under temperature inversion at the product's low-voltage operating point, is slowest cold; the symptom is a field or SLT failure population that the hot-only sort never saw, and the fix is characterising each path class at each voltage before deciding which temperatures production needs. *Cold test removed too early*: the statistical case for dropping the cold insertion is only as good as the lots it was built on, so a process shift after the cold test was removed can reintroduce cold-only failures unseen; the sample cold test that is kept is what catches it.

Thermal chucks are integrated by the prober makers, TEL and Accretech, and third-party thermal-chuck specialists (ERS electronic of Germany is one, among others) also supply chuck systems to prober and engineering-station builders.

## Adaptive Test and Data Analytics

Fixed test limits, applied identically to every die, are inefficient: a limit tight enough to catch subtle defects rejects good dies at the process corners, and a limit loose enough to pass the corners lets defective dies through. The remedy is to compare each die not against a fixed number but against its neighbors.

**Part average testing (PAT)**, formalized in the Automotive Electronics Council's AEC-Q001, computes each parametric test's distribution across a population (a lot in static PAT; the wafer or a moving window in **dynamic PAT (DPAT)**) and sets limits at the mean ± 6σ or a **robust** median-based equivalent, computed from the median and quartiles so that a few wild values do not widen the limits. (σ is the standard deviation, the typical spread of the distribution; ±6σ is so far out that a good die almost never lands there.) A die whose IDDQ, V_min, or ring-oscillator frequency is within datasheet limits but far outside its wafer's own distribution is rejected as an **outlier**, on the principle that a die unlike its neighbors probably contains a defect the functional tests did not directly detect. **Nearest-neighbor residual (NNR)** methods model each parameter as a smooth function of wafer position and flag dies with a large **residual**, the difference between a die's value and what its position on the wafer predicts, removing the radial variation that would otherwise widen DPAT limits. **Good-die-in-bad-cluster (GDBC)** rules reject a passing die surrounded by failures, since defects cluster spatially (Module 13's negative-binomial model).

> **Worked example: DPAT on IDDQ (illustrative).** On one wafer, the passing dies' IDDQ has a median of 1.20 A and a robust σ of 0.08 A. The DPAT window is 1.20 ± 6 × 0.08 = 0.72 to 1.68 A. A die that reads 1.9 A passes the datasheet limit of 2.5 A comfortably, but it sits (1.9 − 1.2) / 0.08 ≈ 9σ above its neighbours, and DPAT rejects it. The bet is that a die drawing 60 percent more idle current than every die around it, made from the same silicon on the same day, contains a bridging defect that scan and MBIST did not happen to observe. On a data-center or automotive product that bet is worth a fraction of a percent of yield.

These methods embody the automotive **zero-defect** mindset: accept some yield loss (typically 0.5 to 2 percent of good dies) for a large reduction in DPPM, with the goal of no field failures rather than a tolerable rate. The same techniques have moved into data-center silicon, where a field failure in a GPU cluster justifies the yield hit. **Adaptive test** generalizes this to the flow itself: based on results so far, a wafer well within distribution skips a redundant test, a die marginal on V_min gets an extended list, and a lot flagged by the fab for an **excursion** (a lot that left its normal process window) gets 100 percent cold test where the baseline flow samples it.

> **What can go wrong.** *Edge dies over-rejected*: many parameters vary smoothly from wafer centre to edge, so a wafer-wide DPAT limit rejects good edge dies that are merely at the end of the trend; NNR exists to model the trend out first. *Small populations*: a lot of a few dozen dies gives a σ that jumps from lot to lot, so limits are unstable; the fix is a minimum sample size or a rolling window across lots. *Bimodal wafers*: a wafer with two populations (say, dies at two positions within the scanner's exposure field that differ systematically) has a wide σ that hides a real shift in one of them; the analysis has to be done per population. *Yield loss creep*: each new outlier rule takes a little more yield, and without a budget the total drifts above the 0.5 to 2 percent the product can afford; every rule is tracked for the escapes it actually caught at final test.

## Test Data: STDF and the Loop Back to the Fab

Every test on every die produces a record, and the industry-standard container is the **Standard Test Data Format (STDF)**, a binary format originated by Teradyne in the 1980s and now at version 4. An STDF file carries lot and wafer identification, the test program name and limits, and per-die per-test results, at a volume of megabytes to gigabytes per wafer for a rich parametric program. Downstream software (PDF Solutions Exensio, Synopsys Silicon.da and the broader SLM suite, yieldWerx, Optimal+ now within NI/Emerson, Advantest's ACS platform, and in-house systems at every large fabless company) ingests STDF from all test insertions, joins it by ECID, and produces the analyses that drive yield learning:

- Wafer maps of every parameter, revealing spatial signatures: a radial gradient (deposition uniformity), a scratch-shaped line (handling), a repeating **reticle-shot** pattern (the reticle shot is the rectangle exposed at one time by the scanner and repeated across the wafer; a defect at the same place in every shot points at the mask or the lithography), an edge ring (the **edge bead**, the thick rim of resist at the wafer edge, or the edge roll-off of chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP), Module 12).
- Correlation of sort bins against WAT parameters and fab tool history, so a step in the speed-bin distribution can be traced to the exposure tool or etch chamber that ran the lot.
- Failure-mode **Pareto charts** (bar charts of failure causes sorted by frequency, so the biggest cause is attacked first) by soft bin, feeding the fab (systematic defects) and the design team (coverage holes, marginal circuits).
- Scan diagnosis: the tester's fail log is fed back into the ATPG tool, which computes the most likely failing net; aggregated over thousands of dies, this **volume diagnosis** pinpoints systematic defects (a specific via layer, a specific standard cell) that inline inspection could not see, confirmed by **FIB** cross-section (a **focused ion beam** mills a trench through the suspect spot so the defect can be seen edge-on in an electron microscope).

> **Worked example: from a fail log to a failing net (illustrative).** What the tester records for a scan failure is small: for each mismatch, the pattern number, the scan chain, and the position in that chain, which together name one scan cell that captured the wrong value. Suppose die (wafer 7, X = 12, Y = 5) fails patterns 1,203, 4,417 and 8,809, each time at chain 17, cells 1,240 and 1,241, and passes the other 29,997 patterns. The ATPG tool can simulate, for any modelled fault, which cells would capture a wrong value under every pattern (a precomputed table of these is a **fault dictionary**). Diagnosis runs that simulation backwards. It shortlists the faults whose **fan-out cone** (the set of flip-flops a net can reach through the logic) includes cells 1,240 and 1,241 of chain 17, simulates each candidate against the three failing patterns and a sample of passing ones, and scores it: a candidate that explains all three fails and predicts no fail on any passing pattern scores highest. Here the winner is a stuck-at-0 on one net, say the output of a NAND3 cell in streaming multiprocessor 12, which those three patterns happen to drive to 1 and observe, and which no other pattern observes. That is one die and a suspect a few micrometres across. Volume diagnosis repeats it over every failing die in the lot and bins the suspects not by net name (every die fails on a different net) but by physical feature: the layer of the suspect's via, the standard cell type, the metal track it runs on. If 3 percent of ten thousand diagnosed failures land on a metal-5-to-metal-4 via inside one cell type, where random defects would spread them evenly over dozens of layers and hundreds of cell types, that is a systematic defect. A FIB cut on three of those dies shows the same voided via, the fab traces the cell's via geometry and the etch chamber that ran the lot, and the fix is a layout rule or a chamber recipe. Inline inspection never saw it because the void is under a micrometre and buried under five more metal layers.

This loop is what turns a 30 percent yield ramp into 85 percent mature yield over the first year of a node, and test data is its richest single source.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Inline inspection (Module 13) sees a wafer a few times during its months in the fab and only where it is told to look; sort measures every transistor path of every die, once, at the end. A systematic defect that inline inspection missed shows up in volume diagnosis as the same net failing on thousands of dies, and that pointer, joined to the tool history, is what lets the fab fix a specific chamber instead of hunting. The 30 to 85 percent ramp is the money: on a $18,000 wafer with ~65 candidates, every percentage point of yield is worth about two-thirds of a die per wafer, and a node that ramps a quarter faster is worth billions to the foundry and its lead customer.

## GPU-Specific Test Challenges

A data-center GPU compounds every difficulty in this module.

**Power.** A device in the roughly 1,000 W class and rising (B300-class parts, NVIDIA's 2025 Blackwell Ultra generation, are quoted at ~1,400 W as of 2025) cannot run at full load at sort; the probe card's power path and the chuck's thermal capacity do not allow it. Sort is designed around a fraction of **TDP** (thermal design power, the sustained power the cooling must remove) by testing subsets of SMs at a time, using on-die **power gating** (switches that cut the supply to idle blocks), and keeping at-speed windows short. The transient when a scan pattern begins toggling can be hundreds of amps in nanoseconds; on-card and on-die decoupling (**MIM** capacitors, metal-insulator-metal capacitors built in the wiring layers, and **deep-trench** capacitors etched into the silicon or interposer) must supply it because the DPS cannot respond fast enough, and a droop below V_min causes a false fail that on-die droop monitors help diagnose.

> **Worked example: why the supply cannot keep up (order of magnitude).** A current step of 200 A in 10 ns is di/dt = 2 × 10¹⁰ A/s. Through even ~50 pH (picohenries, 5 × 10⁻¹¹ H) of effective loop inductance between the nearest capacitor and the die, V = L·di/dt = 5 × 10⁻¹¹ H × 2 × 10¹⁰ A/s = 1 V, more than the whole 0.75 V rail. That 50 pH is far smaller than the ~0.5 to 2 nH of a single probe because the path is not one probe: ~1,000 power probes and as many ground probes act in parallel (1 nH / 1,000 = 1 pH each way), and the on-card and on-die capacitors sit closer to the die than the probes do; 50 pH is an order-of-magnitude figure for the whole loop, not a measured value, and the point is that even a loop that small is too much. Real steps are spread over more nanoseconds and the loop is broken into stages, but the conclusion holds: the first nanoseconds of any current step must come from capacitors on the die and on the card, sized so that their charge covers the step until the supply, which responds in microseconds, catches up. The tester's job is only to recover within those microseconds.

**Thermal.** The die heats locally where the pattern is active; a hot spot 30 °C above the chuck changes local timing and leakage, so programs distribute activity, include cool-down pauses, and read on-die temperature sensors to confirm the intended test temperature was actually achieved.

**HBM interface.** The GPU's HBM PHYs face the interposer through thousands of microbumps per stack. At sort they are tested through loopback and the IEEE 1500 wrapper, with the microbump array probed at reduced pin count or not at all; the interface is fully tested only after chip-on-wafer assembly, which is why the CoW-stage test is a required insertion and why a failing HBM interface there scraps a package's worth of parts.

**Scale and sensors.** Hundreds of scan chains, thousands of memory instances, dozens of PLLs and PHYs, and a redundancy map with hundreds of entries produce a test program with tens of thousands of tests and a development effort measured in engineer-years. On-die ring oscillators, droop monitors, and temperature sensors are read at every insertion and serve as the primary inputs to PAT and lifetime analytics; a die whose sensors drift between sort and final test is a candidate escape.

> **What can go wrong.** *False V_min fails from droop*: the V_min search reports a higher V_min than the die deserves because the rail sagged at the start of the loop, and the die is binned down; droop monitors distinguish a real V_min from a delivery artefact. *Hot-spot mis-binning*: an SM cluster that ran 30 °C hotter than the chuck during its at-speed test fails a timing check it would pass at the intended temperature, so the program spreads activity and re-tests suspect blocks after a pause. *Sensor drift as an escape flag*: a die whose ring oscillators read 2 percent slower at final test than at sort, with no temperature explanation, is treated as a candidate for a latent defect and routed to extended test rather than shipped.

## Who Does the Testing

Three kinds of organizations run wafer sort.

**Foundries.** TSMC's back-end organization (its advanced packaging and testing business, which operates a series of Advanced Backend Fabs in Taiwan) performs WAT on every wafer and does sort and CoW-stage test for its integrated CoWoS and SoIC customers, since those flows require test between assembly steps that happen inside TSMC. Samsung and Intel Foundry have equivalent organizations.

**OSATs and pure-play test houses.** An **outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT)** company packages and tests chips for others; a **pure-play** test house does only test, no assembly. **ASE** (with SPIL), **Amkor**, **JCET**, and **Powertech (PTI)** all offer sort, but the largest volume of NVIDIA and other high-end sort in Taiwan goes to **King Yuan Electronics (KYEC)**, the world's largest pure-play test house and the second-largest test provider overall after ASE. KYEC’s [2025 consolidated statements](https://www.kyec.com.tw/en/Ir/Quarterly) report NT$34.93 billion of revenue and NT$32.36 billion of cash acquisitions of property, plant and equipment (PP&E). These correspond roughly to US$1.1 billion and US$1.0 billion at an illustrative NT$32 per US dollar. PP&E cash spend includes the group’s facilities and equipment; it is not an equipment-only order budget. AI and **high-performance computing (HPC)** devices are important demand drivers. In July 2026 KYEC announced a US facility of up to US$1.4 billion to support American CoWoS capacity; location and timeline were undisclosed at the announcement. Other Taiwanese specialists include **Ardentec** (wafer probing, affiliated with the foundry UMC), **Sigurd**, **ChipMOS**, and **Chipbond**. Test is even more Taiwan-concentrated than assembly because it sits between TSMC's fabs and TSMC's CoWoS lines.

**Fabless companies' own test engineering.** NVIDIA, AMD, Apple, Qualcomm, and Broadcom do not own testers in volume, but they write and own the test programs, specify the probe cards, correlate results across sites, and negotiate test-time budgets with the OSAT. A GPU test program encodes the redundancy architecture and the SKU strategy and is treated as design IP. IDMs (SK hynix, Samsung, Micron, Intel) test in-house at wafer level, since memory repair and HBM stacking must be tightly coupled to manufacturing.

## The Equipment Market

The ATE market is a duopoly. **Advantest** (Japan) held ~56 percent of the SoC tester market in 2024 and, by its own reporting, ~66 percent in 2025 on V93000 EXA Scale wins in AI accelerators and HBM; **Teradyne** (US) held most of the remainder, ~35 percent in 2024 and ~25 to 30 percent in 2025, strongest in mobile SoCs and in memory with Magnum (as of 2025; analyst estimates of both shares differ by several points). Together they hold ~80 to 90 percent of total ATE revenue, which has run at roughly $6 to 9 billion per year through the AI upcycle (as of 2025). The rest is **Cohu** (US), **Chroma** (Taiwan), and Chinese entrants **Hangzhou Changchuan** and **Beijing Huafeng**, growing in domestic mature-node markets under export-control pressure. Probers are the TEL and Accretech duopoly described above. Probe cards are a market of roughly $3 billion per year as of ~2025, led by FormFactor (~$0.8 billion revenue, roughly a quarter to 30 percent share), with Technoprobe a close second and gaining at TSMC on 3 nm and 2 nm qualifications, MJC and JEM dominant in DRAM full-wafer cards, and MPI behind them; the five largest suppliers hold about three quarters of the market.

## Summary

- Test exists because defects are random and design verification cannot see them; the rule of ten says every stage a bad die slips through multiplies its cost, and for CoWoS the first step is far steeper.
- The flow is WAT (process, not product), wafer sort, dicing and packaging, final test, burn-in and SLT, with extra CoW-stage and HBM-stack insertions for a GPU; every insertion is a cost line and a fallout point.
- A sort cell is a prober (mechanics: chuck, ±1 µm positioning, −40 to +150 °C, hot alignment because silicon grows 78 µm across 300 mm for 100 K) plus an ATE (electronics), joined by a probe card.
- Overtravel compresses each probe spring to 2 to 8 gf and scrubs through the native aluminum oxide; 12,000 probes push ~350 N on a GPU card, 100,000 probes ~2 kN on a DRAM card.
- Probe cards come in cantilever, vertical (cobra) and MEMS forms; advanced MEMS cards cost $100k to $1 M+, need ~10 to 25 µm planarity, and are limited to ~40 µm pitch, nanohenries of inductance and hundreds of milliohms per probe, which is why full-rate SerDes is not tested at sort.
- An ATE is thousands of per-pin driver, comparator and PMU channels with tens-of-picosecond timing, plus multi-hundred-amp supplies with remote sensing; Advantest and Teradyne make essentially all of them.
- A sort program runs cheapest-first with stop-on-fail: continuity, leakage and delta-IDDQ, DC parametrics, scan/ATPG (99 percent-plus stuck-at, 90 percent-plus transition, 50 to 200× compression), MBIST with repair, functional, loopback, stress and e-fuse programming.
- None of that works without DFT: scan chains, compression, JTAG (1149.1), core wrappers (1500), IJTAG (1687), 3D access (1838), BIST and on-die monitors, at a few percent of die area.
- Tester time costs ~$0.01 to 0.10 per second; multi-site makes a mobile SoC's sort ~$0.08 per die while a single-site GPU sort is ~$26 per die, and a 100,000-wafer product needs ~58 sort cells.
- Escapes are inevitable: Williams-Brown DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T) gives ~3,560 DPPM at Y = 0.7, T = 0.99 and ~357 DPPM at T = 0.999; at 106 dies per Blackwell package, 500 DPPM per DRAM die costs ~5 percent of packages.
- HBM reaches known-good-stacked-die through a six-step flow: front-side DRAM sort with repair, a second probe after TSV reveal, stacking, a stack test through the base die's IEEE 1500 wrapper and direct-access port (with TSV, lane and post-package repair), stack burn-in, and a CoW-stage test where the wide interface first runs at speed.
- Sort outputs a hard and soft bin, a speed bin from V_min and F_max searches, a partial-good harvest map (144 SMs built, 132 enabled on H100 SXM) burned into e-fuses with the ECID, and an electronic wafer map (SEMI E142) plus an STDF datalog that follow the die for life.
- Temperature, PAT/DPAT outlier screening and adaptive test trade a little yield for large DPPM reductions; STDF data joined by ECID across insertions is the fab's richest yield-learning source; KYEC, TSMC's back-end fabs and the OSATs do the testing, on Advantest and Teradyne testers, TEL and Accretech probers, and FormFactor, Technoprobe, MJC and JEM cards.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Test just checks whether the chip works."** → Reality: test is a structured search for manufacturing defects using fault models (stuck-at, transition, cell-aware) that have nothing to do with the chip's function; functional vectors are a small and inefficient part of a modern program. A passing chip has been shown to be free of a specific list of modeled defects to a quantified coverage, nothing more.
- **"Wafer sort finds all the bad dies, so final test is a formality."** → Reality: probe-card parasitics, power delivery limits, and single-temperature testing leave real coverage gaps at sort; the Williams-Brown arithmetic guarantees escapes, and final test, burn-in, and SLT exist to catch them. Sort's job is to keep the escape rate low enough that downstream insertions are economical.
- **"Test cost is negligible."** → Reality: for a small die tested 16 sites in parallel it is under a percent of die cost, but for a single-site reticle-sized GPU tested at multiple temperatures it is several percent at sort alone and approaching $100 per unit across all insertions; test capacity was reported as one of the constraints on the Blackwell ramp.
- **"A tester is a fast computer that runs the chip."** → Reality: an ATE is a rack of precision instruments (drivers, comparators, PMUs, multi-hundred-amp supplies, serial-link generators) with picosecond timing that runs pre-computed vectors; it does not execute the chip's software, and at sort it cannot even run the chip at full power.
- **"Yield means the fraction of perfect dies."** → Reality: for large dies, most shipped products have disabled units. Partial-good harvesting via e-fuse configuration (144 SMs built, 132 enabled) is the primary yield lever, and sort is where the harvest decision is made.
- **"The probe card is a simple fixture."** → Reality: an advanced MEMS probe card is a $1 million, 12-week-lead-time custom precision instrument with tens of thousands of lithographically fabricated springs, a multilayer ceramic space transformer, and planarity specs of ~10 µm, with its own lifetime, cleaning schedule, and failure modes.
- **"Cold is always the slow, worst corner."** → Reality: at nominal voltage, cold speeds transistors up and hot slows them, but at the low supply voltages of advanced nodes the threshold voltage's fall with temperature can dominate and transistors get faster when hot (temperature inversion). Which corner is worst depends on the path and the operating voltage and has to be characterised, not assumed.
- **"IDDQ testing is obsolete."** → Reality: a fixed IDDQ limit is useless on a leaky advanced node, but delta-IDDQ between vector states and DPAT outlier screening against the wafer's own distribution still catch bridging defects that scan misses, and IDDQ remains a standard input to PAT.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Wafer sort consumes finished wafers from the BEOL (Module 12) together with the WAT data and inline inspection records from Module 13, and it is the point where Module 13's yield model becomes a measured number: sort yield per wafer, the failure Pareto, and the wafer maps that go back to the fab as the primary yield-learning feedback. Its outputs are an electronic wafer map with hard bins, speed bins, and harvest configurations for every die, an ECID and repair map burned into each die's e-fuses, and a datalog in STDF that follows the die for life. The wafer then ships (from TSMC's fab to TSMC's Advanced Backend Fab, or to KYEC and then to an OSAT) into Module 15's memory world in the case of HBM and into Modules 16 and 17 for dicing and packaging, where the pick-and-place equipment reads the map and picks only the dies sort declared good. Module 18 picks the story up at final test, burn-in, and system-level test, where the escapes that this module's arithmetic says must exist are hunted down in the package.
