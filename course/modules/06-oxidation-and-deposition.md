# Module 06: Thermal Oxidation and Thin-Film Deposition

A finished logic chip is a stack of a few hundred distinct thin films on a silicon wafer. Some are 1 nm thick (about four atoms) and must be exactly that thick on the top, both sides and the underside of a silicon ribbon only 10 nm wide. Some are 1 µm thick and must fill a trench 20 nm wide and 300 nm deep without leaving a bubble inside. Some are perfect single crystals deliberately squeezed; some are glass-like solids with no order at all. Some carry current as well as a copper wire; others must block current even when a volt is applied across a single nanometre. Those are different jobs, so making a film starts with choosing the electrical behaviour it must provide. This module is about how those films are put there.

Three things make it hard. Exactness: at 1–2 nm an error of a tenth of a nanometre is a 10% error, and the film must be that exact across a 300 mm wafer and across tens of thousands of wafers a month. Coverage: the films must coat vertical walls and undersides as evenly as flat tops, which nothing that simply falls from above can do. And heat: most of the chemistry here goes faster when hot, but a wafer arriving at a deposition step already carries earlier work that heat would ruin. The **thermal budget** is the allowable combination of temperature and time after a given point in the flow. Think of heating a dish whose ingredients cook at different speeds: a step that finishes one ingredient can overcook another. Here the “ingredients” are existing films, doped regions and strained crystals. The analogy explains why earlier work constrains later steps; the actual limit comes from how each material changes with temperature and time. Every hot step blurs the dopant atoms that define the transistors (Module 10) and relaxes the strain that speeds up their channels (**strain** here is a deliberate stretching or squeezing of the channel crystal that makes the charges carrying its current move faster; Sections 7 and 8), and once copper wiring exists nothing may exceed ~400 °C, so the temperature allowed for each new film falls step by step through the flow.

A fab has five ways to put a film on a wafer. **Thermal oxidation** grows silicon dioxide out of the wafer itself by heating it in oxygen or steam. **Epitaxy** grows new single crystal in register with the crystal beneath. **Chemical vapor deposition (CVD)** lets gases react on a hot surface and leave a solid behind. **Atomic layer deposition (ALD)** is CVD chopped into self-limiting steps that add a fraction of an atomic layer at a time. **Physical vapor deposition (PVD)**, also called sputtering, knocks atoms off a solid metal target with ions and lets them land on the wafer. That is also the order of falling temperature: oxidation and epitaxy near 1,000 °C, CVD from 900 °C down to 300 °C, ALD at 100–400 °C, PVD at room temperature.

By the end you will be able to explain why a thermal oxide grows into the wafer rather than on it, why ALD thickness is digital (you count the cycles), why sputtered metal cannot coat the bottom of a narrow hole, why a 2 nm-class chip needs on the order of 100 ALD steps, and who sells the machines that do each job.

## Before you start

- **Crystal orientation** (Modules 02, 03). A wafer is one single crystal of silicon; the labels (100), (110) and (111) name the crystal plane along which the surface was cut, and different planes expose different numbers of silicon atoms per cm², so they react at different rates. Standard wafers are cut on (100).
- **What a transistor is, minimally** (Modules 00, 11). A switch: a gate electrode sits on a thin insulator, the gate dielectric, above a strip of silicon called the channel, and voltage on the gate turns conduction in the channel on or off. The thinner the insulator, the firmer the gate's grip. Modern channels are vertical fins or stacks of thin ribbons with the gate wrapped around them on all sides.
- **Carriers, holes, mobility, NMOS and PMOS** (Module 11). Current in silicon is carried by **carriers** of two kinds: free electrons, and **holes**, which are missing electrons in the bond network that move through the crystal like positive charges. **Mobility** is how fast a carrier drifts per unit of electric field (in cm² per volt-second), so higher mobility means a faster transistor. An **NMOS** transistor conducts with electrons and turns on with a positive gate voltage; a **PMOS** transistor conducts with holes and turns on with a negative one. **CMOS** logic uses one of each in every gate, so every film step in this module has to serve both, and the two often want opposite things (opposite strain, different threshold voltages).
- **Pressure units.** 1 atmosphere = 760 torr; 1 mtorr = 0.001 torr. The lower the pressure, the farther a gas molecule flies between collisions (its mean free path): ~0.07 µm at 1 atmosphere, ~5 cm at 1 mtorr.
- **Arrhenius behaviour.** Most reaction rates scale as exp(−Ea/kT): Ea is the **activation energy**, the energy hill the reaction must climb; k is Boltzmann's constant, 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV per kelvin; T is the temperature in kelvin (°C + 273); kT is the typical thermal energy of a molecule (0.11 eV at 1,000 °C). For the 1–2 eV hills in this module a 50–100 °C rise roughly doubles the rate.
- **Diffusion (Fick's law).** Molecules drift from where they are concentrated to where they are scarce, at a rate proportional to the concentration difference; the diffusivity D sets how fast, and the distance spread in time t is ~√(Dt).
- **Plasma** (Module 09). A gas partly torn into ions, free electrons and reactive fragments (radicals) by a radio-frequency field. Ions pick up their energy in the sheath, a thin dark layer next to any surface held at negative voltage, so a plasma can bombard a surface while the gas stays cool.
- **Capacitance and dielectric constant.** Two conductors separated by an insulator form a capacitor with C = k·ε₀·A/t: A is the area, t the insulator thickness, ε₀ a constant of nature, and k the **dielectric constant**, a pure number saying how much more charge the insulator lets the capacitor hold than vacuum would. For silicon dioxide k = 3.9; "high-k" and "low-k" mean higher or lower than that.
- **Dopants, ion implantation and the thermal budget** (Module 10). Transistors are defined by dopant atoms (boron, phosphorus, arsenic) placed in the silicon, today almost always by **ion implantation**: the dopant is shot into the wafer as a beam of ions, which also damages the crystal, so a later heating step must repair it and put the dopants on lattice sites where they are electrically active. Every hot step after they are placed blurs them. Copper wiring, once present, must never see more than ~400 °C.
- **The fab context** (Module 05). A wafer goes through 1,000–1,500 process steps, travelling between tools in a sealed 25-wafer carrier called a FOUP; a "step" is one tool running one recipe; particles are the enemy.
- **Units in this module.** Resistivity in µΩ·cm (microohm-centimetres; copper is 1.7, titanium nitride ~100–200). Electric field in MV/cm (10 MV/cm is 1 V across 1 nm). Å (ångström) = 0.1 nm. at.% = atomic percent, the fraction of all atoms that are of a given element.

## 1. Thermal Oxidation: Growing SiO2 from the Wafer Itself

Silicon dioxide (SiO2, the same compound as quartz and window glass) does four jobs on a chip: it is the insulator between a transistor's gate electrode and its channel, thin enough for the gate's field to reach the channel yet leaking almost nothing; the insulator between neighbouring transistors; a mask that blocks dopants or etchants wherever it lies; and a skin that is grown and stripped to carry away a damaged surface layer. A film for those jobs can be deposited from gases (Section 4) or grown from the wafer's own silicon, and for the job that matters most, the gate insulator, growing wins for one reason: the reaction that makes the oxide happens *underneath* the oxide, at a silicon surface that has never seen air, water or a cleanroom. The boundary between silicon and its grown oxide is the cleanest electrical interface known in any material system. What makes the step hard today is that the oxides that matter are ~1 nm thick, controlled to a tenth of that, on surfaces facing every direction.

> **Intuition:** *The transistor from the film's point of view.* A modern transistor (Module 11) is a fin or, at the 2 nm-class nodes, a stack of three silicon ribbons ("nanosheets"), each ~10 nm thick, standing on the wafer. The gate wraps around each ribbon on all four sides: ~1 nm of silicon dioxide, then ~2 nm of a higher-k insulator, then several 1–3 nm metal layers, then a metal fill, all of it passing through the ~10 nm gap between ribbons. Every film in this module therefore has to coat tops, sidewalls, undersides and the inside of a slot equally; a film that lands only from above is useless for the gate.

### 1.1 The chemistry and the buried interface

Heat a silicon wafer to 800–1,200 °C in oxygen or steam and it converts its own surface to silicon dioxide:

- Dry oxidation: Si + O2 → SiO2
- Wet oxidation: Si + 2 H2O → SiO2 + 2 H2

The critical mechanistic fact is *where* the reaction happens. The oxidant does not react at the top of the oxide; it dissolves into the existing SiO2, diffuses through it as molecular O2 (or H2O), and reacts at the **Si/SiO2 interface** at the bottom. Marker experiments with oxygen isotopes settled this in the 1960s: new oxide appears at the interface and the old oxide rides upward on top of it. Every oxidation step therefore produces a freshly formed interface that has never been exposed to air, handling, or the cleanroom. This is the single most important reason thermal oxide is such a good **gate dielectric** (the insulating film between a transistor's gate electrode and its channel).

Because the reaction consumes the substrate, the oxide is partly "in" the original silicon surface. The number density of Si atoms in the crystal is 5.0 × 10²² cm⁻³; the density of SiO2 "molecules" in amorphous thermal oxide (2.2 g/cm³, 60.08 g/mol) is 2.2 × 10²² cm⁻³. Every Si atom becomes one SiO2 unit, so a thickness x of oxide consumes 2.2/5.0 = **0.44 x** of silicon. Grow 100 nm of oxide and the silicon surface has retreated 44 nm; the top of the oxide sits 56 nm above where the surface used to be. Textbooks quote 44–46% depending on the exact oxide density assumed. This matters when you grow an oxide through a mask (the classic **LOCOS** "bird's beak" of the 1980s: an isolation scheme in which oxide grown under the edge of a **silicon nitride** mask lifted the mask like a beak; silicon nitride, Si3N4, is a hard, dense insulator that oxygen and most etchants barely penetrate, which is why it serves as a mask, and how it is deposited is in 4.1), when you grow a **sacrificial oxide** (an oxide grown only to be stripped again, taking a damaged or sharp-cornered layer of silicon with it) to round fin corners or remove etch damage, and when you calculate how much of a 10 nm nanosheet a 1 nm interfacial oxide has eaten.

### 1.2 The Deal-Grove model

Bruce Deal and Andrew Grove (later CEO of Intel) published the model in 1965 and it has held up remarkably well for oxides thicker than ~30 nm. It treats the process as three flows of oxidant in series, in **steady state** (all three flows equal, so nothing piles up anywhere):

1. **Gas-phase transport** to the oxide surface: F1 = h (C* − C0), where C* is the equilibrium concentration of oxidant dissolved in the oxide surface, C0 is the actual surface concentration, and h is a gas-phase mass-transfer coefficient (how readily the gas delivers molecules to the surface). C* follows **Henry's law**: the amount of a gas that dissolves in a solid is proportional to the gas's partial pressure above it.
2. **Diffusion through the oxide**: F2 = D (C0 − Ci) / x, which is **Fick's law** (flow proportional to the concentration difference divided by the distance) across the existing thickness x, where Ci is the concentration at the interface and D is the diffusivity of oxidant in SiO2.
3. **Reaction at the interface**: F3 = ks Ci, **first order** in oxidant (the rate is simply proportional to how much oxidant is present), with a surface rate constant ks.

Setting F1 = F2 = F3 = F and noting that the growth rate is dx/dt = F / N1, where N1 is the number of oxidant molecules per unit volume of oxide (2.2 × 10²² cm⁻³ for O2, and twice that, 4.4 × 10²² cm⁻³, for H2O because it takes two water molecules per SiO2), gives a differential equation whose solution is the **linear-parabolic law**:

x² + A x = B (t + τ)

Read this first as a relationship between oxide thickness x and growth time t. The constants A and B summarize the reaction and transport conditions; A has units of length and B has units of length squared per time. The offset τ accounts for the starting oxide. At small x the A x term is more important, so thickness grows roughly with time. At large x the x² term dominates, so further growth slows: the reacting molecules must travel through more oxide to reach the silicon.

More precisely, A = 2D (1/ks + 1/h), B = 2 D C* / N1, and τ is a time offset that accounts for any oxide present at t = 0 (including the **native oxide**, the ~1–2 nm of SiO2 that a bare silicon surface grows on its own within hours in room air, and the anomalously fast initial dry growth). Solving for x:

x(t) = (A/2) [ √(1 + 4B (t + τ)/A²) − 1 ]

Two limits fall out:

- **Linear regime** (thin oxide, x ≪ A): x ≈ (B/A)(t + τ). Growth is limited by the interface reaction; the oxidant reaches the interface as fast as it can be consumed. B/A is the **linear rate constant**, and it carries the activation energy of the Si–Si bond-breaking reaction, ~2.0 eV for both dry and wet.
- **Parabolic regime** (thick oxide, x ≫ A): x² ≈ B t. Growth is limited by diffusion through the ever-thicker oxide; the rate falls as 1/x. B is the **parabolic rate constant** and carries the activation energy of oxidant diffusion in SiO2: ~1.23 eV for O2, ~0.78 eV for H2O.

The crossover is at x ≈ A. The rate constants, in the compact Arrhenius form used in most textbooks (for (111) silicon; divide B/A by 1.68 for (100), for the reason given in 1.3), are:

| Ambient | B (µm²/h) | B/A (µm/h) |
|---|---|---|
| Dry O2 | 772 · exp(−1.23 eV / kT) | 6.23 × 10⁶ · exp(−2.0 eV / kT) |
| Wet (H2O, ~640 torr) | 386 · exp(−0.78 eV / kT) | 1.63 × 10⁸ · exp(−2.05 eV / kT) |

In the exponents k is Boltzmann's constant (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K) and T the temperature in kelvin, so exp(−Ea/kT) is the fraction of attempts that carry enough thermal energy to clear the hill Ea.

> **Intuition:** *An activation energy is the height of a hill.* A reaction step happens only when the molecules involved momentarily have at least Ea of energy, and the fraction that do at any instant is exp(−Ea/kT). At 1,000 °C, kT = 0.11 eV, so a 2.0 eV hill is cleared by only exp(−18) ≈ 10⁻⁸ of attempts; the reaction proceeds because there are enormously many attempts per second. The same arithmetic says why temperature is so powerful: raising 1,000 °C to 1,100 °C lifts kT to 0.118 eV and multiplies exp(−2.0/kT) by ~4, which is the factor between the dry B/A entries at 1,000 and 1,100 °C in the table below (0.071 → 0.30 µm/h).

Evaluated, Deal and Grove's own experimental table gives roughly:

| T (°C) | Dry: A (µm) | Dry: B (µm²/h) | Dry: B/A (µm/h) | Dry: τ (h) | Wet: A (µm) | Wet: B (µm²/h) | Wet: B/A (µm/h) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 920 | 0.235 | 0.0049 | 0.021 | 1.4 | 0.50 | 0.203 | 0.41 |
| 1000 | 0.165 | 0.0117 | 0.071 | 0.37 | 0.226 | 0.287 | 1.27 |
| 1100 | 0.090 | 0.027 | 0.30 | 0.076 | 0.11 | 0.510 | 4.64 |
| 1200 | 0.040 | 0.045 | 1.12 | 0.027 | 0.05 | 0.720 | 14.4 |

Why is wet oxidation 20–25× faster in the parabolic regime? Not because H2O diffuses faster (it diffuses roughly ten times more slowly than O2 in silica) but because its solubility C* in SiO2 is enormously higher: ~3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for water versus ~5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ for O2 at 1,000 °C, a factor of ~600. Water also reacts with the silica network itself (Si–O–Si + H2O → 2 Si–OH), opening it up and making the oxide slightly less dense, which is why wet oxides are marginally worse dielectrics and were historically reserved for thick field and isolation oxides while thin gate oxides were always grown dry.

> **Worked example: why wet is 25× faster, not 600×.**
> B = 2 D C* / N1, so B_wet/B_dry = (D_wet/D_dry) × (C*_wet/C*_dry) × (N1_dry/N1_wet). The solubility ratio is ~600 and the N1 ratio is 1/2 (two water molecules per SiO2), so if water diffused as fast as oxygen the ratio would be ~300. The measured ratio at 1,000 °C is 0.287/0.0117 = 24.5, so D_wet/D_dry ≈ 24.5/300 ≈ 1/12: water diffuses roughly ten times more slowly than oxygen through silica.
> The same relation gives the diffusivities. Dry: B = 0.0117 µm²/h = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, so D = B N1 / (2 C*) = 3.3 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 2.2 × 10²² / (2 × 5 × 10¹⁶) ≈ **7 × 10⁻⁹ cm²/s**. Wet: B = 0.287 µm²/h = 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ cm²/s, so D ≈ 8.0 × 10⁻¹³ × 4.4 × 10²² / (2 × 3 × 10¹⁹) ≈ **6 × 10⁻¹⁰ cm²/s**. Water's huge solubility beats its slow diffusion by ~25, and that is the whole reason thick oxides are grown wet.

> **Worked example: how long to grow an oxide?**
> Suppose a 1970s-style process (the era of ~100 nm gate oxides) needs a 100 nm dry gate oxide at 1,000 °C. Using the (111) table, A = 0.165 µm, B = 0.0117 µm²/h, τ = 0.37 h, and x = 0.100 µm:
> t = (x² + A·x)/B − τ = (0.0100 + 0.0165)/0.0117 − 0.37 = 2.26 − 0.37 ≈ **1.9 h**.
> Note that x = 100 nm is smaller than A = 165 nm, so this oxide is grown mostly in the mixed linear-parabolic region: the linear term (A·x = 0.0165) is bigger than the parabolic term (x² = 0.010). We use the (111) table because it is the one Deal and Grove published; on a standard (100) wafer the linear rate constant is 1.68× smaller (B/A = 0.071/1.68 = 0.042 µm/h, so A = B/(B/A) = 0.277 µm while B is unchanged), and the same 100 nm oxide takes (0.0100 + 0.0277)/0.0117 − 0.37 ≈ **~2.9 h**, 50% longer. The (100) correction matters whenever x is comparable to or smaller than A, i.e. for every thin oxide; for the thick oxides below, where x² dominates, it lengthens the times by only ~10%. Now compare a 1 µm oxide for isolation:
> Dry at 1,000 °C: (1.0 + 0.165)/0.0117 − 0.37 ≈ **99 h**. Nobody does this.
> Wet at 1,000 °C: A = 0.226, B = 0.287: (1.0 + 0.226)/0.287 ≈ **4.3 h**. This is why thick oxides are always grown wet.
> Wet at 1,100 °C: (1.0 + 0.11)/0.51 ≈ **2.2 h**. Every 100 °C roughly halves the time.
> In all cases the silicon consumed is 0.44 × 1 µm = 440 nm. For the 100 nm gate oxide, the channel surface moved down 44 nm, which is why the depths of the pre-oxidation implants (dopants shot in as an ion beam; see Before you start and Module 10) in old processes were specified relative to the *post-oxidation* surface.

The model breaks down for thin dry oxides (< 20–30 nm), where growth is faster than predicted for reasons that are still argued about; the τ term patches this empirically. Nobody today grows a sub-2 nm gate oxide with Deal-Grove: they use the rapid-thermal and radical methods of 1.5 and calibrate the recipe by measurement.

### 1.3 Orientation, pressure, and chlorine

**Orientation dependence.** The linear rate constant scales with the number of silicon bonds available to react per unit area of interface: textbook "available bond" counts give 11.8 × 10¹⁴ per cm² on (111) versus 6.8 × 10¹⁴ on (100), and the (111) linear rate is empirically 1.68× that of (100). The parabolic constant B, being a bulk-diffusion property of amorphous SiO2, is orientation-independent. This matters more than it used to: a FinFET fin has a (100) top and (110) sidewalls, and a nanosheet has (100) top and bottom surfaces and (110) edges, so a "conformal" thermal oxide is intrinsically thicker on the sidewalls. (Orientation also changes the number of electrical defects at the interface, which is taken up in 1.6.)

**Pressure.** B is proportional to C*, hence to oxidant partial pressure; dilute O2 (in N2 or Ar) is used today to slow growth enough to control 1–2 nm films.

**Chlorine.** Adding 1–5% HCl, or an organic chlorine source like trans-1,2-dichloroethylene (DCE) that decomposes to HCl, to a dry O2 ambient **getters** mobile alkali ions (Na⁺, K⁺), meaning it chemically captures them and carries them off as volatile chlorides. This was the fix for the **threshold-voltage** drift that plagued early MOS: the threshold voltage is the gate voltage at which a transistor turns on, and a stray positive ion sitting in the gate oxide shifts it, so the transistors on early chips drifted over hours of operation. Chlorine also getters heavy metals from the silicon surface and modestly increases the oxidation rate. Too much chlorine roughens the oxide and leaves Cl at the interface, so the fraction is tightly controlled; chlorine oxidation is still used for thick I/O gate oxides and sacrificial oxides.

### 1.4 Pyrogenic steam and the wet ambient

Early wet oxidation bubbled O2 through a flask of water at 95 °C; the vapor pressure at that temperature (~640 torr) is why the classic wet rate constants are quoted at 640 torr. Production furnaces replaced this with **pyrogenic steam**: high-purity H2 and O2 are metered by **mass-flow controllers** (MFCs: valves that measure a gas flow from the heat it carries away and hold it to ~1% of the setpoint) into a quartz torch at the furnace inlet, where they react (2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O) at the ~800 °C tube temperature. The H2:O2 ratio is held slightly below 2:1 (a typical recipe is ~1.8:1, just under the **stoichiometric** 2:1, the exact proportion in which the reaction equation consumes them), so there is always a small excess of oxygen and every hydrogen molecule is burned in the torch; the torch region is well above hydrogen's auto-ignition temperature (~570 °C), so combustion is complete and no unreacted hydrogen reaches the wafers or the exhaust. Because the water is made in situ from semiconductor-grade gases, it carries none of the metal ions a liquid-water bubbler would. Water content is set by the H2:O2 ratio.

The machine is the vertical furnace of Section 2 with a torch option (TEL and Kokusai both offer external-combustion steam on their furnaces): the torch is a small separate quartz chamber with its own heater and igniter, so the hydrogen is lit before it can reach the process tube. A typical thick-oxide recipe: load at ~700 °C, ramp at ~10 °C/min to 1,000–1,100 °C in nitrogen with a little O2 (so the surface never sits bare and hot), switch to steam for the growth time (a few hours for 1 µm, from the table in 1.2), then finish with a few minutes of dry O2 and an inert-gas hold to densify the top of the film and lower its fixed charge (1.6), and ramp down. The interlocks matter as much as the chemistry: O2 must be flowing and the torch at temperature before H2 is admitted, a torch thermocouple confirms ignition, the H2:O2 ratio is watched continuously, and any fault closes the hydrogen valve at the gas cabinet, because hydrogen reaching a 1,000 °C tube full of oxygen unburned is an explosion.

> **Worked example: what pressure of steam does the torch make?**
> Feed 1.8 volumes of H2 per volume of O2. Burning consumes all 1.8 H2 and 0.9 O2, producing 1.8 H2O and leaving 0.1 O2, so the gas over the wafers is 1.8/1.9 ≈ 95% water at atmospheric pressure: a water partial pressure of ~720 torr if no diluent is added. B scales with oxidant pressure (1.3), so the parabolic constant is ~720/640 ≈ 1.13× the 640 torr bubbler value in the tables, and the 1 µm wet oxide of the 1.2 example grows in ~3.8 h rather than 4.3 h at 1,000 °C. Add N2 as a diluent and the growth slows in proportion; that is the knob for fine-tuning a wet oxide thickness without changing temperature.

Today steam is used for oxides thicker than a few tens of nanometres (~): field and isolation oxides in analog, power and MEMS flows, the oxidation of thick **polysilicon** (silicon deposited as a mass of tiny, randomly oriented crystals called **grains**, rather than as one crystal; 4.1) in older memory flows, and, most often in a leading-edge fab, as the ambient of the steam **densification anneals** that convert and densify the deposited gap-fill oxides of 4.4. (An **anneal** is a heating step that deposits nothing: it lets atoms move just enough to repair damage, settle bonds or drive a reaction, and the gas it is done in is part of the recipe. The word recurs throughout the module: a hydrogen anneal caps broken bonds at an interface, a nitrogen anneal lowers an oxide's fixed charge, a spike anneal activates dopants.) No gate oxide is grown wet: the Si–OH groups that water leaves in the network (1.2) make the film slightly less dense and a worse insulator.

> **What can go wrong:** *Hydrogen excursion*: an oxygen-lean ratio or a torch that has cooled leaves unburned H2 in an oxygen-filled tube; the interlocks above exist because the consequence is a detonation, which is why furnaces with a torch carry hydrogen detectors and why H2 is the most interlocked gas in the fab. *Thickness drift from MFC drift*: the water partial pressure follows the H2:O2 ratio, so a 2% drift in either MFC is a ~2% drift in the wet oxide rate, caught by the monitor wafers of 2.1. *Wet-ambient devitrification*: steam attacks quartz faster than dry O2 does, so tubes and boats in steam service cloud over and shed flakes sooner and are replaced on a shorter schedule (2.1). *Condensation*: steam reaching an unheated exhaust line condenses and corrodes it, so the exhaust is heat-traced.

### 1.5 In-situ steam generation (ISSG) and radical oxidation

For films below ~5 nm a furnace is too slow to control and too hot for the thermal budget. **ISSG** does the pyrogenic reaction not in a torch but directly above the hot wafer inside a lamp-heated single-wafer chamber (Applied Materials' Vantage RadOx/RTO chambers are the canonical example). H2 (typically 1–33%) and O2 at low pressure (~5–20 torr) flow over a wafer held at 900–1,100 °C for a few seconds to a minute. The wafer itself is the ignition source, and the combustion intermediates include a high concentration of atomic oxygen **radicals**: molecule fragments with an unpaired electron, far more reactive than the stable molecule they came from, which react on first contact with almost anything. Radicals live for microseconds before recombining, which is why they must be made at the wafer rather than piped in. Radical oxidation has three useful properties: the rate is far less orientation-dependent than molecular O2 (so fin sidewalls and tops oxidize equally); it readily oxidizes silicon nitride (the dense mask material of 1.1), which molecular O2 barely touches, so a nitride surface can be capped with oxide (and a nitride mask is not safe from it); and the oxide it makes has excellent interface quality at very thin thickness. ISSG oxides are used for the ~1 nm gate interfacial layer, for the thin liner inside **shallow-trench isolation (STI)** (the oxide-filled trench that electrically separates neighbouring transistors), for rounding fin and trench corners (the stress of the growing oxide suppresses the rate at a convex corner, so sharp corners grow less oxide and end up rounded), and for the **tunnel oxides** (the thin oxide electrons are pushed through to charge or erase a flash memory cell) in some NAND flows. Plasma oxidation (an O2 plasma at 300–600 °C, e.g. TEL's Trias SPA tools) is the low-temperature alternative for 1–3 nm oxides.

### 1.6 The Si/SiO2 interface, and why silicon won

Germanium had the higher **carrier mobility** (carriers are the electrons or holes that carry a transistor's current, and mobility is how fast they drift per unit of electric field, so higher mobility means a faster transistor; see Before you start), and Bell Labs' first transistors were germanium. Silicon won in the 1960s for one reason: its oxide. GeO2 is water-soluble and sublimes as GeO above ~400 °C; GaAs has no stable, stoichiometric, low-defect native oxide at all; silicon grows a dense, amorphous, thermally stable, insulating oxide whose interface with the crystal is, after a hydrogen anneal (the heating step defined in 1.4, done here in hydrogen because hydrogen caps broken bonds), almost electrically perfect. Its **bandgap** (the energy needed to free an electron from its bond so it can carry current) is ~9 eV, so essentially no electrons are free at any temperature, which is what "insulator" means; it withstands ~10 MV/cm before breaking down. The interface is abrupt to within about one or two atomic layers, with a sub-oxide transition region of ~0.5 nm in which silicon atoms are bonded to one, two or three oxygens instead of four (the Si¹⁺, Si²⁺ and Si³⁺ states seen by XPS, an X-ray method described in Section 9).

> **Intuition:** *A trap is a parking space for an electron.* An **interface trap** is a broken bond at the Si/SiO2 boundary where an electron can park. A parked electron is a fixed charge right next to the channel: it shifts the threshold voltage and scatters the electrons flowing past, so at 10¹² traps per cm² a transistor would neither switch cleanly nor carry much current. A hydrogen anneal caps the broken bonds and removes ~99% of the parking spaces.

The defects that remain are well catalogued. **Interface traps** (Dit, the number per cm² per eV of energy) are mostly **Pb centers**: silicon dangling bonds at the interface, ~10¹² cm⁻² eV⁻¹ as grown, reduced to ~10¹⁰ cm⁻² eV⁻¹ by a **forming-gas anneal** (4–10% H2 in N2, 400–450 °C, 20–30 min) that terminates the dangling bonds with hydrogen. **Fixed oxide charge** (Qf) is a positive charge within ~2 nm of the interface associated with incompletely oxidized silicon; it is minimized by finishing the oxidation in dry O2 and by a post-oxidation anneal in inert gas (the "Deal triangle" is the classic chart of Qf against temperature and cooling ambient). **Mobile ionic charge** is the alkali problem fixed by chlorine and by cleanroom hygiene. **Oxide trapped charge** comes from radiation or from **hot-carrier injection**, electrons accelerated in the channel that jump into the oxide and stay there. The reason these matter: 10¹⁰ traps per cm² per eV is one defect per ~10⁴ nm², and a 2 nm-node transistor's gate area is only a few thousand nm². Silicon's oxide is the only one that gets this clean. Trap densities are higher on (110) and (111) surfaces than on (100), which is the original reason (100) became the standard CMOS orientation.

> **What can go wrong:** *Mobile sodium*: parts-per-billion Na⁺ from handling or unclean quartz drifts under the gate field and shifts the threshold voltage over hours; the fix is chlorine and cleanroom hygiene, and the detector is a **C-V sweep** (measure the gate capacitance while sweeping the gate voltage; each kind of oxide charge shifts or stretches the curve in its own way). *Thin-oxide non-uniformity*: at 1 nm a ±0.1 nm variation is ±10%, which is why gate oxides moved to single-wafer chambers with radial temperature control; ellipsometry maps (Section 9) catch it. *Corner thinning*: a thermal oxide is thinner on the convex corners of a fin, exactly where the gate field is strongest, so the corner breaks down first; the fix is radical oxidation or deliberate corner rounding. *Slip*: a too-fast furnace ramp shears the crystal near the wafer edge (Section 2), and the resulting line defects kill every transistor they cross.

### 1.7 Where thermal oxide is used today

The gate oxide was the marquee application for 40 years. Its thickness followed scaling from ~100 nm in the early 1970s to ~1.2 nm of **nitrided oxide** (SiON: oxide with a few percent nitrogen added by a nitrogen plasma or an NH3 anneal, which raises k slightly and blocks boron from the gate electrode from diffusing into the channel) at the 90 and 65 nm nodes, where **direct tunneling** leakage became untenable. Tunneling is a quantum effect: an insulator thinner than ~3 nm lets electrons pass straight through it without ever having the energy to climb over it, at a rate that rises exponentially as the film thins (roughly 10× per 0.2 nm). Intel's 45 nm process in 2007 replaced most of the oxide with a **high-k** film, hafnium dioxide (HfO2), deposited by ALD (Section 5), under a metal gate: the **high-k metal gate (HKMG)** stack. The point of a high-k insulator is captured by the **equivalent oxide thickness (EOT)**: because capacitance is proportional to k/t, a film of dielectric constant k and physical thickness t stores the same charge as SiO2 of thickness EOT = t × 3.9/k. Two nanometres of HfO2 (k ~20) has EOT = 2 × 3.9/20 = 0.39 nm: electrically as thin as 0.4 nm of oxide, physically five times too thick to tunnel through. But a purely high-k-on-silicon interface has poor mobility and too many traps, so every HKMG stack still has an **interfacial layer** (IL) of SiO2 under the HfO2. It is formed as a ~0.5–1.0 nm chemical oxide by ozonated water or **SC1** (the ammonia/peroxide clean of Module 03) in the pre-gate clean, or by ISSG/plasma oxidation, and is deliberately thinned to ~0.5–0.7 nm by **scavenging** (a Ti- or Al-containing layer above the HfO2 that pulls oxygen out of the IL during the anneal), so that the stack's total EOT lands at ~0.8–1.0 nm. Thermal oxidation still appears throughout the flow: the pad oxide under the nitride **CMP stop** (**chemical mechanical polishing**, CMP, is the step of Module 12 that grinds a wafer flat with a slurry; a CMP stop is a hard film the polish cannot remove, so polishing ends on it), the STI liner, sacrificial oxides grown and stripped to remove etch-damaged silicon, screen oxides for implants, and the 3–7 nm gate oxides of the 1.8 V and 3.3 V I/O transistors that live on every chip alongside the core logic.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The interfacial layer is the largest single contributor to EOT, and EOT sets how firmly the gate controls the channel; its thickness and quality also set carrier mobility and the threshold voltage. In Module 11 the tenths of a nanometre argued over here become the drive current and leakage of the transistor.

## 2. Furnaces and Rapid Thermal Processing

Every minute a wafer spends at 1,000 °C spreads its dopants, and the further into the flow it is, the more there is to spoil. That single constraint decides which of two very different heating machines a step uses. A **furnace** heats 100 or more wafers together for hours, and is the right tool when the *film* is the product and time at temperature costs nothing: thick oxides, the nitride and polysilicon of Section 4. A **rapid thermal processor** heats one wafer for seconds, and is the right tool when the *thermal history* is the product: dopant activation (Module 10), a thin oxide, an anneal that must fix one thing without moving anything else. At the leading edge most hot steps have migrated to single-wafer tools; furnaces survive for batch CVD, batch ALD and long anneals.

### 2.1 The vertical batch furnace

Oxidation, **low-pressure CVD** (LPCVD, the furnace-based deposition of Section 4.1), and many anneals are done in **vertical furnaces**, which replaced horizontal tube furnaces in the 1990s because a vertical tube has no boat-to-tube contact to scrape particles, a smaller footprint, and better temperature uniformity, since hot gas rises symmetrically around a vertical axis instead of along one side of a horizontal tube. The market is led by Tokyo Electron (TELINDY PLUS series) and Kokusai Electric (AdvancedAce-300, and its TSURUGI-C² batch ALD platform), with ASM International (A412 series) a distant third.

The anatomy: a quartz (or, for high-temperature and chlorine processes, silicon carbide) process tube about 1.5 m tall, surrounded by a resistive heater with 3–5 independently controlled zones. Wafers sit horizontally in slots of a quartz or SiC boat holding 100–150 (125 is typical) 300 mm wafers at ~6–10 mm pitch. The boat is loaded from **FOUPs** (the sealed 25-wafer carriers of Module 05) by a robot in a nitrogen-purged load area, lifted into the tube by an elevator, and sealed at the bottom. Gas enters from the top (or through injector tubes running the length of the boat) and exits at the bottom to a vacuum pump (LPCVD, 0.1–2 torr) or exhaust (atmospheric oxidation).

Temperature is everything. The tube is held at a standby of ~600–700 °C, wafers are loaded at that temperature, and the furnace ramps at 5–15 °C/min to the setpoint, then stabilizes for 10–20 min before gas is introduced, holds for the process time, and ramps down at a similar rate. Ramp rates are limited by **slip**: the edge of a wafer heats and cools faster than its centre, the temperature difference puts the crystal under stress, and above a critical stress the crystal shears along its weakest planes (the {111} planes; curly braces mean the whole family of planes equivalent to (111) by symmetry, of which there are four in silicon, whereas (111) in parentheses names one of them), leaving line defects called dislocations that ruin any transistor built on them. A full cycle including load, ramp, process, ramp-down and unload is 3–6 hours for a 100-wafer batch, so the throughput can still be tens of wafers per hour. Temperature is controlled to ±0.5 °C along the boat by thermocouples in each zone plus a profile thermocouple inside the tube, using models that correct for the thermal mass of the load.

**Dummy wafers** (also called filler or baffle wafers) occupy the top and bottom ~5–10 slots. Gas is depleted as it flows down the boat, the ends of the boat see different thermal environments, and the first wafers scrub contaminants from the gas; the dummies absorb these edge effects so product wafers see uniform conditions. They are reclaimed and reused hundreds of times; a leading-edge fab consumes tens of thousands of dummy and monitor wafers a month, a real line item in the Shin-Etsu and SUMCO order books (Module 03).

> **What can go wrong:** *Slip* from a too-fast ramp or a warped boat, seen as lines near the wafer edge under a microscope or as a ring of dead dies at wafer sort (a **die** is one chip on the wafer; **wafer sort** is the electrical test of every die before the wafer is cut up). *Gas depletion* down the boat, so top and bottom wafers get different thicknesses; countered by injector tubes, zone temperature offsets and the dummies. *The first-wafer effect*: the first lot after an idle period or a clean sees different wall chemistry and comes out thinner or thicker, so fabs run a warm-up lot or "season" the tube. *Devitrification*: after hundreds of hours quartz slowly crystallizes and sheds flakes, so tubes and boats are replaced on a schedule. All are caught by **monitor wafers** (blank wafers placed at several boat positions in every run) whose thickness maps are measured minutes after unload.

### 2.2 Single-wafer RTP

**Rapid thermal processing** (RTP) heats one wafer at a time with radiation from banks of tungsten-halogen lamps, ramping at 50–250 °C/s, holding for a second to a minute, and cooling at up to ~100 °C/s. Applied Materials' Vantage platform (Radiance, Radiance Plus, and the Astra DSA (laser) variants) dominates; Mattson (now Chinese-owned, "Beijing E-Town") and Screen are the alternatives.

Inside a Vantage chamber, several hundred small lamps in a honeycomb array sit above a wafer rotating at ~200–300 rpm on an edge ring; 5–8 **pyrometers** (non-contact thermometers that read the infrared glow of the wafer's backside) at different radii feed a closed loop that adjusts the lamp zones to hold the radial temperature profile flat to ±1–2 °C during a ramp. The loop needs an in-situ measurement of **emissivity**, how brightly a surface glows for its temperature, because backside films and patterning change it and a wrong emissivity is a wrong temperature. RTP applications include implant **activation** (jolting implanted dopant atoms onto lattice sites so they become electrically active, by **spike anneals** that ramp to ~1,050 °C and immediately back down, spending well under a second at peak; Module 10), RTO and ISSG, **silicide** formation at 250–500 °C (reacting a metal with silicon to make the low-resistance compound at a contact), and high-k post-deposition anneals, at ~50–100 wafers per hour per chamber. **Dynamic surface anneal (DSA)** goes further: a continuous infrared laser is focused into a thin line and swept across the wafer, so that any one point is at peak temperature for only ~0.1–10 ms (the **dwell time**), reaching ~1,200–1,300 °C (~), just below silicon's 1,414 °C melting point, while the wafer as a whole stays at a few hundred degrees. The point is that activation needs temperature but diffusion needs time: the fraction of dopant atoms sitting on lattice sites rises with peak temperature (and a millisecond quench freezes in more than the equilibrium limit allows), while the boron diffusion length in 1 ms at 1,300 °C, by the arithmetic of the worked example below, is under 1 nm. During a millisecond dwell the heat penetrates only ~100 µm (~) into the 775 µm wafer, so the bulk of the wafer is its own heat sink and the surface cools in milliseconds with no external quench. The costs are pattern-dependent absorption (a laser is even more sensitive to it than a lamp), stress from the steep temperature gradient at the edge of the line, and a throughput set by the scan speed.

> **What can go wrong:** *Pattern-dependent heating*: dense metal or polysilicon regions absorb the lamps differently from open areas, so the temperature can vary by ~10 °C across one die during a spike; mitigated by absorber caps, backside heating and rotation. *Emissivity error*: a backside film changes how the wafer glows, and if the compensation lags the whole wafer runs hot or cold. *Edge-ring slip*: the support ring is a heat sink or source at the edge, and a mismatch there gives slip lines in the outer millimetres. Detection is by thermal-monitor wafers (an implanted wafer whose **sheet resistance** after anneal is a thermometer; sheet resistance, Rs, is the resistance of a square patch of a thin film, in ohms per square, the same for any size of square, and for an implanted layer it tracks how many dopants have been activated, so a hotter anneal reads as a lower Rs) and by slip inspection.

> **Worked example: what "thermal budget" means in nanometres.**
> How far does a dopant spread during a hot step? The characteristic distance is √(Dt). Take boron in silicon with the intrinsic Arrhenius fit used by Plummer, D = 0.76 exp(−3.46 eV / kT) cm²/s (a low-concentration value; at the ~10²¹ cm⁻³ doping of a real source/drain diffusion is faster, so treat the results as ~).
> Furnace: 2 h at 1,000 °C. kT = 0.110 eV, so D = 0.76 × exp(−31.5) ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, and √(Dt) = √(1.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 7,200) ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ cm ≈ **~100 nm**.
> Spike anneal: 1 s at 1,050 °C. kT = 0.114 eV, D = 0.76 × exp(−30.4) ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, and √(Dt) ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻⁷ cm ≈ **~2 nm**.
> Laser DSA: 1 ms at 1,300 °C. kT = 0.136 eV, D = 0.76 × exp(−25.5) ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹² cm²/s (~120× the spike-anneal value, because of the higher temperature), but t is 1,000× shorter, so √(Dt) ≈ 8 × 10⁻⁸ cm ≈ **~0.8 nm**: hotter than a spike and yet less diffusion.
> A furnace step blurs a dopant profile by about the length of a modern channel; a spike anneal blurs it by a few atomic spacings. That factor of ~50 is why every anneal after the source/drain implants is a single-wafer step, and why "thermal budget" is quoted in practice as an equivalent √(Dt).

## 3. Chemical Vapor Deposition: Fundamentals

### 3.1 The sequence of events

In **chemical vapor deposition** (CVD), gaseous **precursors** (molecules that carry the atom you want, wrapped in disposable groups that fall away on the hot surface) are introduced over a heated wafer, react on (or near) the surface, and leave a solid film behind. The steps in series are:

1. Convective transport of reactants into the reactor.
2. Diffusion of reactants across the stagnant **boundary layer** that forms above the wafer surface.
3. **Adsorption** on the surface: a molecule sticks to the surface without entering it (not to be confused with absorption, which is soaking in).
4. Surface diffusion, reaction, and incorporation into the growing film.
5. **Desorption**, the reverse of adsorption, of gaseous byproducts (HCl, H2, CH4, HF (hydrogen fluoride), and so on).
6. Diffusion of byproducts back across the boundary layer and convective removal.

### 3.2 Surface-reaction versus mass-transport limited growth

> **Intuition:** *Two taps in series.* Gas supply and surface reaction are two taps on one pipe: whichever is more nearly closed sets the flow. Heat opens the surface-reaction tap exponentially. At low temperature it is nearly shut and is the bottleneck: only temperature matters, and the gas can be delivered any old way. At high temperature it is wide open, and the gas-supply tap (how fast molecules cross the stagnant layer above the wafer) sets the rate: now flow geometry is everything and temperature hardly matters.

The two rate-limiting cases define how a reactor must be built. Using the same steady-state treatment Grove applied to oxidation: let the flux through the boundary layer be F1 = hg (Cg − Cs), with hg = D/δ the gas-phase mass-transfer coefficient (D the gas diffusivity, δ the boundary-layer thickness, Cg and Cs the precursor concentrations in the bulk gas and at the surface), and the surface reaction flux be F2 = ks Cs. In steady state the growth rate is

R = (ks · hg / (ks + hg)) · (Cg / N)

where N is the number of atoms per cm³ in the solid film, which turns a flux of atoms into a thickness rate.

- If ks ≪ hg (low temperature): R ≈ ks Cg / N. The process is **surface-reaction limited**. ks = k0 exp(−Ea/kT) with Ea typically 1–2 eV, so the rate rises steeply with temperature (see the worked example below). Gas flow uniformity hardly matters; temperature uniformity is everything. This is the regime for LPCVD in batch furnaces: at 0.1–1 torr the diffusivity is ~1,000× larger than at atmospheric pressure, hg is huge, and 100+ wafers stacked 5 mm apart can all be fed adequately as long as the tube temperature is uniform.
- If ks ≫ hg (high temperature): R ≈ hg Cg / N. The process is **mass-transport limited**. The rate depends on D/δ, only weakly on temperature (D ∝ T^1.5–2), but strongly on flow geometry. Every wafer must see the same boundary layer, which is why atmospheric CVD and epitaxy are done one wafer at a time (or a few) with carefully engineered **laminar flow** (smooth, layered flow with no turbulence, so the boundary layer over the wafer is steady and predictable).

An **Arrhenius plot** (log of the rate against 1/T) shows the two regimes as a steep straight line at low T, whose slope is the activation energy, bending to a nearly flat line at high T. Production processes are placed deliberately: LPCVD deep in the reaction-limited regime for batch uniformity; epi and TEOS/ozone in the transport-limited regime for high rate.

> **Worked example: how tightly must a furnace hold temperature?**
> In the reaction-limited regime R ∝ exp(−Ea/kT), so the fractional change of rate per degree is d(ln R)/dT = Ea/(kT²). For silane polysilicon at 600 °C (T = 873 K, kT = 0.0752 eV) with Ea = 1.5 eV: 1.5 / (0.0752 × 873) = 0.023 per kelvin, i.e. **2.3% per °C**. A 10 °C error changes the rate by exp(0.23) − 1 ≈ 25% (about 16% if Ea is 1.0 eV). A ±0.5 °C hold, which is what a production furnace achieves along a 125-wafer boat, keeps the rate within ~±1%. This is the whole reason LPCVD tubes have multi-zone heaters, profile thermocouples and a 10–20 min stabilization before the gas is turned on.

The boundary layer for laminar flow over a plate thickens along the flow direction as δ ~ √(μ x / ρ v), where μ is the gas viscosity, ρ its density, v the flow speed and x the distance along the flow. That is why old horizontal reactors tilted the **susceptor** (the heated plate the wafers rest on) to compensate for depletion downstream, and why modern single-wafer chambers use a **showerhead**: a perforated plate (hundreds to thousands of 0.5–1 mm holes) a few cm above the wafer, fed from a **plenum** (a gas-filled cavity behind the plate that evens out the pressure so every hole delivers the same flow), so gas arrives perpendicular and uniform across the whole 300 mm. The wafer sits on a heated pedestal (300–700 °C) or is lamp-heated; in a plasma reactor the showerhead is usually the RF electrode.

### 3.3 Step coverage and conformality

> **Intuition:** *Spray paint into a narrow slot.* A paint that sticks the instant it touches builds up on the rim of the slot, where it is easiest to hit, and seals the slot over with air still inside. A paint that bounces off many times before it sticks wanders down into the slot and coats the walls as evenly as the rim. Deposition chemistries differ in exactly this way.

**Step coverage** is the ratio of film thickness on the sidewall or bottom of a feature to that on the flat top; a film with 100% step coverage everywhere is called **conformal**. The feature's **aspect ratio** (depth divided by width) is what makes coverage hard. Coverage is governed by the **sticking coefficient** (the probability that an arriving molecule reacts on first contact) and by the geometry. A precursor with a sticking coefficient near 1 deposits where it first lands; the top corners of a trench see the widest solid angle of gas, so they grow fastest, forming an overhang ("bread-loafing") that closes the trench and leaves a **void** or **keyhole** inside. A precursor with a low sticking coefficient (10⁻³ to 10⁻²) bounces many times before reacting, samples the entire feature, and deposits uniformly: this is why oxide from TEOS and ozone (TEOS is a liquid oxide precursor introduced in 4.1) is conformal and silane/oxygen oxide is not. In the limit of a strictly self-limiting surface reaction (sticking coefficient effectively zero once the surface is saturated, until the next reactant clears it) you get the atomic layer process of Section 5, with 100% step coverage in features of aspect ratio > 100:1.

Surface diffusion of adsorbed species before incorporation also improves conformality and is why higher-temperature LPCVD films are more conformal than PECVD ones.

### 3.4 Gas-phase nucleation

If precursor molecules react with each other in the gas above the wafer instead of on it, they nucleate particles that fall on the wafer as "snow" and produce hazy, low-density films. The risk rises with pressure, temperature, and precursor partial pressure; silane is especially prone (SiH4 → SiH2 + H2, and SiH2 inserts into another SiH4 to start a chain ending in a silicon particle). LPCVD's low pressure, silane dilution, and the short ~10–20 mm showerhead gap of PECVD are all defenses. Particles (Module 05) are as often generated inside deposition chambers as brought in from outside. Between lots the chamber walls are etched clean by fluorine atoms from an NF3 plasma struck in a side chamber (a **remote-plasma clean**), then coated with a thin fresh film (**seasoning**) so that the first product wafer sees the same wall chemistry as the last; these two routines are central to how every CVD tool is run.

> **What can go wrong:** The dominant particle source in a deposition tool is its own walls: film builds up on the showerhead, chamber and pumping lines with every wafer, and once thick enough its stress makes it flake onto the wafer. Silane "snow" from gas-phase nucleation is the other classic. Both are caught with **particle monitor wafers**, blank wafers run through the chamber and scanned by a laser surface inspector (Module 13), and both set the clean interval: the number of wafers or micrometres of film between NF3 cleans.

### 3.5 Delivering the precursors

Gaseous precursors come from cylinders through gas cabinets, purifiers, and the MFCs of 1.4, accurate to ~1% of setpoint. Liquid precursors are delivered either by a **bubbler**, where a carrier gas (N2 or Ar) passes through the heated liquid and picks up vapor at a rate set by the liquid's vapor pressure at the bubbler temperature (TEOS, for example, has ~1.5 torr vapor pressure at 20 °C, so a bubbler runs at 40–65 °C), or by a **direct liquid injection** (DLI) vaporizer, where a liquid flow controller meters the liquid and a heated, atomized injector flashes it into a carrier gas. DLI is more repeatable because it does not depend on the liquid level or on bubbler thermal history. Solid precursors sublime from a heated ampoule at 100–200 °C under a carrier-gas sweep, with every downstream line and the showerhead heat-traced above the condensation temperature. The precursors you will meet in the rest of the module:

## 4. The CVD Family

CVD is a family, sorted by temperature and by job. The hot batch films (polysilicon, nitride, dense oxide) are grown in furnaces at 600–900 °C early in the flow, before anything heat-sensitive exists. Once the transistors are doped, and certainly once metal is present, films must be made below ~400 °C with the help of a plasma. Two members of the family exist purely to fill narrow gaps without voids, and the last deposits the metals of contacts and memory word lines. This section takes them in that order.

### 4.1 LPCVD

**Low-pressure CVD** at 0.1–2 torr and 550–900 °C in a vertical furnace makes the workhorse dielectric and semiconductor films of the **front end of line (FEOL)**, the transistor-building steps before any wiring exists (its counterpart, the back end of line, is defined in 4.2). Batch size and uniformity are its strengths; temperature and hours-long cycle times are its limits.

**Polysilicon** (polycrystalline silicon, the film of tiny grains introduced in 1.4): SiH4 → Si + 2 H2 at 580–650 °C, ~0.2–1 torr, ~5–20 nm/min. Below ~580 °C the deposit is amorphous (smoother, and often crystallized later); above it, polycrystalline. In-situ doping with PH3 is possible but phosphine adsorbs strongly and poisons the silane reaction, cutting the rate several-fold, so poly is often implant-doped instead. Poly forms the **dummy gates** of gate-last flows (a placeholder gate that is later removed and replaced with metal; Module 11), the channels and the sacrificial **word-line stacks** of 3D NAND (word lines are the horizontal conductor sheets of the memory array, stacked hundreds high; Module 15), and DRAM plugs.

**Silicon nitride**: 3 SiH2Cl2 + 4 NH3 → Si3N4 + 6 HCl + 6 H2 at 700–800 °C (~750 °C typical), 0.2–0.5 torr, with a large excess of ammonia (NH3:DCS ~5–10:1). The film is near-stoichiometric, contains a few atomic percent hydrogen, is highly tensile (~1 GPa, so thick films crack), and is chemically tough: it is the CMP stop for STI, the **hard mask** for fin patterning (a tough film that carries the pattern into the etch when photoresist alone would be eaten away), and, before ALD took over, the **gate spacer** (the thin insulating wall on each side of the gate that holds the source/drain contacts a fixed distance from the channel).

**TEOS oxide**: tetraethyl orthosilicate, Si(OC2H5)4, decomposes at 650–750 °C to conformal, dense SiO2, used for spacers, liners, and hard masks when a 700 °C step is affordable; **high-temperature oxide (HTO)** (SiH2Cl2 + N2O at ~900 °C) is the highest-quality deposited oxide, used in memory **ONO** stacks (an oxide/nitride/oxide sandwich in which the nitride stores the charge of a flash cell).

> **What can go wrong:** *Phosphine poisoning*: in a doped-poly run PH3 is scrubbed from the gas as it flows down the boat, so the rate and doping vary from slot to slot. *Nitride cracking*: at ~1 GPa tension a nitride film thicker than ~100–200 nm can crack or peel, especially at sharp steps, so thick nitride is deposited in stages or replaced by a lower-stress PECVD film. Both show up on monitor-wafer thickness maps and under optical inspection.

### 4.2 PECVD

Once the first copper or aluminum layer is on the wafer, nothing may exceed ~400 °C: copper diffuses, aluminum pushes up **hillocks** (bumps it raises to relieve its own stress) and the low-k films degrade. This part of the flow is the **back end of line (BEOL)**, the wiring levels built above the transistors (Module 12). **Plasma-enhanced CVD** supplies the activation energy electrically instead of thermally: a **capacitively coupled plasma (CCP)**, struck between two parallel plates (the showerhead and the pedestal) driven at 13.56 MHz (a frequency reserved internationally for industrial use, so equipment can radiate at it without interfering with anything), at 100–2,000 W and 1–10 torr, creates electrons at 2–5 eV that dissociate the precursors into radicals, which react on a wafer at only 300–400 °C. The films are amorphous, hydrogen-rich (10–30 at.% H, as Si–H and N–H), less dense than their LPCVD equivalents, and non-conformal (step coverage 50–70%), but they are fast (100s of nm/min) and cool.

Applied Materials' Producer platform (twin-chamber modules, three per tool, so six wafers in parallel) and Lam's Vector (four-station sequential-deposition chambers) dominate PECVD; TEL and, in China, Piotech are the challengers. The films:

- **SiO2** from SiH4 + N2O (or TEOS + O2) for **interlayer dielectrics** (the insulator between one wiring level and the next), hard masks, and **passivation** (the final protective skin over the finished chip).
- **SiNx:H** (the x says the nitrogen-to-silicon ratio is not fixed at the 4:3 of Si3N4 but set by the recipe, and the :H that the film carries bonded hydrogen) from SiH4 + NH3 + N2 for **etch stops** (a thin film of a different material that an etch cannot penetrate, so the etch ends on it), passivation, and hard masks. Adding a low-frequency (300–400 kHz) RF component drives ions into the film and switches the stress from tensile (~+500 MPa) to compressive (~−2 GPa); in the "dual stress liner" era (90–32 nm) this was used to strain NMOS and PMOS channels separately (the electron-carrying and hole-carrying transistors of Before you start: electrons speed up in a stretched channel, holes in a squeezed one, so a tensile nitride was laid over the NMOS and a compressive one over the PMOS).
- **SiCN and SiCO** from trimethylsilane (3MS) or tetramethylsilane with NH3 or CO2: the copper capping/etch-stop layers between BEOL levels (k ~4.5–5.5, replacing SiN at k ~7), 20–30 nm thick, which must also block copper diffusion and survive CMP.
- **Low-k SiOC:H** ("carbon-doped oxide", Applied's Black Diamond, Lam's Coral-lineage films): methyl-substituted silanes (e.g. diethoxymethylsilane, DEMS) plus O2 give an oxide network with ~15–20% terminal Si–CH3 groups that lower density and polarizability, k ~2.7–3.0 versus 3.9–4.2 for SiO2. **Porous ultra-low-k** (k ~2.2–2.5) adds an organic **porogen** that is driven out by a UV cure at ~400 °C, leaving 20–30% porosity with ~2 nm pores. The price is a mechanically weak, moisture-absorbing film that plasma etch and **ash** (the oxygen-plasma stripping of photoresist) damage, which is why Module 12 spends time on sidewall repair and on airgaps as the alternative.

### 4.3 HDP-CVD

**High-density plasma CVD** solves the gap-fill problem for moderate aspect ratios by etching while it deposits. An **inductively coupled plasma (ICP)** source, in which a coil outside the chamber drives the plasma through its magnetic field rather than through two plates, runs at 1–5 mtorr and produces ion densities of 10¹¹–10¹² cm⁻³, ten to a hundred times a CCP, so SiH4 + O2 dissociates completely and deposits SiO2 rapidly. A separate RF bias on the **electrostatic chuck** (a plate that holds the wafer flat by electrostatic attraction and cools it with helium flowing behind it) accelerates Ar⁺ (and O⁺) ions into the wafer at 100–300 eV. **Sputtering** (knocking atoms off a surface by ion impact; it is also the mechanism behind PVD, Section 6) is fastest on surfaces inclined ~45°, because an ion striking at a glancing angle deposits its momentum close to the surface where it can knock atoms out, whereas at normal incidence the momentum is buried; the overhanging corners that would otherwise close the gap are exactly such slopes, so they are preferentially removed while the trench bottom, hit at normal incidence, keeps filling. The process is tuned by the **deposition-to-sputter ratio** (D/S: the deposition rate on a flat surface with the bias off, divided by the rate at which the bias alone sputters the film away, typically 3–10); too much sputtering redeposits material on the opposite sidewall and can clip the tops of the underlying features. The wafer heats to 300–400 °C from ion bombardment alone (backside helium cooling limits it). Recipe numbers on a 300 mm chamber: SiH4, O2 and Ar at 1–5 mtorr, source power of several kilowatts and a bias of a kilowatt or more (~), deposition rates of a few hundred nanometres per minute (~), and the D/S measured on blanket wafers at set-up rather than copied from a recipe, because it depends on the chamber's own ion flux. HDP-CVD (Lam Speed, Applied Ultima/Centura HDP) filled STI trenches and inter-metal gaps from the 250 nm to ~65 nm nodes, and still fills wide gaps, the **pre-metal dielectric** (PMD, the first insulator over the transistors, under the first wiring level), and 3D NAND **slits** (the deep trenches that separate blocks in the memory array); for narrow FinFET-era trenches it cannot keep up.

> **Worked example: what the deposition-to-sputter ratio does at a corner.**
> Suppose the bias-off deposition rate on a flat surface is 400 nm/min and the bias alone sputters oxide from a flat surface at 100 nm/min: D/S = 4, and the trench bottom, hit at normal incidence, fills at a net 400 − 100 = 300 nm/min. On a 45° corner facet two things change. The incoming flux is mostly vertical, so it deposits at only cos 45° ≈ 0.7 of the flat rate, about 280 nm/min; and the sputter yield at 45° is ~2–3× the normal-incidence yield (~), say 250 nm/min. The corner's net rate is ~30 nm/min, a tenth of the bottom's, so the overhang that would seal the trench never forms and the fill grows from the bottom up. Raise D/S to 10 (sputter 40 nm/min): the corner nets 280 − 100 = 180 nm/min, over half the bottom rate, and in a narrow trench it can still pinch off and trap a void. Drop D/S to 2 (sputter 200 nm/min): the corner nets 280 − 500 < 0, i.e. it is etched, the material sputtered off it lands on the facing sidewall (**redeposition**), and once the deposited oxide on the corner is gone the ions start eating the nitride hard mask or the silicon fin beneath (**corner clipping**). The usable window sits between those two failures.

> **What can go wrong:** *Corner clipping*: a D/S too low (too much bias) sputters through the oxide on the top corners and into the nitride cap or fin top beneath, seen in a cross-section under a **scanning electron microscope (SEM)** (an image at nanometre resolution made by scanning a focused electron beam over the sample; top-down SEM images are the routine inspection tool of the fab) as rounded, shortened features and, electrically, as fin-height and hard-mask-thickness variation. *Redeposition and voids*: material sputtered from one corner lands on the opposite sidewall as a low-density overhang that can trap a void just as a sticky CVD film would; the fix is a lower bias, a slower deposition, or a deposit-etch-deposit sequence with an in-situ NF3 etch between steps. *Plasma charging damage*: the ion flux and the bias charge any conductor that is not grounded, and a long polysilicon or metal line connected to a small gate acts as an antenna that pushes the collected charge through the gate oxide; caught by antenna test structures at electrical test and limited by design rules on the antenna ratio. *Helium leak at the chuck*: if the backside helium is lost the wafer overheats under the ion bombardment and the film's stress and thickness map change. Film quality is checked by wet-etch rate and by refractive index (Section 9).

### 4.4 SACVD, HARP, and flowable CVD for gap fill

At the FinFET nodes (16/14 nm and beyond) the STI trench between fins is 20–40 nm wide and 200–300 nm deep (aspect ratio 5–15); the space between DRAM **bit lines** (the wires that read and write a column of memory cells) and the spaces in 3D NAND are worse. Three approaches:

**Sub-atmospheric CVD (SACVD)** uses TEOS + O3 at 200–600 torr and 400–550 °C. Ozone attacks TEOS on the surface via a low-sticking-coefficient mechanism that gives near-conformal and even bottom-up, "flow-like" fill in narrow gaps, at the cost of a lower-density film that must be densified by a steam or dry anneal at 600–1,000 °C and shrinks a few percent in the process. Applied's **HARP** (high aspect ratio process) on Producer was the STI fill of record at 65–28 nm. The weakness is a seam where the sidewall growth fronts meet; a subsequent wet etch opens the seam into a slot.

**Flowable CVD (FCVD)**, introduced by Applied (Producer Eterna) in 2010 and now also offered by Lam and others, takes a different route: a silicon-nitrogen precursor (trisilylamine, TSA, (SiH3)3N) reacts with NH3 radicals from a remote plasma at low temperature (near or below room temperature to ~100 °C) to form an **oligomeric** (short chains of a few precursor units, so still liquid-like) Si–N–H film that literally flows into and wets trenches by **capillary action** (the same pull that draws water up a narrow tube), filling them seamlessly from the bottom regardless of aspect ratio. The film is then converted to SiO2 by an ozone/steam cure at 200–500 °C followed by anneals at 600–1,000 °C. Conversion of the buried Si–N–H to Si–O in a narrow trench is diffusion-limited and the film shrinks 10–20%, so a thin ALD oxide liner is deposited first, the cure recipe is a carefully guarded piece of know-how, and the top of the fill is always wet-etched back and re-capped. FCVD is the STI fill for FinFET and nanosheet nodes and is used in DRAM and 3D NAND wherever a narrow, deep dielectric fill is needed.

**ALD oxide** fills the very narrowest gaps by pure conformality (it closes a gap from both sides with a seam at the middle), and is used for liners, for the multi-patterning spacers of 5.6, and as the fill itself when the gap is < 10 nm.

> **Worked example: when does a liner pinch off?**
> A perfectly conformal film grows at the same rate on both sidewalls of a 30 nm wide trench, so the trench closes when each wall carries 15 nm and the two fronts meet, leaving at worst a thin seam. Now suppose the top corners grow 25% faster than the sidewalls, as they do with a sticky precursor (3.3). The corners meet when the sidewall film is 30 / (2 × 1.25) = 12 nm thick, and at that moment the walls below are still 30 − 24 = 6 nm apart: a void 6 nm wide, running the full depth of the trench, is sealed in. Voids are made by the *ratio* of corner to sidewall rate, not by the total thickness, and the only cures are a lower sticking coefficient, an etch-back that reopens the corners (4.3), or a liquid that fills from the bottom (FCVD).

> **What can go wrong:** A seam or void in an STI fill is invisible at the time and lethal later: the next clean in dilute **hydrofluoric acid (HF)**, the standard wet etchant for silicon oxide, wicks into the seam and opens it into a slot, the gate metal deposited afterwards fills the slot, and two neighbouring transistors are shorted or leak through the isolation. It is found by cross-section TEM (Section 9) in development and by electrical yield in production, and prevented by the ALD liner, the etch-back-and-recap of the fill top, and wet-etch-rate tests of the cure (a film that etches too fast in HF is under-converted).

> **Why it matters downstream:** The STI fill sets fin-to-fin leakage and gate-metal shorts in the transistor of Module 11, and its shrinkage sets the stress on the fins, which changes their carrier mobility. A fill recipe is never changed without re-qualifying the transistor.

### 4.5 Metal CVD: tungsten and titanium nitride

**Tungsten** has been the metal of the contact and via **plug** (the vertical metal column joining a transistor to the first wire, or one wire to the next) since the late 1980s because it deposits conformally by CVD and tolerates the 400–450 °C process. The chemistry has two stages, done on a Lam Altus (Lam holds the majority of this market) or Applied Endura/Centura W chamber:

1. **Nucleation layer** (2–5 nm): WF6 + SiH4 (or B2H6) at 300–400 °C, pulsed in an ALD-like sequence. This is needed because WF6 will not reduce on a bare dielectric and, worse, reacts with silicon (2 WF6 + 3 Si → 2 W + 3 SiF4), tunnelling into it ("wormholes") and spreading sideways under the oxide ("encroachment"), while the HF byproduct attacks oxides, and gas trapped under a film erupts as "volcanoes". Contacts are therefore first lined with Ti (to reduce native oxide and form the **silicide** TiSi2 at the bottom, a low-resistance titanium-silicon compound) and TiN (as the fluorine barrier and adhesion layer) before any WF6 sees the wafer.
2. **Bulk fill**: WF6 + 3 H2 → W + 6 HF at 400–450 °C, 40–300 torr, at hundreds of nm/min with excellent conformality. The film is polycrystalline with bulk resistivity ~10 µΩ·cm at thickness but rises steeply below 20 nm because of grain-boundary and surface scattering (a **grain boundary** is the disordered seam where two of the film's small crystals meet; electrons scatter off it as they do off a wall), which is the issue that drove contact metallurgy toward cobalt at some makers' 10/7 nm-class nodes and now toward ruthenium and molybdenum (Module 12).

> **Intuition:** *Why the metal changes when the wire shrinks.* An electron in bulk copper flies ~40 nm between collisions. In a wire 10 nm wide it hits the walls first, and in a film whose grains are 10 nm across it hits a grain boundary first, so resistivity climbs steeply once a dimension drops below the electron's natural flight length. Tungsten's flight length is shorter than copper's but its grain boundaries scatter strongly; molybdenum has a short flight length and clean boundaries, so its thin-film penalty is the smallest of the three. That, not bulk resistivity, is the whole reason the metal of the narrowest features keeps changing.

Fluorine is the enemy: it diffuses into gate dielectrics and the residual fluorine in a nucleation layer raises resistance. **Fluorine-free tungsten** (FFW) from WCl5 or WCl6 (solid precursors at ~130–170 °C) with H2 at 400–500 °C became necessary for 3D NAND word lines, where a 20–30 nm tungsten film must fill a lateral recess that is tens of nanometers tall and micrometers deep through hundreds of **tiers** (one tier is one word-line level of the stack), and for the gate fill in FinFET/GAA. Lam and Applied both offer it; the newest word-line and gate-fill metal is **molybdenum** from MoO2Cl2 or MoCl5, whose thin-film resistivity beats tungsten below ~15 nm and which needs no separate barrier, with Lam's Altus Halo as the first purpose-built tool (as of ~2025, Mo is in 3D NAND high-volume manufacturing at some makers and under evaluation for logic).

**Titanium nitride** is the universal glue, barrier, and metal-gate layer. Thermal CVD from TiCl4 + NH3 at 450–650 °C gives a dense, low-resistivity (~100–200 µΩ·cm) film with a few percent residual chlorine; **MOCVD** (CVD from a metal-organic precursor, one in which the metal is bonded to carbon- or nitrogen-based groups) from tetrakis(dimethylamido)titanium (TDMAT) at 350–450 °C, followed by an N2/H2 plasma densification per ~5 nm deposited, gives a chlorine-free film with more carbon and is preferred where chlorine attacks the underlying metal. At the finest dimensions both are replaced by ALD TiN (Section 5.5), where TiN's role in setting the transistor's threshold voltage is explained.

> **What can go wrong:** *Fluorine attack*: WF6 or its HF byproduct reaching the gate oxide through a thin or damaged TiN barrier shifts threshold voltages and degrades reliability; caught by electrical test, prevented by barrier thickness and a robust nucleation layer. *Nucleation-layer resistance*: the silane- or borane-reduced **seed** (a thin starting film on which the bulk fill grows; the word recurs for copper in Section 6) is fine-grained and fluorine-rich, and in a 20 nm plug it can be a large fraction of the total resistance, which is why its thickness is minimized and why fluorine-free chemistries exist. *Seams in word-line fill*: tungsten growing in from both faces of a NAND recess meets with a seam or trapped void that raises word-line resistance; found by cross-section TEM and at electrical test.

## 5. Atomic Layer Deposition

Why does a fab need a deposition method that puts down a fraction of an atomic layer per step and takes minutes to make 2 nm? Because at 2 nm nothing else is exact enough. A good CVD process holds thickness to ±5%; at 2 nm that is ±0.1 nm, the entire thickness of one ALD cycle. CVD films also thin on the way down a 100:1 hole and on the underside of a nanosheet, and most CVD needs heat the flow no longer allows. ALD gives thickness control by counting, perfect coverage of any shape the gas can reach, and deposition at 100–400 °C; it is now the method behind the gate dielectric, the metal-gate stack, the spacers, the multi-patterning films and the memory capacitor.

> **Intuition:** *A rubber stamp that prints only on unpainted surface.* Press the stamp once and the whole surface is covered exactly once, no matter how hard or how long you press, because ink cannot stick where ink already is. Rinse. Press with a second stamp whose ink sticks only to the first ink, and the surface is ready for the first stamp again. Count the presses and you know the thickness. ALD works this way, and the "stamp" reaches every surface the gas can reach, including the inside of a hole 100 times deeper than it is wide.

### 5.1 The two half-reactions

**Atomic layer deposition (ALD)** separates film growth into successive surface reactions. “Self-limiting” means the first reactant stops adding material once it has used the available reactive sites, even if more of that reactant arrives. The chamber is purged before the next reactant changes the surface and makes the next part of the cycle possible. Separating the reactants prevents them from reacting together in the gas phase; the film forms through their ordered reactions at the surface. The canonical example, trimethylaluminum and water depositing Al2O3, uses a surface that always ends in **hydroxyl** groups (–OH, an oxygen with a hydrogen attached, dangling off the surface). In the notation below ‖ stands for the solid surface, and the methyl groups (–CH3) on the aluminium are its **ligands**: the disposable arms on the precursor molecule that make it volatile and that fall off, one per reaction, at the surface.

- **Half-reaction A**: pulse Al(CH3)3 (TMA). Each TMA reacts with a surface –OH: ‖–OH + Al(CH3)3 → ‖–O–Al(CH3)2 + CH4↑. When every accessible –OH has reacted, the surface is covered with methyl groups, and further TMA does not stick (it has nothing to react with and does not decompose at the process temperature). The reaction is **self-limiting**: doubling the dose or the pulse time changes nothing.
- **Purge**: inert gas (N2 or Ar) sweeps out unreacted TMA and methane. If any TMA remains when water arrives, they react in the gas as CVD, producing particles and thickness non-uniformity.
- **Half-reaction B**: pulse H2O. ‖–Al(CH3)2 + 2 H2O → ‖–Al(OH)2 + 2 CH4↑. The surface is once again hydroxylated and ready for the next TMA pulse.
- **Purge** again.

One cycle deposits a fixed amount of Al2O3, the **growth per cycle** (GPC): ~0.1 nm (1.0–1.2 Å) between 150 and 300 °C. A monolayer of Al2O3 is ~0.3 nm, so each cycle deposits roughly one-third of a monolayer, not a full one, because the methyl ligands are bulky and **sterically block** neighbouring –OH sites (a big arm physically covers the sites next to it, so they cannot be reached until the arm is removed); the material is completed over several cycles. Thickness is simply N cycles × GPC, digital and repeatable to a fraction of an ångström, and independent of dose, flow uniformity, or feature geometry once saturation is reached. The **ALD window** is the temperature range in which both half-reactions saturate: below it the precursor condenses (or reacts incompletely), above it the precursor thermally decomposes (turning into CVD) or surface groups desorb.

Timing: a pulse of 0.05–1 s and a purge of 0.5–5 s, so a cycle takes 2–10 s in a single-wafer chamber and 20 cycles for 2 nm takes a couple of minutes. The slowness is intrinsic; equipment engineering is about shortening purges (small chamber volumes, high-conductance pumping) or amortizing them over many wafers (batch, or **spatial ALD**, where the wafer moves under continuously flowing precursor zones separated by gas curtains, so the "purge" is the travel time between zones). Good precursors are volatile (> ~0.1 torr below 150 °C), thermally stable at the process temperature, highly reactive with surface groups, and leave non-corrosive byproducts: chlorides (HfCl4, TiCl4, WCl5) are cheap and stable but release HCl; alkylamides (TEMAH, TDMAT, PDMAT) are more reactive at lower temperature but less stable; alkyls (TMA) are nearly ideal, which is why Al2O3 is the textbook case.

### 5.2 Thermal versus plasma-enhanced ALD

In **thermal ALD** the second reactant is a small molecule (H2O, O3, NH3, H2S) and the wafer temperature supplies the energy. In **plasma-enhanced ALD** (PEALD), the second reactant is a plasma (O2, N2, H2, NH3, or N2/H2) whose radicals react far more aggressively. PEALD extends deposition to lower temperatures (< 100–200 °C, needed on photoresist mandrels, the temporary lines of 5.6, and on low-k), gives denser films with less hydrogen, deposits nitrides that thermal NH3 cannot at low temperature, and shortens the **nucleation delay** (the first few cycles on a foreign surface often deposit little, because the surface has few reactive sites until some film exists; on an inert surface this delay can be tens of cycles). Its costs: plasma damage to underlying thin dielectrics, and lower conformality in very deep features because the radicals recombine on the sidewalls before reaching the bottom (a 100:1 3D NAND channel hole is beyond PEALD; thermal ALD is used there).

The plasma can be made in three places. A **direct** (in-situ) plasma is struck between the showerhead and the wafer, as in PECVD (4.2), at ~50–500 W and ~1–5 torr (~), so the wafer sees ions and ultraviolet light as well as radicals; it gives the densest films and the fastest saturation. A **remote** plasma is struck in a separate source (usually an ICP, 4.3) upstream of the showerhead, so mostly neutral radicals reach the wafer; it is gentler on the film beneath at the cost of some radical loss on the way. In spatial PEALD (5.1) the wafer moves under a plasma zone. A PEALD cycle looks like: precursor pulse 0.1–1 s, purge 1–3 s, plasma step 1–5 s, purge 0.5–2 s, so ~3–10 s per cycle (~), comparable to thermal ALD, but without waiting for a slow-desorbing molecule like water to purge, which is what makes low temperatures workable. Production PEALD films: SiO2 from an aminosilane and O2 plasma at 50–300 °C for the multi-patterning spacers of 5.6, SiN from DCS or another chlorosilane and N2 or NH3 plasma at 300–500 °C for gate spacers, TiN from TiCl4 and N2/H2 plasma for gate metals, and Al2O3 from TMA and O2 plasma at low temperature. The tools are the single-wafer PEALD platforms of 5.7 (ASM Eagle on the XP8, Lam Striker) and, for spacer-grade nitrides on many wafers at once, batch furnaces that add plasma electrodes inside the tube (~).

> **What can go wrong:** *Plasma damage*: ions of even a few tens of eV and the vacuum-ultraviolet light of an O2 or N2 plasma create traps in a thin gate oxide beneath and shift the threshold voltage; fixed by a remote source, a lower plasma power or a few cycles of thermal ALD as a buffer first, and caught by C-V (1.6) and reliability tests. *Bottom thinning in deep holes*: radicals recombine on every wall they touch, so the film at the bottom of a 50:1 or 100:1 hole is thinner and less dense than at the top; measured by cross-section TEM (Section 9) of test holes, and the reason DRAM capacitors and NAND channel holes use thermal ALD. *Composition drift*: the density, hydrogen content and stress of a PEALD nitride all move with plasma power and step time, so a chamber whose RF matching drifts makes a different film at the same recipe; tracked by wet-etch rate and refractive index. *Oxidation of the metal beneath*: an O2 plasma step oxidizes any exposed metal under the film (a TiN work-function layer, a copper line), so the first cycles on a metal use a milder oxidant or thermal ALD.

### 5.3 High-k HfO2

> **Intuition:** *The gate is a capacitor.* The gate electrode, the insulator and the channel form a capacitor, and the charge it holds per volt is the gate's grip on the channel: more capacitance per unit area means more carriers pulled into the channel for the same voltage and a sharper switch. For 40 years the way to raise capacitance was to thin the SiO2, until at ~1.2 nm the tunneling current, which rises exponentially with thinning, leaked more than the transistor could tolerate. A high-k film escapes the trap: it is physically thick, so almost nothing tunnels, but electrically thin, because capacitance depends on k/t and its k is five times that of oxide.

The numbers: C = k·ε₀·A/t, so a film of thickness t and dielectric constant k is electrically equivalent to SiO2 of thickness EOT = t × 3.9/k. For 2 nm of HfO2 with k ~20, EOT = 2 × 3.9/20 = **0.39 nm**. Add the ~0.5–0.7 nm interfacial SiO2 of 1.7, which contributes its own physical thickness, and the stack's total EOT is ~0.8–1.0 nm (0.39 + 0.5 ≈ 0.9 nm for these numbers): the grip of a 0.9 nm oxide, which would have leaked hopelessly, at a physical thickness of ~2.5 nm.

The film that made ALD indispensable is therefore the gate dielectric. Intel's 2007 HKMG process reportedly used **HfCl4 + H2O** at ~300 °C (HfCl4 sublimed from a heated solid source; Intel has never published its precursor); most of the industry moved to **TEMAH** (tetrakis(ethylmethylamido)hafnium, a liquid) or the dimethylamido analogue TDMAH with H2O or O3 at 250–300 °C, which nucleate better on the chemical interfacial oxide (**nucleation** is the forming of the first islands of film on a foreign surface; the sooner they form, the sooner the film is continuous) and leave no chlorine. GPC is ~0.05–0.1 nm depending on precursor and temperature; a 1.5–2.0 nm HfO2 film takes ~20–30 cycles. As deposited, the film is amorphous; a post-deposition anneal (700–1,000 °C, seconds) crystallizes it into the **monoclinic** phase (the crystal structure HfO2 takes at low temperature, k ~16–20). Nitrogen incorporation (HfON, HfSiON) or zirconium alloying tunes the phase and reliability. The ASM Pulsar (thermal ALD on the XP8 platform) is the tool of record for high-k in many logic fabs; Applied's Olympia and TEL's NT333 (a semi-batch spatial-ALD system) compete. DRAM capacitor dielectrics are the other huge high-k ALD application: each DRAM cell stores its bit in a hollow cylinder 1–2 µm tall and a few tens of nanometres wide, coated inside and out with a ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 ("ZAZ") stack at an aspect ratio of 50–100 that only ALD can coat, largely on batch tools.

### 5.4 Worked example: a 2 nm HfO2 gate dielectric

> **Worked example: cycles, time, and atoms for a 2 nm HfO2 film.**
> Take a GPC of 0.1 nm/cycle (upper end for TEMAH/H2O near 300 °C). Cycles needed: 2.0 nm / 0.1 nm = **20 cycles**. With a 0.3 s TEMAH pulse, 3 s purge, 0.2 s H2O pulse, 4 s purge (~7.5 s/cycle), deposition takes 150 s plus ~1 min of pump-down, pre-heat, and transfer: a single chamber makes ~15–20 wafers/hour, a 4-chamber tool ~60–80. A batch furnace running 100 wafers at ~40 s/cycle (long purges for the large volume) takes 20 × 40 s ≈ 13 min of deposition plus ~1 h of load/ramp/unload, still >100 wafers/hour.
> Atoms per monolayer: HfO2 has density 9.68 g/cm³ and molar mass 210.5 g/mol, so its formula-unit density is n = (9.68/210.5) × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ **2.8 × 10²² cm⁻³**. The mean spacing between Hf atoms is n^(−1/3) ≈ 0.33 nm, and one monolayer therefore contains n^(2/3) ≈ **9 × 10¹⁴ Hf atoms/cm²**. A 0.1 nm GPC is 0.1/0.33 ≈ 0.3 monolayer, i.e. ~2.8 × 10¹⁴ Hf atoms/cm² per cycle. The finished 2 nm film holds 2.8 × 10²² × 2 × 10⁻⁷ = **5.5 × 10¹⁵ Hf atoms/cm²**, about 6 monolayers. Over a 300 mm wafer (707 cm²) that is 3.9 × 10¹⁸ Hf atoms ≈ 6.5 µmol ≈ **1.2 mg of hafnium**; a precursor utilization of a few percent means a few tens of milligrams of TEMAH per wafer, which is why precursor cost is a real but not dominant line item.
> For comparison the Si(100) surface has 6.8 × 10¹⁴ atoms/cm², so the 20-cycle film is about 8 silicon-surface-equivalents of hafnium, and the ~0.7 nm interfacial SiO2 beneath it consumed ~0.3 nm (about two atomic layers) of the channel.

### 5.5 ALD metals and barriers

A metal's **work function** is the energy it takes to pull an electron out of it, and in a gate stack it sets the transistor's threshold voltage; NMOS and PMOS transistors need different threshold voltages, so they get different metals or different thicknesses of the same metal (Module 11). **TiN** by ALD (TiCl4 + NH3 at 350–450 °C, or PEALD with N2/H2 plasma) is the work-function and capping metal in every HKMG stack. In a **replacement-metal-gate (RMG)** flow (Module 11) the polysilicon dummy gate is removed late in the process and the real stack is deposited into the empty trench it leaves, ~15–20 nm wide. Into it must fit the whole stack: IL / HfO2 / TiN cap / a TaN layer that acts as an **etch stop** (so the layers above it can be stripped from one transistor type without touching those below) / TiAl for NMOS or thicker TiN for PMOS as the work-function layers whose choice and thickness tune the threshold voltage / TiN / W or Co fill, each layer 1–3 nm and conformal around a fin or, in a gate-all-around device, around and *between* stacked nanosheets separated by ~10 nm. Only ALD can do it. **TaN** (from pentakis(dimethylamido)tantalum, PDMAT, + NH3 or plasma) is also the copper diffusion barrier for the narrowest BEOL lines, where PVD TaN's overhang costs too much of the trench cross-section; the current approach at 3 nm and 2 nm is ALD TaN (~1–1.5 nm) under a PVD or ALD Co/Ru liner. ALD Ru (from various Ru precursors + O2) and ALD W (WF6/B2H6 or WCl5/H2) are seed and fill layers for the smallest vias.

### 5.6 ALD spacers and patterning films

The gate spacer must be conformal on a fin sidewall, have low k (it sits between gate and contact, adding parasitic capacitance), and survive multiple wet cleans and the **source/drain recess etch** (the etch that digs out the silicon beside the gate so that the epitaxial source/drain of Section 7 can be grown in its place). LPCVD nitride at 750 °C is too hot after the high-k and too high-k. The answer is PEALD or thermal ALD **SiN**, **SiCN**, **SiOCN**, and **SiOC** (from DCS, hexachlorodisilane or trisilylamine plus N2/NH3 plasma, sometimes with a carbon source, at 400–600 °C), tuned to k ~4–5.5 and wet-etch rates far below PECVD nitride. Nanosheet transistors also need **inner spacers**: the nanosheet stack is grown as alternating layers of silicon and **sacrificial SiGe** (silicon-germanium that exists only to hold the sheets apart and is removed at the end; 7.3), and after the SiGe is laterally recessed from the source/drain side, a low-k dielectric is deposited by ALD to fill the ~5–10 nm cavities under each sheet end and then etched back, leaving an insulating plug between gate and source/drain under every sheet. Only a perfectly conformal film can do this.

Multi-patterning (Module 07) is the other ALD consumer. **Self-aligned double and quadruple patterning (SADP, SAQP)** beat the resolution limit of the lithography tool with a trick: print a line, called a **mandrel** (a temporary line, usually of amorphous carbon or resist), coat it with a conformal spacer, etch the spacer off the flat surfaces so only the two sidewall coats remain, and remove the mandrel: two lines now stand where one was printed, at half the pitch; repeat and you have four. The spacer is ALD SiO2 or SiN deposited at < 100–300 °C, and its thickness *becomes the critical dimension* of the final line (the **critical dimension (CD)** is the width of the smallest feature printed on a layer, the number by which lithography is judged; Module 07), so 0.1 nm/cycle control is what makes a 20 nm pitch line 20 nm. Lam's Striker and ASM's Eagle/XP8 PEALD tools are built for this.

### 5.7 Batch versus single-wafer, and why ALD is now everywhere

The ALD tool market splits by geometry. **Single-wafer ALD** (ASM's Pulsar, EmerALD, and Eagle families; Applied Olympia; TEL Triase+; Lam Striker and Altus) offers fast cycles, easy plasma, and clustering with other steps. **Batch ALD** (Kokusai TSURUGI-C², TEL's TELINDY-based batch ALD, ASM A412) loads 25–150 wafers into a furnace-like tube and cycles precursors over all of them at once; cycles are slower but throughput per tool for thick films (DRAM capacitor dielectrics, 3D NAND liners, memory-cell nitrides) is far higher. Who leads each segment is in Section 10.

The number of ALD steps in a leading logic flow rose from a handful at 45 nm (high-k, a barrier) to dozens at 16/14 nm (spacers, SADP, work-function metals) to on the order of a hundred at 3 nm/2 nm. The reasons are structural: as features drop below ~20 nm, the only films thin enough, conformal enough, and precise enough are ALD films; nanosheets wrap every gate film around four surfaces of a ribbon; memory makers build capacitors and channels with aspect ratios in the hundreds; and low thermal budgets rule out most LPCVD.

> **What can go wrong:** *A CVD component*: a purge too short for the chamber volume leaves precursor in the gas when the second reactant arrives, the two react in flight, and the result is particles and a thickness that varies with where the gas mixes; the signature is a GPC that depends on dose or purge time, which a true ALD process never shows. *Incomplete saturation in deep holes*: the bottom of a 100:1 hole receives far less precursor per unit area, so a pulse that saturates the top can leave the bottom thin; fixed by longer pulses at the cost of throughput. *Decomposition above the window*: running hot for a denser film lets the precursor break down on its own, and thickness stops being digital. *Plasma damage*: PEALD ions and ultraviolet light create traps in a thin oxide beneath. Detection is by thickness maps (ellipsometry) and by **wet-etch-rate tests**: a film's etch rate in dilute HF is a sensitive proxy for its density and hydrogen content, and a film that etches too fast is not the film the recipe promised.

### 5.8 Area-selective deposition

The frontier, still a side note at its 2025 maturity, is **area-selective deposition** (ASD): making a film grow on one material and not its neighbour. The motive is **edge-placement error**, how far the edge of a via lands from where the wire beneath expects it; if the via metal could be grown only from the metal below (**via prefill**, growing the metal up from the bottom of the hole and nowhere else), or a dielectric only on dielectric, alignment would take care of itself. The mechanisms exploit ALD's dependence on surface chemistry: a **small-molecule inhibitor** (SMI) or a **self-assembled monolayer** (a one-molecule-thick coat that forms spontaneously on a chosen surface; aminosilanes such as dimethylamino-trimethylsilane on oxide, thiols or alkyl phosphonic acids on metal) is dosed to **passivate** the "non-growth" surface (here meaning to cap its reactive sites chemically so the precursor finds nothing to bond to; the same word names the protective passivation film of 4.2), ALD proceeds on the growth surface, and periodic gentle etch-back removes the few nuclei that form on the passivated area before they coalesce. Selective metal-on-metal (Ru, Co, W on Cu with no growth on low-k) and selective dielectric-on-dielectric are reported in limited **high-volume manufacturing (HVM)** use at leading foundries for via prefill and self-aligned patterning as of ~2025, with ASM, Applied, and Lam all shipping recipes; imec is the main public research center for it.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Two ALD thicknesses become other people's specifications. The SAQP spacer thickness *is* the line width in Module 07, so a 0.2 nm drift in the finished spacer thickness (about two cycles' worth on a 20 nm spacer) is a 1% change in every critical dimension on the layer. And the work-function metal thickness sets the threshold voltage in Module 11: a few ångströms of TiN is tens of millivolts of threshold voltage (Vt), which is why the ALD tools of 5.5 are qualified on wafer-to-wafer repeatability, not only on their average.

## 6. Physical Vapor Deposition

Sputtering is the crude member of the family and the one no fab can do without. It deposits a metal exactly as pure as the target it came from, at room temperature, fast, with no precursor chemistry to go wrong and no fluorine, chlorine or carbon left behind; it is the cheapest way to put down a pure, low-resistivity metal. Its one weakness is fundamental: the atoms fly in straight lines from a source above the wafer, so they land on whatever faces the source and miss the bottom of a narrow hole. Most of PVD engineering is about that weakness.

### 6.1 Sputtering mechanism

> **Intuition:** *Sandblasting with argon.* Fill a chamber with a whisper of argon, make a plasma, and hold a slab of the metal you want at a few hundred volts negative. Every argon ion that falls into the slab is a cue ball breaking a rack: it knocks a few metal atoms out of the surface, and they fly across the near-vacuum and stick to the wafer facing the slab.

**PVD** in the fab means **magnetron sputtering**. A target (a disk of the metal to be deposited, 300–450 mm diameter, bonded to a cooled copper backing plate; see the sputter-target section of Module 04) is the cathode, held at −300 to −700 V DC. Argon at 0.5–5 mtorr is ionized; Ar⁺ ions accelerate across the cathode **sheath**, the thin dark layer next to the target across which almost the whole voltage drops, so an ion gets its full kick there, and hit the target at several hundred eV. Each impact sets off a collision cascade (a chain of atom-on-atom collisions) in the top few nanometers that ejects target atoms with a few eV of energy. The **sputter yield** (atoms per incident ion) is 0.5–3 for common metals at these energies (Cu ~2.3, Al ~1.0, Ti ~0.5, Ta ~0.6, W ~0.6 at 500 eV). Sputtered atoms leave with a roughly **cosine** angular distribution (most near the perpendicular, few at grazing angles) and fly across the 50–400 mm gap to the wafer, where they condense. Whether they get there in a straight line depends on the **mean free path**, the average flight between gas collisions: for argon at room temperature it is ~5 cm divided by the pressure in mtorr (a rough rule of thumb), so at the low end of the pressure range a sputtered atom crosses a short gap with few or no collisions.

The **magnetron** is a set of permanent magnets behind the target whose field lines arc over the target face. In plain words, the magnets bend the plasma's electrons into loops that skim just above the target, so each electron makes many more ionizing collisions with argon before it escapes than it would in a plain DC discharge. The result is a plasma that runs at mtorr pressures (long mean free path) and at high rates (µm/min for aluminum). The price is uneven erosion: the plasma is densest along a **racetrack** under the magnet gap, and a static magnetron uses only ~30% of the target before the groove threatens to punch through; rotating magnet assemblies sweep the racetrack to reach 50–70% utilization, but target replacement every few thousand wafers is a routine consumable cost. Insulating targets need RF (13.56 MHz) power, because an insulating surface under DC simply charges up and repels the ions; alternating the voltage lets electrons neutralize it every half-cycle. Nitrides are made by **reactive sputtering**, flowing N2 with the argon so a Ti or Ta target deposits TiN or TaN; the catch is the **poisoned target**, where too much nitrogen nitrides the target surface itself, the rate drops sharply, and the process jumps between the two states with hysteresis unless the N2 flow is controlled in closed loop.

The recipe numbers for a 300 mm chamber: DC power of ~10–40 kW on the target (~), which at the −300 to −700 V above means tens of amperes of ion current; argon at 0.5–5 mtorr; a target-to-wafer gap of 50–400 mm; the wafer on an electrostatic chuck at room temperature to ~200 °C, or heated to 300–400 °C when a copper seed is meant to reflow (6.3); and deposition rates from a few nanometres per second for a barrier or seed step, so that a 2 nm film takes a fraction of a second and the shutter timing and power ramp matter more than the steady rate, up to ~1 µm/min for thick aluminium pads (~). In ionized PVD (6.2) the wafer is also given an RF bias that holds it a few tens to a couple of hundred volts negative (~), enough to steer metal ions to the via bottom and, at the top of that range, to resputter it. A 300 mm target is replaced every few thousand wafers, so target cost per wafer is a line item the fab tracks.

### 6.2 The step-coverage problem and its fixes

Sputtered atoms travel in straight lines and arrive from a wide range of angles, so a narrow via receives material mostly on its top corners (**overhang**, the lip that grows out over the opening) and upper sidewalls, little at the bottom, and almost none on the lower sidewalls. For a 20 nm wide, 60 nm deep via this is fatal if the film must be continuous. The fixes, in historical order:

- **Collimation** (1990s): a honeycomb plate between target and wafer absorbs atoms travelling more than ~10–20° off normal. Bottom coverage improves; deposition rate falls by 3–5× and the collimator clogs and flakes.
- **Long-throw**: a 300–400 mm target-to-wafer gap at very low pressure (< 1 mtorr) so that only near-normal atoms arrive. Wafer-edge asymmetry is the problem.
- **Ionized PVD** (I-PVD): an RF coil in the chamber ionizes 50–90% of the sputtered metal atoms in flight; a negative RF bias on the wafer pulls the metal ions in at normal incidence, so they reach the via bottom and, at higher bias, **resputter** the bottom (knock its atoms back off and onto the lower sidewalls), thinning the via-bottom barrier (which adds resistance) while thickening the sidewall (which is where continuity matters).
- **Self-ionized plasma** (SIP): Applied's Cu and Ta sources combine a very high-power rotating magnetron, long throw, and the fact that a dense enough copper flux ionizes itself, so no separate coil is needed. Endura "Ventura", "Cirrus", and "Amber" chambers are the current implementations for barrier, liner, and seed.

> **Worked example: why long-throw needs the pressure below 1 mtorr.**
> The fraction of atoms that cross a gap d without a single collision is exp(−d/λ), with λ the mean free path (~5 cm / P in mtorr, treating the sputtered atom like an argon atom; a fast heavy metal atom does somewhat better). Ordinary chamber, d = 5 cm at 1 mtorr: λ ≈ 5 cm and exp(−1) ≈ 37% arrive untouched; at 3 mtorr, λ ≈ 1.7 cm and only ~5% do, so the flux at the wafer comes from all directions and step coverage is poor. Long-throw, d = 30 cm at 0.5 mtorr: λ ≈ 10 cm and exp(−3) ≈ 5% survive, still too few; at 0.1 mtorr, λ ≈ 50 cm and ~55% survive. So a long-throw chamber must run at a few tenths of a millitorr or less, which is why it needs a magnetron strong enough to keep a plasma alive at that pressure, and why its deposition rate is low.

### 6.3 What PVD deposits in a modern chip

In the BEOL (Module 12), the **dual-damascene** metallization (etch both the trench for a wire and the via beneath it into the insulator, then fill both with copper in one go) runs on an **Applied Endura**: a cluster tool with a central wafer handler under high vacuum (10⁻⁸ torr base) and 6–8 process chambers, so that a wafer goes through the whole sequence without breaking vacuum:

1. **Degas** (150–400 °C) to drive moisture out of the porous low-k before any metal touches it.
2. **Pre-clean**: a soft Ar⁺ sputter etch or a reactive H2/He ICP clean to remove copper oxide from the via bottom.
3. **TaN barrier** (reactively sputtered, ~1–2 nm) and **Ta liner** (~1–2 nm), or ALD TaN then PVD Ta, to stop copper diffusing into the insulator.
4. **Co or Ru liner** (CVD/ALD or PVD, ~1–2 nm) to wet copper and enable **reflow**, in which a gently heated liner lets copper atoms migrate along it to the bottom of the via.
5. **Cu seed** (PVD, 2–20 nm depending on the level, thick at the top levels and vanishingly thin at **M1**, the first and narrowest wiring level above the transistors), the conductive, continuous layer the **electroplating** bath needs as its cathode when it grows the bulk copper from solution (Module 12).

At the tightest pitches (~20–24 nm at 3 nm/2 nm) the total barrier plus liner plus seed budget is ~4–5 nm out of a ~10–12 nm trench width (figures inferred from teardown analyses, not foundry disclosures), which is what motivates barrierless Ru or Mo lines and the interconnect changes discussed in Module 12.

> **Worked example: what the liner costs in a 12 nm trench.**
> Take an M1 trench 12 nm wide and 24 nm deep (aspect ratio 2) and give barrier, liner and seed together the ~4–5 nm budget above: ~2.25 nm on each sidewall and on the bottom. Copper then occupies (12 − 4.5) × (24 − 2.25) ≈ 7.5 × 21.75 ≈ 163 nm² of the trench's 288 nm² cross-section, 57%. The liner carries almost no current (TaN is ~200 µΩ·cm or more (~) against copper's 1.7), so the wire conducts like a 7.5 nm-wide copper line, and by the flight-length argument of 4.5 copper that narrow has a resistivity several times bulk. Now the coverage side: to leave even 1 nm of continuous seed on the lower sidewall with a sidewall step coverage of ~25% (~, an ionized-PVD figure) needs ~4 nm on the field, and the top corners, growing at ~1.5× the field rate, would carry 6 nm each and close half the 12 nm opening before the lower sidewall is coated. That arithmetic is why the M1 barrier is ALD, the liner is CVD or ALD cobalt or ruthenium, the copper seed there is only a couple of nanometres of PVD that reflows along the liner, and PVD copper seed in the 10–20 nm range is reserved for the wider upper levels.

Elsewhere PVD makes the Ti/TiN contact liner, the aluminum bond pads and top-metal redistribution layers, NiPt for **silicides** (the metal-silicon contact compound of 4.5), and the phase-change and magnetic-tunnel-junction stacks of the emerging memories (PCRAM and MRAM). Applied had shipped more than 4,500 Endura systems by 2010 and many more since, and is estimated to hold on the order of 80% or more of the semiconductor PVD market; Ulvac, Evatec, and, in China, Naura are the other names.

> **What can go wrong:** *Shield flaking*: the chamber shields that catch stray metal build up thick, stressed deposits that eventually flake onto the wafer; shields are swapped on a wafer-count schedule and the chamber is qualified with particle monitor wafers. *Arcing*: an insulating inclusion or nodule on the target draws a spark that splatters metal droplets onto the wafer; modern power supplies detect arcs in microseconds and cut the power. *A discontinuous seed*: where the seed thins to nothing on a lower sidewall the plating bath cannot grow copper, leaving a void that opens the wire now or after a few thousand hours; caught by plating-void inspection and by continuity at test. *Target end-of-life*: as the racetrack deepens the rate drifts and the thickness map changes; sheet-resistance maps of monitor wafers track the drift and set the replacement interval.

## 7. Epitaxy

Epitaxy has three jobs in a modern transistor flow. It grows the source and drain of every transistor as crystals that squeeze the channel to make its carriers faster. It makes those crystals so heavily doped that the metal contact on top sees almost no resistance. And, for the nanosheet transistor, it grows the starting stack of alternating silicon and silicon-germanium layers from which the ribbons are later carved. None of these can be done with a deposited film, because all three require the new material to be a single crystal continuous with the one beneath it.

### 7.1 Growing crystal on crystal

**Epitaxy** deposits a single-crystal film whose atomic lattice continues the substrate's, atom row by atom row, as if the crystal had simply kept growing. Silicon epi is done by CVD from silane, disilane, trisilane, or dichlorosilane (DCS) in hydrogen at 550–1,150 °C in a **cold-wall**, lamp-heated single-wafer chamber (only the wafer is hot, so nothing deposits on the chamber walls; Applied Centura Epi/Prime Epi, ASM Intrepid ES). The wafer rotates on a SiC-coated graphite susceptor (the heated plate it rests on) and the process pressure is 10–100 torr (**reduced-pressure CVD**, RPCVD) or atmospheric. The pre-epi clean is critical: any oxide or carbon on the surface nucleates defects, so a wet **HF-last** clean (the last wet step is dilute hydrofluoric acid, which strips the native oxide and leaves the bare silicon capped with hydrogen atoms) is followed by an in-situ H2 bake (800–1,000 °C for blanket epi; lower with a plasma or **SiCoNi**-type dry clean, Applied's NH3/NF3 chemistry that converts the last oxide to a salt and sublimes it off, for the low-thermal-budget source/drain epi). Module 03 covered blanket epi wafers; here the subject is the selective, doped, and strained epi grown inside the transistor flow.

### 7.2 Selective SiGe:B and Si:P source/drain

> **Intuition:** *An oversize brick in a wall.* Force a brick slightly too large into a gap in a wall and it pushes on its neighbours. Germanium atoms are bigger than silicon atoms, so a silicon-germanium crystal grown in a cavity beside the channel is an oversize brick: it wants to be larger than the silicon lattice it is locked to, and the channel between two such bricks is squeezed along the direction the current flows. Recall that a hole is a missing electron in the bond network that moves through the crystal like a positive charge, and that PMOS transistors conduct with holes while NMOS conduct with electrons (Before you start). Holes move faster in squeezed silicon. Phosphorus atoms are smaller than silicon, so a phosphorus-rich crystal does the opposite and stretches the channel, which electrons prefer. That is the whole reason the two transistor types get opposite source/drain crystals: compressive SiGe for PMOS, tensile Si:P for NMOS.

Intel introduced embedded SiGe source/drains in PMOS at 90 nm in 2003 and everyone followed. The mechanism: after the gate and spacers are formed, the silicon of the source/drain regions is recessed by etch into a **sigma-shaped cavity** (its sidewalls are angled crystal planes, so in cross-section it looks like the Greek letter Σ, with points reaching in under the spacer toward the channel), and **SiGe:B** (boron-doped silicon-germanium) is grown selectively in the cavity. Germanium's **lattice constant** (the repeat distance of the crystal) is 4.2% larger than silicon's, so a Si(1−x)Ge(x) film forced to match the silicon lattice in-plane is under **biaxial** compression (squeezed equally in both in-plane directions), and because it is pinned at the channel edges it squeezes the channel along the transport direction: a **uniaxial** (one-direction) stress of ~1–2 GPa, which raises hole mobility (1.6) by 50–100%. Germanium content rose from ~17% at 90 nm to ~30–40% at 22 nm and to ~50–65% (~, inferred from conference papers and teardowns) in the **tip layers** (the last, highest-Ge portion of the source/drain nearest the channel) at 5/3/2 nm, with boron up to ~1–3 × 10²¹ cm⁻³ (more boron than the crystal could hold at equilibrium, frozen in because at these low growth temperatures each boron atom is buried by the next layer before it can cluster or escape). The high Ge and B content also lowers the **contact resistivity** to the Ti-based silicide/germanide on top: contact resistivity is the resistance of the metal-semiconductor junction multiplied by its area, and 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² across a 20 × 20 nm contact is 10⁻⁹ / (4 × 10⁻¹² cm²) ≈ 250 Ω, comparable to the whole transistor, so at 2 nm this is a bigger benefit than the strain in a nanosheet, whose channel strain is partly lost when the sacrificial SiGe is removed. For NMOS, **Si:P** with phosphorus at 2–4 × 10²¹ cm⁻³ (sometimes Si:CP with ~1–2% carbon) is grown in the source/drain recess; the small P atom introduces slight tensile strain and the very high active doping gives record-low n-type contact resistivity (~10⁻⁹ Ω·cm²).

> **Worked example: strain and stress from the germanium fraction.**
> The lattice mismatch of Si(1−x)Ge(x) to silicon is 4.2% × x. For x = 0.3 (a 22 nm-era composition) the film wants to be 1.26% larger than the silicon it is locked to, so it is compressed by 1.26% in-plane: a strain of 0.0126. Stress is strain times the biaxial modulus, ~180 GPa for silicon (Section 8) and somewhat lower for SiGe, so σ ≈ 0.0126 × 180 GPa ≈ **~2 GPa** in the film. The channel between two such source/drains sees a fraction of that, and misfit dislocations or partial relaxation in later hot steps reduce it further, which is why measured channel stresses are quoted as ~1–2 GPa rather than the ideal number.

The epi is a **raised source/drain**: on a FinFET or nanosheet, the fin or sheet ends are too small to contact, so the epi is grown outward until the diamond-shaped crystals from neighbouring fins merge into a single low-resistance block on which the contact lands. The diamonds are a case of **faceting**: a crystal grows fastest on some planes and slowest on others, so a freely growing crystal ends up bounded by its slowest planes.

**Selectivity** is the key trick. Precursors: DCS or SiH4, GeH4, B2H6 or PH3, with **HCl** added, all in H2 at 550–700 °C (lower for high-Ge SiGe, which would relax or roughen at higher temperature; higher for Si:P) and 10–100 torr. Silicon nucleates immediately on exposed crystalline silicon but only after an incubation delay on SiO2 and SiN, as isolated amorphous nuclei; HCl etches those small nuclei (and the crystalline film) at a rate that depends on the surface, and at the right HCl:Si ratio the net rate on dielectric is zero while the net rate on silicon stays positive. Chlorine-containing precursors (DCS) provide selectivity with less HCl; silane-based **cyclic deposition-etch** (CDE) alternates a fast non-selective deposition with a short HCl (or Cl2) etch that strips the dielectric nuclei, giving higher-quality high-Ge films at lower temperature. Selective epi suffers from **loading effects** (the growth rate depends on how much exposed silicon there is nearby, because the precursor is consumed locally, so isolated and dense transistors get different source/drain volumes), faceting at spacer edges, and, in nanosheets, the need to grow from multiple stacked sheet ends and merge into a void-free block; the recipes are among the most closely guarded parts of any foundry's **PDK** (process design kit, the rulebook and model set a foundry hands to chip designers).

> **What can go wrong:** *Selectivity loss*: a slightly wrong HCl balance or a dirty dielectric leaves silicon nuclei on the spacer and STI, which grow into stray crystals that short neighbouring transistors; caught by top-down SEM inspection (the scanning electron microscope of 4.3) after epi. *Loading*: a dense block of transistors grows less source/drain than an isolated one and comes out with higher resistance; managed by dummy fins and by pattern-aware recipes. *Faceting and voids*: where the diamonds from neighbouring fins merge, a void can be trapped under the contact, and a facet at the spacer edge leaves the tip thin; seen in cross-section TEM. *Relaxation and defects at high Ge*: a film grown too thick or too Ge-rich for its temperature forms misfit dislocations, losing its strain and adding leakage paths; screened by X-ray diffraction and by the sheet resistance (Rs) and electrical test of monitor structures.

### 7.3 The SiGe/Si superlattice that becomes nanosheets

Gate-all-around transistors (TSMC N2, Samsung SF3/SF2, Intel 18A, all in or entering production as of 2025; Module 11) start with a blanket **superlattice** (a stack of alternating thin crystalline layers) grown by epi on the bulk wafer: typically three (sometimes four) pairs of Si(0.7–0.75)Ge(0.25–0.3) and Si, each layer ~6–12 nm thick, grown at ~550–650 °C by RPCVD with DCS/GeH4 or SiH4/GeH4, with abrupt, atomically flat interfaces and thickness controlled to a few ångströms because the Si layers *are* the future channels. The Ge content is limited to ~25–30% so that the SiGe stays below its **critical thickness**, the thickness above which a strained layer can no longer hold its stretch and snaps into misfit dislocations, at the total stack thickness; the Si layers are unstrained (they match the substrate) and the SiGe is compressive. The stack is then etched into fins, and after the dummy gate is removed the SiGe is stripped by a highly selective (>100:1) etch, historically wet (HCl/H2O2 or NH4OH/H2O2 mixtures) and now often gas-phase HCl or a dry plasma-free etch, releasing the Si nanosheets. Forksheet and CFET research (Module 11) uses taller superlattices with more pairs. Epi also grows **strain-relaxed buffers** (SRB: a thick SiGe layer deliberately allowed to relax, so that a silicon or SiGe channel grown on top is stretched or squeezed by the buffer instead of by the substrate) for SiGe-channel PMOS and the Si:C or high-Ge channel layers explored for future nodes.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The thickness of each silicon layer in the superlattice becomes the thickness of a nanosheet channel, and a nanosheet's threshold voltage depends on its thickness through **quantum confinement**: confining electrons to a sheet only a few nanometres thick raises their lowest allowed energy (the particle-in-a-box effect of school quantum physics, where a shorter box means a higher ground-state energy), and the thinner the sheet the larger the rise, so a thinner sheet needs more gate voltage to turn on. A few ångströms of layer-to-layer or wafer-to-wafer variation in this epi step is therefore a measurable spread in threshold voltage across the chip in Module 11, which is why the superlattice epi is specified to a tighter thickness tolerance than almost any other film in the flow.

### 7.4 Epi tools

A production epi chamber is a quartz-domed vessel with banks of halogen lamps above and below; the wafer rests on a SiC-coated graphite susceptor that rotates at tens of rpm for uniformity, the walls stay cool while the wafer is heated by radiation, and process gases flow laminarly across the wafer from one side of the chamber to the other. Because selective epi leaves silicon on the susceptor and dome edges, the chamber is cleaned in situ with HCl at high temperature between wafers or lots. Applied's Centura Epi (and the newer Centura Prime Epi) and ASM's Intrepid ES together own essentially the whole leading-edge silicon epi market, each holding roughly half depending on the customer (vendor and analyst estimates); ASM gained share with the Intrepid at the GAA transition. A selective source/drain epi chamber processes only ~10–30 wafers per hour, since each wafer needs a bake, a clean, a low-temperature growth of tens of nanometres with CDE cycles and a cool-down, so a 100k wafer-start-per-month fab, with several epi steps per wafer, has dozens of them.

## 8. Film Stress Engineering

Every deposited film is stressed, and the wafer bends in response. **Thermal stress** arises from the mismatch in thermal expansion between film and silicon over the temperature change from deposition to room temperature; **intrinsic stress** arises from the film's microstructure: **atomic peening** by ions (energetic ions hammer atoms into the film more tightly than they would settle on their own, which is compressive), grain-boundary voids and hydrogen loss (tensile), lattice mismatch in epi. Film stress σf is measured through wafer curvature via the **Stoney equation**:

σf = Es · hs² · κ / (6 (1 − νs) · hf)

where Es is silicon's Young's modulus and νs its **Poisson's ratio** (how much a material contracts sideways when stretched); their combination Es/(1 − νs) = 180 GPa is the **biaxial modulus** of Si(100), the stiffness that applies when a plate is stretched equally in both in-plane directions. hs is the wafer thickness (775 µm), hf the film thickness, and κ the change in **curvature** (1 divided by the radius of the bend), measured by scanning a laser across the wafer before and after deposition (KLA and Frontier tools). The sign convention: a tensile film, one that wants to shrink, pulls the wafer's edges up so the film side is concave; a compressive film bows it the other way.

> **Worked example: how much does a 1 GPa film bend a wafer?**
> Rearranging Stoney, κ = 6 σf hf / (M hs²) with M = 180 GPa. For a 100 nm film at 1 GPa on a 775 µm wafer: κ = 6 × 10⁹ × 10⁻⁷ / (1.8 × 10¹¹ × (7.75 × 10⁻⁴)²) = 600 / 1.08 × 10⁵ ≈ **5.6 × 10⁻³ m⁻¹**, a radius of curvature R = 1/κ ≈ **180 m**. The bow of a wafer of diameter D on a sphere of radius R is D²/(8R): for 300 mm, 0.09 / (8 × 180) ≈ 6.2 × 10⁻⁵ m ≈ **~60 µm** centre-to-edge; for 200 mm, ~28 µm. A wafer flat to a fraction of a micrometre leaves the deposition tool bent by more than the thickness of a human hair, and the lithography tool has to level it out again.

Stress is a tool: PECVD nitride can be tuned from −2 GPa compressive (heavy ion bombardment) to +1.5 GPa tensile and was used as a channel stressor; LPCVD nitride's ~1 GPa tension is what limits its thickness. Stress is also a problem: a 3D NAND stack of 200–300 alternating oxide/nitride layers, several micrometers thick, bows a wafer by hundreds of micrometers and distorts **overlay** (the alignment of one patterned layer to the one below it, Module 07), so makers deposit compensating backside films; low-k cracking and the delamination of thick tungsten word lines are stress-driven; and a 20 nm SAQP spacer at +1 GPa can bend a 30 nm-tall mandrel enough to shift the pattern.

The compensating film is a recipe, not a trick. Bow depends on stress multiplied by thickness (Stoney has σf·hf in the numerator), summed over every film on a face, and a film on the *back* of the wafer with the same sign of stress bends it the opposite way. So a 3D NAND maker that measures, say, 300 µm of bow after the stack deposits a backside film whose σ·h matches: by the worked example above, 1 GPa × 100 nm gives ~60 µm on a 300 mm wafer, so cancelling 300 µm needs σ·h five times that, for instance ~500 nm of nitride at ~1 GPa of the same sign as the front-side stack's net stress, or ~250 nm at ~2 GPa. The films are the PECVD oxides and nitrides of 4.2 with their stress set by the low-frequency RF fraction, deposited in a chamber that exposes only the backside; deposition vendors (Applied among them) sell backside-deposition chambers built for this (as of ~2025), and a furnace LPCVD nitride, which coats both faces of every wafer anyway, was the original version of the trick. The compensation is re-tuned whenever the stack changes and is often applied in stages, because the bow must be within the lithography tool's chucking range at *every* layer, not only at the end.

> **What can go wrong:** *Cracking*: a tensile film past its critical thickness (LPCVD nitride above ~100–200 nm, 4.1) cracks, usually starting at a sharp step, and the crack runs through everything above it; seen under optical inspection and fixed by staging the deposition or lowering the stress. *Buckling and peeling*: a compressive film that is too thick or poorly adhered lifts off in the wavy "telephone-cord" blisters that are the signature of compressive delamination, typically at the wafer edge or over a weak interface; fixed by an adhesion layer, a degas step before deposition, or a less compressive recipe. *Bow beyond the chuck*: a wafer bowed by more than the scanner's or the etcher's electrostatic chuck can flatten (a few hundred micrometres (~)) cannot be clamped or focused, and one bowed less than that still prints with overlay error; the curvature scan after every thick film is what decides whether a compensation step is added. *Stress that moves*: PECVD films lose hydrogen and change stress in later anneals, and metals creep at their process temperatures, so the bow measured at deposition is not the bow at lithography a week later; managed by measuring at the lithography step, not only after deposition. *Mandrel and fin bending*: the SAQP spacer case above, and tall fins bent by stressed gate films; found by CD-SEM as **pitch walking** (alternating wide and narrow spaces where equal ones were intended) and fixed by lowering the spacer's stress.

## 9. Film Characterization, Briefly

This section is a side note; Module 13 covers metrology properly. A deposition engineer lives by three techniques. **Spectroscopic ellipsometry** shines polarized light (190–1,700 nm wavelengths) onto the film at a glancing ~65–75° angle and measures how the reflection has changed the light's polarization (two angles, Ψ and Δ, at each wavelength); fitting an optical model to those angles returns the thickness to < 0.1 nm and the refractive index (a proxy for density, hydrogen content, and porosity) of any transparent film, and every furnace and ALD tool has a thickness map from a KLA, Nova, or Onto ellipsometer within minutes of unloading. **X-ray reflectivity** (XRR) shines a narrow X-ray beam at the film at a grazing angle and records how the reflected intensity oscillates as the angle is increased: the oscillation period gives the thickness, the angle at which reflection begins to fall gives the density, and how fast the oscillations die away gives the roughness, all with no optical model, making it the reference for ALD metals and high-k. **X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy** (XPS) shines X-rays onto the top ~5–10 nm and measures the energies of the electrons they knock out; each element, and each chemical state of it (Si⁰ versus Si⁴⁺, Hf–O versus Hf–Cl), releases electrons at its own energy, so the result is the composition and bonding of the surface layer, confirming stoichiometry and interfacial layers. Supporting methods: the **four-point probe** (press four needles in a line onto a metal film, pass current through the outer pair and read voltage across the inner pair to get sheet resistance), wafer-curvature stress (Section 8), and, for the final answer to any argument, a cross-sectional **TEM** image (a transmission electron microscope picture of a slice of the structure thin enough to see individual atomic layers).

## Summary

- A fab has five ways to put a film on a wafer, in order of decreasing temperature: thermal oxidation, epitaxy, CVD, ALD, PVD. The order is also the order in which the thermal budget shrinks through the flow.
- Thermal oxide grows *into* the silicon: the oxidant diffuses through the existing oxide and reacts at the buried interface, consuming 0.44 nm of silicon per nm of oxide. The interface is born clean, which is why Si/SiO2 is the cleanest electrical interface known and why silicon beat germanium.
- Deal-Grove: x² + Ax = B(t + τ); reaction-limited (linear) when thin, diffusion-limited (parabolic) when thick. Wet oxidation is ~25× faster because water is ~600× more soluble in silica, offset by ~12× slower diffusion and the factor of 2 in N1.
- Sub-2 nm oxides are made by ISSG/radical or plasma oxidation in single-wafer tools and calibrated empirically; every HKMG gate still keeps a ~0.5–1 nm SiO2 interfacial layer under the high-k.
- Furnaces are for steps where the film is the product; RTP is for steps where the thermal history is the product. √(Dt) turns "thermal budget" into nanometres: a 2 h furnace step spreads boron ~100 nm, a 1 s spike anneal ~2 nm.
- CVD has two regimes: surface-reaction-limited (temperature rules; batch LPCVD; Ea/kT² ≈ 2% per °C) and mass-transport-limited (flow rules; single-wafer showerheads; epi). The sticking coefficient sets conformality.
- The CVD family by job: LPCVD (poly, nitride, TEOS: hot, conformal, batch), PECVD (< 400 °C wiring-level oxides, nitrides, low-k), HDP and flowable CVD (gap fill), tungsten and molybdenum CVD (plugs and word lines; fluorine is the enemy).
- ALD is two self-limiting half-reactions separated by purges: ~0.1 nm per cycle (about a third of a monolayer), thickness = cycles × GPC, conformal in 100:1 holes, slow. Thermal versus plasma ALD; the ALD window.
- High-k HfO2 (k ~20) is physically thick but electrically thin: EOT = t × 3.9/k, so 2 nm of HfO2 is 0.39 nm of oxide equivalent; ~20 cycles make it. ALD steps rose from a handful at 45 nm to ~100 at 2 nm.
- PVD is magnetron sputtering: argon ions knock atoms off a target in a near-vacuum. Line-of-sight arrival is its weakness; ionized PVD and the Endura barrier/liner/seed sequence are the fixes. Applied holds most of the market.
- Epitaxy grows single crystal on single crystal: selective SiGe:B and Si:P source/drains for strain and contact resistance, and the Si/SiGe superlattice that is the raw material of nanosheet transistors.
- Every film bends the wafer (Stoney: a 1 GPa, 100 nm film bows a 300 mm wafer ~60 µm). Stress is both a tool (channel strain) and a problem (3D NAND stacks, low-k cracking).
- Deposition is about a quarter of fab equipment spend: Applied (PVD, CVD, epi, RTP), Lam (W/Mo, dielectric CVD/ALD), ASM (single-wafer ALD, epi), TEL and Kokusai (furnaces, batch ALD).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Thermal oxide is deposited on top of the silicon."** → It is grown *into* the silicon: the oxidant diffuses through the existing oxide and reacts at the buried interface, consuming ~44% of the oxide thickness in substrate silicon. This is why the interface is so clean and why the surface moves.
- **"Wet oxidation is faster because water molecules diffuse faster."** → H2O diffuses roughly ten times more slowly than O2 in SiO2; wet oxidation is 20–25× faster because water's solubility in silica is ~600× higher, so the concentration gradient driving the flux is far larger.
- **"ALD deposits one monolayer per cycle."** → A typical GPC of ~0.1 nm is only ~1/3 of a monolayer, because bulky ligands sterically block adjacent surface sites. ALD is "atomic layer" in its precision, not in its count.
- **"Since high-k replaced SiO2, there is no thermal oxide in the transistor anymore."** → Every HKMG stack retains a ~0.5–1 nm SiO2 interfacial layer under the HfO2; a bare HfO2/Si interface has unacceptable mobility and trap density. Thermal and chemical oxides also persist as STI liners, sacrificial oxides, and I/O gate oxides.
- **"PVD is obsolete at the leading edge."** → Copper seed, Ta liners, TiN, Al pads, MRAM/PCRAM stacks and many gate metals are still sputtered; ALD has taken thin, conformal layers but PVD remains the cheapest route to pure, low-resistivity metal, and Endura remains one of the best-selling tools in the industry.
- **"CVD tungsten is just WF6 plus hydrogen."** → Without a Ti/TiN liner and a silane- or borane-based nucleation layer, WF6 would attack the silicon and oxide (wormholes, volcanoes, HF damage) and would not nucleate on dielectric at all; the two-step chemistry and the fluorine-free (WCl5) and molybdenum alternatives exist precisely because of fluorine.
- **"PECVD at 400 °C is a cold process."** → It is the hottest step the back end of line allows: one degree more and copper diffusion, aluminum hillocks and low-k damage begin. It is "cold" only next to the 600–900 °C furnace films of the front end.
- **"ALD is too slow to matter."** → A 2 nm film takes minutes per wafer, yet a 2 nm-class logic flow contains on the order of 100 ALD steps and ALD tool spend now rivals PVD; the slowness is paid for in chamber count and batch tools, not avoided.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 03 delivered a polished (and possibly epitaxial) 300 mm wafer, and Module 05 carried it in a FOUP into the fab; this module is where the first films go onto it: the pad oxide and nitride for isolation, the STI fill, the SiGe/Si superlattice or fin material, the gate stack, spacers, source/drain epi, contact liners and plugs, and, later, every dielectric, barrier, seed, and cap of the ~15–18 metal layers. The inputs consumed are the specialty gases (SiH4, DCS, NH3, WF6, NF3, GeH4, O2, H2), liquid and solid precursors (TEOS, TMA, TEMAH, HfCl4, TSA, WCl5), sputter targets, and quartzware from Module 04, plus the dummy wafers from Module 03, and the tools are the Applied, Lam, ASM, TEL, and Kokusai systems that make up a quarter of a fab's equipment spend. Every deposition step is followed by patterning: the films are shaped by the lithography of Modules 07 and 08 and the etch of Module 09, doped by Module 10, and their thicknesses verified by the metrology of Module 13.
