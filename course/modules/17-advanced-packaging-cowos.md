# Module 17: Advanced Packaging: CoWoS, SoIC and Chiplets

A large GPU needs more than a powerful compute die: it needs a way to connect that die to nearby memory. Pick up an NVIDIA Blackwell **GPU** package (a graphics processing unit, the chip that does the arithmetic of AI training; the B200 is the Blackwell data-centre part) and you are holding five kinds of object glued into one. On top, under a copper lid, sit two ~800 mm² logic **dies** (a die is one rectangular chip cut from a 300 mm wafer) and eight stacks of **high bandwidth memory (HBM)**, the memory that feeds the GPU. Under them lies a slab of silicon carrying wiring but no transistors, the **interposer**. Under that is a 20-layer **organic** laminate (a polymer-and-glass-fibre board, the green material of a circuit board, as opposed to silicon), and under that a grid of solder balls.

The assembly exists because chip-making ran into three walls at once. A lithography scanner cannot print a die larger than 26 × 33 mm, so the biggest GPU must be two dies. Defects fall on a wafer at random, so the fraction of dies that survive falls exponentially with area. And each HBM stack needs ~1,600 signal wires entering the GPU through ~12 mm of its edge, which no organic board can route: its wires are ~10 µm wide and its **vias** (plated holes that join one wiring layer to the next) ~50 µm across, while HBM's contacts sit only 55 µm apart. Joining several chips on a wire-only silicon slab and then on a laminate is TSMC's **CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate)**, the subject of this module.

It is hard because the assembly is unforgiving. The parts alone (two GPU dies, eight HBM stacks, interposer, substrate) are worth ~$5,000–7,000 before assembly, and once the solder joints are made and locked in epoxy there is no taking them apart: one open solder joint among the ~250,000 microbumps under the dies and the tens of thousands of larger bumps under the interposer (the Anatomy section counts them) scraps the lot. The stack is a sandwich of materials that expand at different rates, so every trip through a ~245 °C solder oven bends it by hundreds of micrometres, enough to lift corner joints off their pads. And it is capacity-bound: from 2023 through 2026 the number of these assemblies TSMC could build each month, not the number of transistors it could print, set the ceiling on how many AI accelerators existed.

By the end of this module you will be able to draw the cross-section from lid to solder ball with the thickness of each layer; walk the six-step flow from bare interposer wafer to tested package and say why each step comes in the order it does; explain why the CoWoS-L variant exists and what nearly went wrong with it on Blackwell; explain what hybrid bonding does that solder cannot; and convert a headline "wafers per month" figure into GPUs.

## Before you start

- **Die, wafer and yield (Module 13).** A die is one rectangular chip cut from a 300 mm wafer that holds tens to hundreds of them; defects land at random, so bigger dies fail more often and yield (the fraction of good dies) falls exponentially with area.
- **The lithography field and the reticle (Module 07).** A scanner images a 152 mm square quartz **reticle** (the photomask) onto the wafer at 4× reduction; the largest rectangle it prints in one shot is 26 × 33 mm.
- **Copper damascene wiring and CMP (Module 12).** Chip wiring is made by etching trenches into an insulator, filling them with plated copper and polishing flat by **chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)**; all wiring in this module, including the interposer's, is built this way.
- **Plasma etch and the Bosch process (Module 09).** A plasma removes material where a mask leaves it exposed; the Bosch process makes deep, straight-walled holes in silicon by alternating short etch and wall-protection cycles.
- **Thin films: CVD, PVD, ALD (Module 06).** Chemical vapour deposition (gases react on the surface to leave a film), sputtering (atoms knocked off a target land on the wafer) and atomic layer deposition (one atomic layer per gas pulse) lay down films from nanometres to micrometres thick.
- **HBM (Module 15).** High bandwidth memory is 8–16 DRAM dies (dynamic random-access memory, the working memory of every computer) stacked on a base die with a 1,024-bit interface, sold as tested stacks with solder bumps on a 55 µm grid underneath.
- **Flip-chip, bumps, underfill, ABF substrate and CTE (Module 16).** A die is mounted face-down on solder bumps onto a laminate built from Ajinomoto build-up film (ABF); materials grow at different rates per degree (the coefficient of thermal expansion, CTE, quoted in ppm/°C, parts per million per degree, i.e. micrometres of growth per metre per degree: silicon 2.6 ppm/°C, laminate ~15), and underfill, an epoxy glue between die and substrate, makes the two bend together so the joints do not shear.
- **Capacitance and energy per bit (school physics).** A wire and its surroundings form a capacitor; sending a bit means charging it to the supply voltage, costing energy of order C·V² per transition, so longer wires cost more per bit.
- **Thermal conductivity and thermal resistance (school physics).** Thermal conductivity (W/m·K) says how easily heat flows through a material; thermal resistance (K/W) says how many degrees of rise each watt causes across a part. They are the heat analogues of conductance and resistance.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## Why the Package Became the Chip

> **Intuition: three walls.** Think of the three constraints as a print size you cannot exceed, a coin toss you lose more often the bigger the die, and a highway with 1,600 lanes that has to enter a 12 mm gate. The first is the reticle: the scanner's projector has one frame size, and a conventional single-field die must fit the frame; specialized wafer-scale designs use stitching across fields. The second is yield: every square millimetre is another toss of a slightly loaded coin, and the die survives only if every toss comes up heads, so doubling the area squares the odds of survival. The third is HBM: a memory stack talks over ~1,600 signal wires spaced 55 µm apart, and all of them must land on a die edge about 12 mm long. No one of these forced the industry to build packages out of several chips; all three together did.

### The reticle wall

A scanner exposes a rectangular **field** by sweeping a slit of light across the reticle while the wafer stage moves in synchrony (Module 07). The reticle is a 152 mm (6 inch) square quartz plate carrying the pattern at 4× its final size. Only a ~104 × 132 mm patch of it can be imaged without distortion, and 104/4 × 132/4 = 26 × 33 mm on the wafer. That 858 mm² is the **reticle limit**, the largest die that can be printed in one exposure, and it has not moved since the 1990s. NVIDIA's GH100 (the die inside the H100, the 2022 generation before Blackwell) is 814 mm², each Blackwell die is ~800 mm², and every large data-centre part since Volta (NVIDIA's 2017 data-centre GPU generation) has sat within a few percent of this ceiling.

High-NA **EUV** (extreme ultraviolet lithography, the 13.5 nm-wavelength scanners of Module 08) makes it worse. **Numerical aperture (NA)** is how wide a cone of light the lens gathers, and a wider cone resolves finer detail; ASML's EXE:5000 is the first EUV scanner at NA 0.55 rather than 0.33. Its optics are **anamorphic**, reducing 4× in one direction and 8× in the other, so the printed field is half as tall: 26 × 16.5 mm = 429 mm². Dies larger than that on High-NA layers require **reticle stitching**, two half-field exposures whose edges overlap by a few micrometres with matched **critical dimensions (CD)**, the widths of the smallest printed features. The reticle wall is a physical fact and the first reason logic must be split into pieces.

### The yield math of big dies

The second reason is statistical. A larger die gives a defect more area to hit. In the simple model used here, defects land independently like raindrops, at an average **defect density D0** per unit area, and any counted defect makes the die fail. This is a model for the calculation, not a claim that every physical imperfection destroys a real chip.

Let A be die area and Y the fraction of dies that survive. A·D0 is the average number of counted defects per die. The **Poisson model** gives Y = exp(−A·D0), the probability of receiving none; exp(x) means e raised to the power x. As the expected defect count rises, the surviving fraction falls. Units matter: D0 is quoted per cm², so an 800 mm² die is A = 8 cm², and at a mature-node D0 of ~0.1/cm², A·D0 = 8 × 0.1 = 0.8. Then an 800 mm² die yields exp(−0.8) ≈ 45%, a 400 mm² die exp(−0.4) ≈ 67%, and a 1,600 mm² die exp(−1.6) ≈ 20%. Splitting a design in two raises the good silicon fraction per wafer from ~45% to ~67% because each half is judged independently (Module 13 covers the models). The practical gain comes from testing and selecting the pieces before joining them: a defective half no longer forces an otherwise good neighboring half to be discarded with it. The eventual package still needs all of its selected pieces and their connections to work. The corollary is that a **chiplet** strategy (building one product from several smaller dies; the Chiplet section) only pays if the connections between the pieces cost almost nothing in power, latency and area relative to on-die wires.

### HBM's thousands of wires

**High Bandwidth Memory (HBM)** (Module 15) is a stack of 8–16 DRAM dies over a base die, and its defining feature is the width of its interface: 1,024 data bits for HBM2 through HBM3E, 2,048 for HBM4. Each bit toggles at 6.4–9.6 Gb/s in HBM3E, and the interface is meant to consume a few picojoules per bit. A **picojoule (pJ)** is 10⁻¹² J, and the scale to remember is that a link moving 8 TB/s (6.4 × 10¹³ bits per second) at 1 pJ per bit burns 64 W.

Why does the wire's length affect energy? A wire has capacitance to surrounding conductors: moving its voltage requires moving charge. Sending a 1 after a 0 means charging that capacitance C to the supply voltage V, which draws energy of order C·V² from the supply per transition. C measures how much charge the wiring stores per volt; V is the voltage swing in this simplified picture. The useful lesson is that more capacitance costs more energy each time the signal changes, even if the wire's resistance is small. Capacitance grows with length: a trace on a circuit board carries ~0.1–0.2 pF per millimetre (1 pF = 10⁻¹² F), so a 10 cm board trace is ~10–20 pF, while a few millimetres of fine interposer wire is only tens to a few hundred femtofarads (1 fF = 10⁻¹⁵ F), one to two orders of magnitude less. Energy per bit therefore tracks wire length, and HBM's few-pJ target is only reachable if the wires are short (a few millimetres), thin and numerous.

Numerous means the count of **I/Os** (input/output pins, one wire's connection point on the die). One HBM3E stack has on the order of 1,500–1,800 signal I/Os once clocks, **command/address** lines (the wires that tell the memory which row and column to read), **strobes** (timing wires that tell the receiver when to sample the data wires) and parity are counted, and ~5,000–6,000 **microbumps** (the ~25 µm solder-capped copper posts that connect a die to whatever is beneath it; fully described in the Anatomy section) including power and ground. An HBM stack is ~11–12 mm wide and butts against the GPU edge, so each stack's ~1,600 signals must enter the GPU through ~12 mm of edge, and the GPU's receiving circuits sit within ~10 mm of that edge. Eight stacks need ~13,000 signal lines.

Now the density arithmetic. Wiring is quoted as **line/space**: the width of a wire and the gap to its neighbour. The **pitch**, the centre-to-centre spacing, is their sum. An organic build-up substrate (Module 16) routes copper at ~8–15 µm line/space with ~50–60 µm microvias; at 10/10 µm the pitch is 20 µm, so a layer holds 1,000/20 = 50 wires per millimetre at best. A silicon interposer patterned with the copper damascene process of Module 12 on a **65 nm-class** process (a chip-making generation from ~2006, named for its smallest printed feature; its tools are old, paid-off and cheap, as the CoWoS-S section explains) routes at ~0.4–2 µm line/space; at 0.4/0.4 µm the pitch is 0.8 µm and one layer holds 1,000/0.8 = 1,250 wires per millimetre. That is a ~25× gap at the finest lines, 20–50× across the useful range, and it is the mechanism behind "HBM requires **2.5D**," the industry's term for dies placed side by side on an interposer. There is a second, blunter reason: even with enough layers, HBM's 55 µm-pitch microbumps cannot land on an organic substrate at all, because its smallest via is ~50 µm and the pad around a via is ~65 µm (Module 16); there is no room for a bump every 55 µm.

### Bandwidth per millimetre of shoreline, picojoules per bit

Two figures of merit organize the field. **Bandwidth density** is quoted as GB/s per millimetre of die edge (the **shoreline**, since side-by-side dies connect only along the edges that face each other) for side-by-side links, or per mm² of face for dies stacked on top of each other. **Energy per bit** is in pJ/bit. 2.5D, again, means dies side by side on an interposer or bridge; **3D** means dies stacked face to face.

A key to the table's rows: **BEOL** (back end of line) is the copper wiring built on top of the transistors inside a chip. **SoIC** and **Foveros Direct** are TSMC's and Intel's names for **hybrid bonding**, fusing two dies' copper pads directly with no solder (the SoIC section). **EMIB** is Intel's version of a small silicon bridge under two die edges (the Competitors section). **UCIe** is the open industry standard for chip-to-chip links inside a package (the Chiplet section); it comes in two tiers, an **advanced-package** version for interposers and bridges and a **standard-package** version for organic substrates, which is why it appears in two rows below (the Chiplet section gives their numbers). **C4** bumps (controlled collapse chip connection) are the ~100 µm solder bumps under an interposer or die, **RDL** (redistribution layer) is the interposer's own wiring, layers whose only job is to re-route connections from one pattern of contact points to another (the Anatomy section), and **ABF** is the epoxy film an organic substrate's layers are made of (Module 16). **Infinity Fabric (IF)** is AMD's on-package link. A **SerDes** (serializer/deserializer) is a circuit that packs many bits onto one very fast wire and unpacks them at the far end, used whenever wires are scarce, as on a board or in a cable; **NVLink** is NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU SerDes link and **PCIe** the standard board-level one. An **800G transceiver** is a pluggable optical module carrying 800 Gb/s, and **COUPE** is TSMC's optical engine that sits on the package instead (the Two Handoffs section). Approximate values as of ~2025:

| Link type | Physical medium | Bump/pad pitch | Bandwidth density (edge) | Energy per bit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| On-die wire | BEOL Cu | n/a | Effectively unlimited | ~0.01–0.1 pJ/bit |
| Hybrid-bonded 3D (SoIC, Foveros Direct) | Cu-Cu direct | 6–9 µm | Areal: >1 TB/s per mm² | ~0.05–0.2 pJ/bit |
| 2.5D on Si interposer or bridge (CoWoS, EMIB; UCIe advanced) | Cu pillar µbump on Si RDL | 25–55 µm | ~0.5–1.3 TB/s/mm | ~0.25–0.6 pJ/bit |
| HBM3E interface (2.5D) | µbumps on interposer | ~50–55 µm | ~100 GB/s per mm of stack edge | ~2.5–4 pJ/bit incl. DRAM I/O |
| Organic substrate die-to-die (UCIe standard, AMD Ryzen IF) | C4 bumps on ABF | 100–130 µm | ~30–200 GB/s/mm | ~0.5–2 pJ/bit |
| Board-level SerDes (NVLink, PCIe 5/6) | PCB traces, connectors | n/a | ~10–50 GB/s/mm | ~5–10 pJ/bit |
| Pluggable optics (800G transceiver) | Fibre | n/a | Limited by how many plug sockets fit on the switch's front panel | ~15–30 pJ/bit |
| Co-packaged optics on CoWoS (COUPE) | Fibre to package edge | n/a | ~1.6 Tb/s per engine | ~5 pJ/bit or lower (target) |

Every step a signal takes off the package costs roughly 10× in energy per bit. The reason is the one above, plus one more: longer wires mean more capacitance to charge, and once a wire is long enough to distort the signal (a board trace at tens of Gb/s), each end needs SerDes circuits that equalize and re-time it, and those circuits burn far more than the wire itself. Packaging technology exists to keep the fattest data flows on the top rows of the table.

> **Worked example: the HBM power budget, and why GDDR could not do it.** A B200 has eight HBM3E stacks delivering ~8 TB/s, i.e. 6.4 × 10¹³ bits per second. At a realistic ~3 pJ/bit for the HBM3E interface (DRAM I/O plus the **PHY**, the transmit and receive circuits that drive and sense the wires, over a few mm of interposer wire), running flat out costs 6.4 × 10¹³ × 3 × 10⁻¹² ≈ 190 W, a large but survivable slice of a 1,000 W package, and real workloads rarely sustain peak bandwidth. **GDDR** is the graphics memory soldered around a gaming GPU on the circuit board; delivering the same 8 TB/s from GDDR-class memory over an organic package and board at ~7 pJ/bit would be ~450 W for memory traffic alone, before the impossibility of fitting the hundreds of 20+ Gb/s pins along the die edge. HBM4 pushes the other way: it doubles the width to 2,048 bits and holds per-pin speed near 8 Gb/s, giving ~2 TB/s per stack at lower energy per bit, at the cost of ~2× the signal I/Os and a base die built on a logic process rather than a DRAM one. More wires, shorter and slower, is the consistent direction.

## Anatomy of a CoWoS Package, Top to Bottom

Before the history and the variants, here is the object itself, as a vertical section through a Blackwell-class part, from the cooler down to the circuit board. Every later section refers back to one of these layers.

1. **Lid.** A ~2–3 mm nickel-plated copper plate covering the whole package top. It spreads heat from the dies to the cooler, protects them, and gives the cold plate (the water-cooled copper block of Module 19) a flat face to press on.
2. **TIM1.** A **thermal interface material** is a soft heat conductor that fills the microscopic air gaps between two solid faces; TIM1 is the layer between the die backs and the lid (TIM2 is between lid and cooler). Indium solder, a filled polymer or a liquid-metal alloy, 50–100 µm thick.
3. **Dies.** Two GPU dies of ~0.7 mm-thick silicon, face-down, and eight HBM stacks (8–16 thinned DRAM dies on a base die) of nearly the same total height, so that one flat lid can touch all of them. The gaps between them are filled with **mold compound**, an epoxy loaded with silica powder that is pressed around the dies and hardens into a rigid slab, ground back so the die backs are exposed.
4. **Microbumps.** Each die's face carries copper pillars ~20–30 µm tall and ~25 µm across, capped with ~10–15 µm of tin-silver solder, on a 40–55 µm grid; after joining, the die stands ~25–35 µm above the interposer. **Pitch** is the centre-to-centre spacing of neighbouring bumps, and it sets the count: at 45 µm pitch one square millimetre holds (1,000/45)² ≈ 490 bumps, so an ~800 mm² die can carry ~100,000 of them.
5. **Interposer.** A ~100 µm-thick slab of silicon, 48 × 58 mm on Blackwell. On its top face are four or five layers of copper RDL wiring (the redistribution layers defined above: wiring whose only job is to re-route connections from one pattern of contact points to another) at 0.4–2 µm line/space. Through it run **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, copper-filled holes ~10 µm across and ~100 µm deep, one per connection that has to cross the slab. On its bottom face a single coarse RDL layer spreads the TSVs out to **C4 pads**, the landing pads for the next layer of solder.
6. **C4 bumps.** Solder bumps ~80–100 µm in diameter on a 130–180 µm pitch, tens of thousands of them, joining the interposer's bottom to the substrate.
7. **Substrate.** An organic **build-up laminate** 2–3 mm thick and 80–100 mm on a side: a ~1 mm rigid **core** of glass-fibre cloth in epoxy, with 10–12 **build-up layers** on each side, each a ~30 µm sheet of **ABF (Ajinomoto build-up film)** epoxy carrying ~10 µm copper lines and laser-drilled vias (Module 16). Its expansion rate is the number that dominates the rest of this module. **CTE** is the coefficient of thermal expansion, the fractional growth per degree; 2.6 ppm/°C means 2.6 µm of growth per metre per degree. The values below recur throughout.
8. **BGA balls.** A **ball grid array**: several thousand solder balls of SAC305 (tin with 3% silver and 0.5% copper, the standard lead-free solder) ~0.6 mm in diameter on a ~1 mm pitch, joining the substrate to the board.
9. **Board.** The GPU module's printed circuit board (the SXM module of Module 19, NVIDIA's GPU module form factor), whose millimetre-scale features the package has now stepped down to.

| Material | Where it sits in the stack | CTE (ppm/°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Silicon | Dies, interposer, bridge chiplets (CoWoS-L) | 2.6 |
| Copper | Wiring, pillars, TSVs, lid | ~17 |
| SnAg / SAC solder | Microbumps, C4, BGA | ~22 |
| Epoxy mold compound | Around the dies and, in CoWoS-L, the interposer | ~8–12 below its glass transition (the softening temperature, ~150–200 °C), more above |
| ABF build-up layers (in-plane) | Substrate wiring layers | ~15–20 |
| Glass-epoxy core | Substrate core | ~10–15 |
| Polyimide RDL | CoWoS-R and CoWoS-L interposers | ~20–50 |
| Organic substrate as a whole (in-plane) | Substrate | ~10–17 |

> **Intuition: the scale of the thing.** The interposer is as thick as a sheet of printer paper (~100 µm) and a little smaller than a playing card, and it carries wiring so fine that 175 of its 0.4 µm lines would fit across one human hair (~70 µm). The microbumps sit about as close together as the pixels on a phone screen. The two GPU dies dissipate ~1,000 W over ~16 cm², the area of a large postage stamp, which is ~10× the heat flux of a kitchen hotplate. Everything about CoWoS follows from those three facts: paper-thin silicon that must be handled, wires that must line up to a fraction of a micrometre, and heat that must leave through a lid.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Every layer in the list above is a thermal resistance in series: heat from the transistors crosses ~0.7 mm of silicon, 50–100 µm of TIM1, ~2–3 mm of copper lid, then TIM2 and the cold plate. Module 19 adds these up to a junction-to-coolant resistance (the junction is the transistor layer, the hottest point) of ~0.05–0.1 K/W, and the thickness of each layer chosen here, especially the TIM1 bond line, is a term in that sum.

## CoWoS: A Short History

TSMC's first 2.5D product was not a GPU. In 2011 Xilinx shipped the Virtex-7 2000T, four 28 nm **FPGA** dies (an FPGA is a chip whose logic is wired up after manufacture by loading a configuration) side by side on a passive 65 nm silicon interposer with more than 10,000 die-to-die connections. Xilinx called it Stacked Silicon Interconnect; TSMC generalized the process and in 2012 named it CoWoS. The interposer started as a 775 µm wafer and was thinned to ~100 µm to reveal its TSVs; the flow, chip-on-wafer first and then wafer-on-substrate, is the one used for a Blackwell today.

The GPU era began with HBM. AMD's Fiji (Radeon Fury X, 2015) was the first GPU with HBM, but its interposer came from **UMC** (Taiwan's second foundry) and assembly from **ASE** (the world's largest outsourced assembly house). NVIDIA's Tesla P100 (2016) put a 610 mm² GP100 die and four HBM2 stacks on CoWoS, and every NVIDIA data-centre GPU since (V100, A100, H100, B200 and Rubin, the generation after Blackwell) has used it, as have Google's **TPUs** (its tensor processing units, the in-house AI chips), AMD's MI250/MI300, Broadcom's and Marvell's custom AI **ASICs** (application-specific integrated circuits, chips designed for one customer's workload), and Amazon's Trainium (its in-house training accelerator). That is how one company's back-end capacity became the industry's bottleneck.

## The Three CoWoS Variants

The problem all three variants solve is the same: connect dies with wiring ~25× denser than a laminate can hold, over an area 3–10× larger than any single lithography exposure, and keep the whole sandwich flat and electrically sound through several trips to 245 °C. They differ in what the interposer is made of.

> **Intuition: three breadboards.** CoWoS-S is a breadboard carved from silicon: every wire on it is as fine as chip wiring, everywhere, whether it needs to be or not. CoWoS-R is a plastic breadboard, cheap and flexible, with wires five times coarser. CoWoS-L is the plastic breadboard with small silicon patches glued in only where the fine wires must be, under the seams where a GPU meets an HBM stack or another GPU. Each step trades cost and mechanical fit against uniformity.

### CoWoS-S: the silicon interposer

**CoWoS-S** (S for silicon) uses a full-size passive silicon interposer, made on a 300 mm wafer on **depreciated** 65 nm-class tools: machines bought around 2006 and long since paid off, so an interposer wafer costs a fraction of a logic wafer. It has four or five RDL layers at 0.4–2 µm line/space, microbump pads on top, and TSVs of ~10 µm diameter and ~100 µm depth connecting the front wiring to backside C4 pads. It contains no transistors, but it can contain **deep-trench capacitors** (TSMC's iCAP option, hundreds of nF per mm²): capacitors made by etching narrow trenches into the silicon and lining them with dielectric and electrode, which pack a large capacitance into a small footprint. Their job is **decoupling**. When millions of transistors switch in the same nanosecond the die draws a current spike that the substrate's wires cannot deliver in time, so the supply voltage sags; a capacitor sitting 100 µm below the die is a local charge reservoir that fills the gap. The arithmetic is Q = C·ΔV, the charge a capacitor gives up equals its capacitance times the voltage drop you are willing to allow: hundreds of nF per mm² means a few µF under a GPU, and a few µF × a tolerable ~50 mV of sag ≈ 0.1–0.2 µC of charge, which at 100 A (t = Q/I) lasts ~1–2 ns. That is exactly the timescale the substrate cannot cover. Its supply wires are millimetres long, and a long wire has **inductance**, the property of a conductor that opposes any change in the current through it (as mass opposes a change in speed), so the current from the substrate cannot ramp up in a nanosecond; it takes over on a timescale of ~tens of nanoseconds, by which time the interposer's capacitors must have carried the spike. This is an underappreciated advantage of silicon over organic.

The interposer is much bigger than a reticle, and its size is quoted as a **reticle multiple**, the interposer area divided by 858 mm². H100's is ~2.5× (~2,100–2,200 mm²); the 2024–2026 generation is 3.3× (~2,800 mm²); TSMC has qualified 5.5× (~4,700 mm², 12 HBM stacks) and roadmapped 9.5× (~8,000 mm² on a 120 × 150 mm substrate) for 2027. The lithography tool here is a **stepper**, the older, simpler cousin of the scanner: it exposes one whole field at a time from a stationary reticle instead of sweeping a slit across it, with the same 26 × 33 mm field, and the interposer's 0.4 µm wiring needs nothing better. Since the stepper exposes 858 mm² at a time, every RDL layer is patterned with multiple **stitched** exposures: the layout is split into fields, each exposed separately, with wires crossing a boundary drawn with overlap. **Overlay** is how precisely one exposure lines up with another, in nanometres, and here it is unforgiving: a 0.4 µm wire that crosses a stitch boundary offset by a quarter of its width loses a quarter of its cross-section, raising its resistance and risking an open, so stitching 0.4 µm lines demands overlay well under 100 nm.

The drawbacks scale with size. A 2,800 mm² interposer means ~16 per wafer, and one killer defect in the RDL or a TSV scraps the whole piece. Silicon (CTE 2.6 ppm/°C) is also a poor thermal-expansion match to the organic substrate below (~10–17 ppm/°C), manageable at H100 scale and increasingly not beyond.

### CoWoS-R: the organic RDL interposer

**CoWoS-R** (R for RDL) replaces the silicon with an organic RDL interposer built with TSMC's **InFO** fan-out process, in which dies are embedded in epoxy and wiring is built on top of them (the Fan-Out section describes it fully). The dielectric is **polyimide**, a heat-resistant polymer film (the orange material of flexible cables), with copper at ~2 µm line/space in several (up to ~6) RDL layers, no TSVs and no TIVs: each layer's vias (~5–10 µm holes opened in the polyimide) drop straight down through the stack to the C4 pads, so the whole interposer is a thin stack of polymer and copper built layer by layer on a carrier and released from it, exactly as the Fan-Out section describes. It is cheaper and better CTE-matched to the substrate. It also has lower line capacitance, because polyimide's **dielectric constant** (the factor by which a material filling the gap between two conductors multiplies their capacitance, relative to empty space) is ~3 against ~4 for silicon dioxide and there is no conductive silicon beneath the wires to act as the capacitor's other plate. Against that, it has ~5× lower routing density and is harder to keep flat over a large area: with no rigid silicon backbone, a polymer expanding at 20–50 ppm/°C bends freely and is held flat only by the dies and mold above it. What goes wrong is what goes wrong with any fan-out RDL (the Fan-Out section): cracks where a 2 µm line crosses a die edge onto a region with a different stiffness, moisture absorbed by the polyimide flashing to steam at reflow, and warpage-driven C4 opens at the corners. TSMC names no customers for it; it serves lower-I/O AI and networking ASICs with moderate HBM counts, and its real importance is as the stepping stone: the polyimide RDL, the carrier build and the release step are exactly the organic half of CoWoS-L. This module treats it as that stepping stone, in this one section, and gives the full treatment to -S and -L.

### CoWoS-L: silicon bridges in an organic interposer

**CoWoS-L** (L for local silicon interconnect) is the hybrid: an organic RDL interposer the size of the whole package, with small silicon **LSI (Local Silicon Interconnect)** bridge chiplets embedded exactly where fine-pitch routing is needed, under the edges where a GPU meets an HBM stack or where two GPU dies meet. Each LSI is a few millimetres by ten or so, fabricated on 65 nm-class tools with the same sub-micron RDL as a CoWoS-S interposer, optionally with deep-trench capacitors. Vertical connections for power, ground and slow signals go through **through-InFO vias (TIVs)**: copper pillars ~100 µm or taller, plated before the mold compound is applied and revealed by grinding. The mold compound here is the same silica-filled epoxy as in the Anatomy section, ~70–90% silica powder by weight, pressed around the parts and cured into a rigid slab (Module 16).

The interposer is built bottom-up on a **carrier**, a temporary rigid glass or silicon wafer that the structure is built on and later released from, which turns it into a **reconstituted** wafer, an artificial 300 mm disc made by placing pieces on a carrier and molding them into one body:

1. Start with a carrier wafer.
2. Build the bottom RDL layers (the future C4 side) on it.
3. Plate the TIVs as tall copper pillars through a thick mask of **photoresist** (the light-sensitive polymer that lithography patterns, Module 07): where the resist has been opened, copper grows up out of the hole.
4. Place and attach the LSI bridges at the positions where die edges will later meet.
5. Apply mold compound over bridges and pillars and cure it.
6. Grind the mold back until the TIV tops and the LSI wiring are exposed and flat.
7. Build the top RDL layers over everything, bridges and mold alike, up to the microbump pads.
8. Only then attach the GPU and HBM dies (the chip-on-wafer step of the next section).

Silicon has been reduced to the few percent of area where it earns its keep.

> **Worked example: interposer area and stitched reticles for a B200-class package.** Two ~800 mm² GPU dies (about 26 × 31 mm, long edges facing so the die-to-die link runs along ~30 mm of shared edge) plus eight HBM3E stacks of ~11 × 12 mm ≈ 130 mm²: 1,600 + 1,040 = 2,640 mm² of die. Add ~1 mm HBM-to-GPU gaps, ~1–2 mm between HBM stacks, and ~2 mm of margin for C4 fan-out and the dicing street (the gap between sites where the saw will cut); two rows of four HBM flanking the GPUs gives roughly 48 × 58 mm ≈ 2,800 mm², or 2,800 / 858 ≈ 3.3 reticles, an illustrative Blackwell-class footprint estimate. TSMC describes its first volume CoWoS-L platform as 3.5× reticle; the calculation here is a simplified floorplan, not a disclosed B200 mask layout. Since the field is 26 × 33 mm, each RDL layer of a 48 × 58 mm interposer needs a 2 × 2 array of stitched exposures (covering 52 × 66 mm with overlapping edges), so a five-layer RDL is twenty stitched fields per interposer, each stitch line held to the tolerance of the 0.4 µm lines crossing it. A 300 mm wafer (~70,700 mm²) fits ~16 such rectangles after edge exclusion, versus ~29 for H100's 2.5× interposer. The 5.5× generation (~4,700 mm², ~60 × 78 mm) needs a 3 × 3 array and fits ~9 per wafer; 9.5× (~8,000 mm², ~75 × 108 mm) fits ~4, which is the arithmetic pushing TSMC to panels.

NVIDIA's Blackwell B200 is the flagship CoWoS-L product. It needed two ~800 mm² dies with a 10 TB/s **NV-HBI** link across their shared edge (NVIDIA's proprietary die-to-die interface, described in the Chiplet section), plus eight HBM3E stacks. A 3.3× reticle CoWoS-S interposer would have been the most expensive piece of silicon in the package after the GPU dies; bridges cut that cost and removed most of the silicon-to-organic mismatch. Blackwell Ultra (B300) and Rubin are on CoWoS-L, and the 5.5× generation for 12-HBM parts was in volume production in 2026 with yields TSMC put above 98% at its 2026 Technology Symposium.

> **What can go wrong: the Blackwell delay.** CoWoS-L also nearly derailed its flagship. In mid-2024 TSMC and NVIDIA found that B200 assemblies warped during **reflow** (the oven pass that melts the solder) and **thermal cycling** (repeated swings between cold and hot to simulate a lifetime of use). As reported by SemiAnalysis and The Information, the CTE mismatch among GPU dies, LSI bridges, molded RDL interposer and substrate produced stresses that broke connections at the bridge edges. The fix required a new **mask set** (the full set of photomasks for a chip; at the 4 nm class a set costs tens of millions of dollars and a re-spin takes about a quarter) for the GPU die's top global-routing metal layers and bump layout, plus a redesign of the bridge dies, costing roughly a quarter of ramp. NVIDIA confirmed a "GPU mask" change in August 2024; TSMC has never described the failure mechanism publicly. Later NVIDIA designs were reportedly laid out with packaging stress analysis in the loop from the start.

## The CoWoS Process Flow, Step by Step

Here is the CoWoS-S flow from bare interposer wafer to tested package, with CoWoS-L differences noted. TSMC's recipes are proprietary; this is the publicly documented sequence and the unit processes underneath it. The overview first, then each step in depth. The time column is an order-of-magnitude guide, since TSMC publishes no cycle times.

### Step 1: Interposer fabrication

> **Intuition: why holes through the silicon, and why only 100 µm of them.** The dies sit on the interposer's top face and the substrate is under its bottom face, and the ~10,000 signals and ~1,000 A of supply current that must pass between them have only one route: straight through the slab. Hence TSVs. But a hole 10 µm across through a full 775 µm wafer would have an **aspect ratio** (depth divided by width) of 77:1, and no plasma can etch that straight, no film can coat its walls evenly, and no plating bath can fill it without sealing a void. A 100 µm-deep hole is 10:1, which can be etched, lined and filled. So the interposer is etched only 100 µm deep, everything is built on top, and the other 675 µm of silicon is ground away later (Step 3), once the wafer has the dies and mold to hold it rigid.

The interposer starts as a standard 300 mm, 775 µm prime wafer (the ordinary polished, defect-screened grade) with no **epitaxial** layer (a crystal-grown surface layer needed for transistors, not for wires), since nothing electrical is built in the silicon.

**TSV formation.** The holes are defined by a **hard mask**, a thin oxide layer patterned first and used as the stencil, because ordinary photoresist would not survive a 100 µm etch. The 10 µm holes are etched ~100 µm deep with the **Bosch process** (Module 09), which alternates cycles of a few seconds each: **SF6** (sulfur hexafluoride) plasma etches silicon, then **C4F8** (octafluorocyclobutane) deposits a Teflon-like polymer film on the walls that protects them during the next etch pulse. Each cycle removes ~0.5–1 µm at the hole bottom while the sidewall stays protected, leaving ~100–200 nm **scallops**, the ripples of successive cycles. Lam Syndion and TEL Tactras are the standard tools. Next the hole is lined. A ~200–500 nm SiO2 liner insulates the copper from the silicon, deposited by **CVD** (chemical vapour deposition: gases react on the hot surface to leave a solid film) from **TEOS** (tetraethyl orthosilicate, a liquid silicon source that gives a **conformal** oxide, one that coats the bottom of a deep hole as thickly as the top) with ozone at sub-atmospheric pressure. Then a ~20–50 nm **barrier** of TaN (tantalum nitride), a metal skin that stops copper diffusing into the oxide and silicon where it would leak and short, and a ~100–200 nm copper **seed**, a thin film that makes the wall conductive so plating current can flow. Both are sputtered by **ionized PVD** (physical vapour deposition: argon ions knock atoms off a copper or tantalum target and they fly to the wafer; a neutral atom cannot be steered, so in the ionized variant a dense plasma ionizes the metal atoms in flight and a negative voltage on the wafer pulls the now-charged atoms straight down into the hole instead of letting them pile up at its mouth) in an Applied Endura chamber, often supplemented with **electroless** (chemically driven, with no applied current) or **ALD** (atomic layer deposition, one atomic layer per gas pulse, perfectly conformal but slow) liners, because a continuous seed on a scalloped wall 100 µm down is genuinely hard.

**Copper electroplating** (Lam SABRE 3D) fills the hole from the bottom up. The acid-copper bath carries three organic additives that together make the copper grow from the bottom up rather than sealing the mouth first. A **suppressor** (a PEG-type polyether, a large polymer) coats the surfaces it can reach quickly, the flat field and the hole's mouth, and slows deposition wherever it sits, but it is bulky and diffuses only slowly into a narrow hole. An **accelerator** (SPS, a small sulfur-bearing organic molecule) is small enough to reach the bottom, sticks to the copper surface and speeds deposition there; as the bottom fills and the surface area down there shrinks, the accelerator molecules already stuck to it are crowded together, so the plating rate rises fastest at the bottom and the mouth stays open until last. A **leveler** (a third additive that adsorbs preferentially on protruding, high-current spots and slows them) smooths the growing surface. A 100 µm via takes on the order of an hour to fill void-free. **CMP** (chemical-mechanical polishing: a rotating pad with abrasive slurry grinds the surface flat) removes the copper **overburden** (the excess plated over the surface), and a ~350–400 °C **anneal** (a controlled bake) grows the copper grains and forces **copper pumping** to happen now rather than later: copper expands ~6× more than silicon per degree, so a heated via extrudes from its top like toothpaste, and it is better that it does so before wiring is built over it.

> **Worked example: how long a TSV takes to etch and fill.** At ~0.5–1 µm per Bosch cycle, a 100 µm hole needs ~100–200 cycles. Each cycle is a few seconds of etch plus a few of passivation, so the etch runs on the order of ~10–30 minutes per wafer, with all of the wafer's hundreds of thousands of holes etching at once. Bottom-up plating of the same hole is ~1 hour. A 775 µm hole, even if it could be etched straight, would need ~800–1,500 cycles, a seed that survives 8× as far down a scalloped wall, and a plating time many times longer. These are order-of-magnitude figures; TSMC does not publish its recipes.

**RDL formation.** Four or five wiring layers are built by **damascene** (Module 12): deposit dielectric, etch trenches and via holes, sputter TaN and copper seed, plate, polish off the excess. Minimum line/space is ~0.4 µm on the fine layers, coarser on the upper power layers, with ~1–2 µm copper thickness because these lines carry amperes over millimetres. Stitched interposers are exposed field by field. A **passivation** (a protective final insulator) is opened over the top pads and a Ti/Cu **under-bump metallization (UBM)** is formed, a metal stack that solder can wet and that stops solder eating into the copper pad; in CoWoS the microbumps themselves are on the dies, so the interposer gets flat pads. The wafer is **probed** (touched with fine needles and tested electrically) for opens and shorts across its thousands of **nets** (each set of points that the design says must be connected), because everything placed on it later is scrapped if it is bad.

> **What can go wrong.** *TSV voids*: a seed gap on the scalloped wall or a plating front that pinches the mouth shut before the bottom fills leaves a cavity, an open or a via with a fraction of its cross-section that later fails by electromigration (current slowly pushing metal atoms out of a narrow conductor; defined fully in What Goes Wrong); found by cross-sectioning sample wafers, by X-ray inspection and by the resistance of TSV chains at wafer probe. *Copper pumping after the fact*: an under-annealed via extrudes during a later step and cracks the wiring above it. *Stitch-boundary opens*: an overlay error between adjacent exposures narrows or breaks the 0.4 µm wires that cross the field boundary; found at interposer probe as open nets clustered along the stitch lines, and fixed by overlay correction on the stepper and wider overlap in the layout.

For **CoWoS-L**, Step 1 is the eight-step reconstitution sequence of the CoWoS-L section (carrier, bottom RDL, TIV plating, bridge placement, mold, grind-back, top RDL), preceded by fabricating the LSI bridges on their own 65 nm-class wafer with the same 0.4 µm damascene RDL, probing them and dicing them; its output is a molded 300 mm wafer whose front looks like a CoWoS-S interposer, and it goes to Step 2 the same way.

### Step 2: Chip-on-Wafer (CoW)

The interposer wafer, with 16 to 30 sites, now receives its dies.

**Bumps on the dies.** GPU dies arrive from **wafer sort** (the electrical test of every die while still on the wafer, Module 14) with **copper pillar microbumps** already formed: a ~20–30 µm copper pillar plated through a resist mask on the die's UBM, capped with ~10–15 µm of SnAg solder (~96.5% Sn / 3.5% Ag), at 40–55 µm pitch. An ~800 mm² GPU carries tens of thousands to over 100,000 of them, mostly power and ground. HBM stacks arrive from SK hynix, Samsung or Micron as tested **known-good** stacks (every stack has passed a full electrical test before shipping, because a dead one cannot be removed once it is soldered down; the Known-good die section below does the economics) with ~55 µm pitch bumps on the base die, at a total height matched by design so that GPU and HBM tops end up nearly coplanar for the lid.

> **Worked example: why a GPU needs tens of thousands of power bumps.** A 1,000 W die at a core supply of ~0.8 V draws I = P/V = 1,000 / 0.8 ≈ 1,250 A. A 25 µm copper pillar can safely carry only ~50–100 mA before electromigration (the slow displacement of metal atoms by a high current, defined in the next section) becomes a lifetime risk, so delivering the supply needs 1,250 / (0.05–0.1) ≈ 12,500–25,000 pillars, and the ground return needs as many again: ~25,000–50,000 bumps for power alone, before a single signal is counted, and ~30,000–50,000 once hotspots and margin are allowed for. That is why most of a GPU's ~100,000 microbumps carry no data at all, and why the bump count is set by current, not by I/O.

**Placement and joining.** A die bonder places each die face-down with its bumps aligned to the pads to within a few micrometres, locking its camera onto **fiducials**, alignment crosses patterned on the wafer. **Mass reflow** places all dies with tacky **flux** (a mild acid that strips the oxide skin off solder so it can wet copper) and runs the wafer through an oven where SnAg melts at 221 °C (peak ~240–250 °C) and wets the pads by surface tension, self-aligning the die; it is fast and cheap. **Thermocompression bonding (TCB)** instead holds each die under a heated **bond head** at ~250–300 °C with controlled force (tens to hundreds of newtons for a large die) for a few seconds, actively flattening the die while the bumps join. TCB tolerates die warpage and fine pitch far better, at a throughput of a few hundred dies per hour per head rather than thousands, and is what large-die CoWoS-L assembly requires. Besi and ASMPT dominate logic TCB; Hanmi's dual-head TC bonders are the standard for HBM stacking at SK hynix.

**Underfill.** A capillary underfill (low-viscosity epoxy loaded with silica) is dispensed along the die edges at ~80–100 °C, wicks into the ~25–35 µm gap, and is cured. It spreads the CTE **shear** stress (the sideways sliding of one layer relative to the one below as they expand differently) across the whole die area instead of concentrating it in the outermost joints, which would otherwise fatigue in a few hundred thermal cycles. For the finest pitches a pre-applied **non-conductive film (NCF)**, an epoxy sheet laminated onto the die before bonding and cured by the TCB head, replaces capillary underfill.

**Gap fill and molding.** The wafer is over-molded with epoxy mold compound and ground back so the die backsides are exposed and coplanar. The mold also stiffens the wafer for the next step.

> **What can go wrong.** *Non-wets*: a bump whose solder never wetted its pad, from oxide, a low spot or too little solder, leaving an open. *Bridging*: too much solder or too much bonding force squeezes neighbours together at 45 µm pitch. *Die tilt*: a die bonded a few micrometres higher at one corner, so one edge's joints are starved and the other's crushed. *Underfill voids*: air pockets that concentrate stress and, holding moisture, burst at the next reflow. Detection is by X-ray for opens and bridges, **C-SAM** (scanning acoustic microscopy, which sends ultrasound through the stack and images gaps and delaminations as reflections) for voids and tilt, and electrical test of the module. Fixes live in the TCB force and temperature profile, the flux chemistry, the bump-height uniformity from the plating line, and the underfill dispense pattern.

### Step 3: Backside reveal and C4 bumps

The interposer is still 775 µm thick and its TSVs stop 100 µm down. The molded front is bonded face-down to a temporary glass **carrier wafer** with a thermoplastic or laser-release adhesive (Brewer Science, 3M; bonders from EV Group and SUSS). A Disco grinder removes the backside in two stages, coarse then fine, to within a few µm of the TSV tips. Grinding leaves a layer of cracked, stressed silicon that would snap a 100 µm wafer, so a **stress-relief etch** or CMP removes that damage layer and the last silicon, leaving the copper TSV ends standing ~1–3 µm proud of (above) the silicon surface. A **PECVD** passivation of silicon nitride and oxide (plasma-enhanced CVD, in which a plasma rather than heat drives the reaction, so it runs below 200 °C, which matters because the front of the wafer is now full of solder and epoxy) is deposited and polished back to expose the copper. One backside RDL layer fans the TSV pitch out to the **C4 bump** grid, UBM is formed, and C4 solder bumps of ~80–100 µm diameter at ~130–180 µm pitch are plated on: tens of thousands on a 2,800 mm² interposer. The stack is **debonded** (the adhesive released by a laser shone through the glass, or by heating and sliding), cleaned and inspected. The interposer is now ~100 µm thick under ~1 mm of die and mold; the mold and dies, not the silicon, hold it flat.

> **Intuition: why chip-on-wafer comes before wafer-on-substrate.** The order is forced by mechanics and money. A 100 µm × 60 mm sheet of silicon cannot survive handling on its own; molding it under a millimetre of dies and epoxy first turns it into a rigid tile, and only then is it safe to grind the silicon thin. Placing dies while the interposer is still a whole 300 mm wafer also lets the bonder use wafer-scale fiducials and a flat, rigid **chuck** (the vacuum plate that holds the wafer), and lets a dead interposer site (found at the Step 1 probe) be skipped before $5,000 of dies are committed to it.

### Step 4: Singulation

The wafer is diced into **CoW modules** with a blade or laser (Module 16). At 3.3× reticle one wafer yields ~16, which is why "wafers per month" maps to package counts differently for every product. Each module is tested again by probing the C4 bumps on its underside, which reach every die through the interposer; it now contains the most expensive parts in the world, and catching a bad one here is far cheaper than after substrate attach.

### Step 5: Wafer-on-Substrate (oS)

The module is flipped onto the **package substrate** (Module 16): the organic build-up laminate of the Anatomy section, for a Blackwell-class part on the order of 80–100 mm on a side and ~2–3 mm thick, with a ~1 mm glass-fibre core and 10–12 build-up layers per side of Ajinomoto ABF and copper, from Ibiden, Unimicron, Shinko, Kinsus or AT&S. The join is standard **flip-chip** mass reflow (the module is mounted face-down so its C4 bumps land directly on the substrate's pads; peak ~245 °C), in which tens of thousands of C4 bumps melt and wet simultaneously, followed by capillary underfill under the whole interposer. This step carries the largest CTE mismatch (silicon at 2.6 ppm/°C against organic at 10–17, over 50+ mm), and it is the step TSMC most readily outsources: the "oS" half of CoWoS-S has run at ASE, **SPIL** (Siliconware Precision Industries, Taiwan's second-largest assembly house, owned by ASE since 2018) and Amkor (the largest US-based one), the **OSATs** (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test houses), since 2023–2024, with TSMC keeping the interposer and CoW in-house.

> **What can go wrong.** *Corner C4 opens*: as the assembly cools from reflow the substrate shrinks faster than the interposer and the module's corners lift; if a corner is further from its pad than the bump height when the solder freezes, the joint never forms. *Centre bridging*: a module domed the other way during reflow squashes its central C4s until neighbours touch. *Underfill delamination*: the underfill separates from a warped surface and traps moisture. Detection is by C-SAM for delaminations and by X-ray for opens and bridges, plus electrical test; the fixes are warpage-matched substrate and mold designs, slower reflow profiles, and the stiffener ring of the next step.

### Step 6: Stiffener, lid, balls, test

A metal **stiffener ring** is glued around the substrate perimeter to hold it flat against warpage, and a nickel-plated copper **lid** is attached over the dies with **TIM1** between die backs and lid. Two polished metal faces pressed together touch only at their high points; the rest is a film of air, and air is a near-perfect insulator, so a soft material must fill the gaps. **Thermal conductivity** in W/m·K is the number of watts that flow through a 1 m cube per degree of temperature difference across it, and the ladder is copper ~400, indium ~80, filled epoxy ~1, air 0.03. TIM1 is an indium solder sheet (~80 W/m·K, reflowed at ~160 °C), a filled polymer, or, reportedly for the >1,000 W parts, a liquid-metal gallium alloy. GPU and HBM tops differ in height by tens of µm, so the TIM bond line must be controlled to tens of µm at the hottest die. **SAC305** BGA balls (~0.6 mm on ~1 mm pitch, several thousand per package) are attached by flux, ball drop and reflow. The package then goes to final test and **burn-in**, running the part hot and over-voltage for hours to force early failures (Module 18).

> **Why it matters downstream.** The GPU-to-HBM height mismatch sets the TIM1 thickness over the GPU, the TIM1 thickness sets the largest single thermal resistance in the stack, and that resistance sets how hot the transistors run for a given coolant temperature. The same lid also cools the HBM, whose DRAM must stay below ~85 °C: DRAM stores each bit as charge on a leaky capacitor and must rewrite (**refresh**) every bit every few tens of milliseconds before the charge drains away; heat roughly doubles the leak, so above ~85 °C the **refresh rate** doubles too, and every refresh is a moment the memory cannot spend serving the GPU, stealing bandwidth and power. Module 19 turns this into the rack's cooling budget: every extra 10 µm of bond line over the GPU adds to the junction temperature and, through the shared lid, to the memory's.

The elapsed time from bare interposer wafer to tested package is on the order of 6–12 weeks, on top of the ~3 months the GPU wafer spent in the front end. Of the six steps, TSMC keeps the ones that look like fab work: interposer fabrication, the CoWoS-L reconstituted interposer, and chip-on-wafer, all of which need fab-grade lithography, plating, cleanliness and fine-pitch bonders. It pushes the ones that look like conventional packaging, substrate attach, lid, balls and test, to ASE, SPIL and Amkor, and by 2025–2026 was moving whole CoWoS-S flows out so its own advanced-packaging fabs could concentrate on CoWoS-L and hybrid bonding.

## What Goes Wrong: The Hard Problems

### Warpage

A CoWoS package is a laminate of every material in the Anatomy section's CTE table: silicon at 2.6 ppm/°C, copper at 17, solder at ~22, mold compound at ~8–12, ABF at ~15–20, a glass-epoxy core at ~10–15 and, in CoWoS-L, polyimide at ~20–50. Every reflow takes it from 25 to ~245 °C and back.

> **Worked example: how much a substrate outgrows its interposer.** Take the substrate at ~15 ppm/°C and silicon at 2.6, so Δα ≈ 12 ppm/°C. A reflow swings ΔT ≈ 220 °C (25 to 245 °C). Over L = 100 mm of substrate, the differential expansion is Δα · ΔT · L = 12 × 10⁻⁶ × 220 × 100 mm ≈ 0.26 mm: the laminate grows about a quarter of a millimetre more than the silicon does. A 50 mm interposer glued to it must bend to accommodate that, and the result is warpage of hundreds of µm across the package. The stack is stress-free at the moment the solder freezes (~217–221 °C), so it bends one way as it cools to room temperature; in the next reflow it straightens as it passes back through that temperature and bends the other way above it, which is why warpage changes sign with temperature. Above the mold compound's **glass transition temperature (Tg)**, ~150–200 °C for these epoxies, the polymer softens and its expansion rate roughly triples, which makes the hot end of the swing worse.

If the surfaces are further apart than the bump height at the corners when the solder freezes, the corner joints open; if the module is domed during oS reflow, the centre C4s squash and bridge. Engineers measure it and design around it. Every substrate and module is measured through a reflow temperature profile by **shadow moiré** (a grating's shadow on the surface reveals height as fringes) or laser scanning; delaminations are found by C-SAM, bump opens by X-ray or X-ray computed tomography; and the qualification gate is **JEDEC temperature cycling** (JEDEC is the memory and packaging standards body), typically −40 to +125 °C for 1,000 cycles, after which every joint must still conduct. Fixes are mold-compound reformulation, thicker or glass cores, stiffener rings, slower reflow profiles, and finite-element stress simulation (computer modelling that divides the stack into small elements and solves the stress in each) of the whole package before the layout is frozen. Every step up in reticle multiple (2.5× → 3.3× → 5.5×) requires re-engineered mold compounds, substrate cores (glass cores are arriving for this reason), stiffeners and reflow profiles. The B200 delay was a warpage failure at the LSI bridge edges.

### Thermal

The lid must pass the whole package's heat: ~700 W for H100, 1,000–1,200 W for B200/GB200, ~1,400 W for B300, and reported ~1,800 W for Rubin, rising toward ~3,600 W for four-die Rubin Ultra-class packages. Over ~1,600 mm² of logic that is ~60–110 W/cm² average with hotspots several times higher (the hotplate comparison of the Anatomy section). TIM1 is the dominant resistance in the stack, and the coplanarity problem means it cannot be made arbitrarily thin. The HBM's DRAM must stay below ~85 °C, where its refresh rate doubles (the Step 6 box), and it sits millimetres from a die dissipating a kilowatt, so the lid must hold the HBM below its limit while the GPU runs at 85–100 °C.

### Bump coplanarity and joint reliability

At 40–55 µm pitch a pillar is ~25 µm across with ~10 µm of solder. Tolerable bump-height variation across an 800 mm² die is a few micrometres; die warpage during bonding is tens of micrometres unless TCB clamps it. Too little solder and a joint opens; too much and neighbours bridge. Afterwards the **intermetallics** grow: wherever copper touches tin, brittle crystalline compounds (Cu6Sn5, then Cu3Sn) form at the interface and thicken with every heating, and at 10 µm of solder they can consume the joint entirely, leaving it glass-brittle. **Electromigration** is the other lifetime limit: at high current density the flow of electrons physically pushes metal atoms downstream, opening a void at the upstream end of the pillar. A 25 µm pillar carrying tens of milliamps is at that limit, which is why the bump worked example in Step 2 puts most of the bumps on power and ground.

### Known-good die and the impossibility of rework

Every die must be known good before placement, because once microbumps are reflowed and underfilled there is no rework. A B200 assembly carries two ~$500–600 GPU dies (a **4N** wafer, TSMC's NVIDIA-tuned 5 nm-class process, at ~$16,000–20,000 yields ~30 good candidates), eight HBM3E stacks at ~$300–500 each (24 GB 8-high at the reported ~$13–20 per GB of 2025; 36 GB 12-high stacks are ~$500–700), plus interposer and substrate. One dead HBM stack, or one open joint among the ~250,000 microbumps (two GPU dies at ~100,000 each plus eight HBM stacks at ~5,000–6,000 each) and the tens of thousands of C4 bumps beneath them, scraps an assembly worth ~$5,000–7,000 in parts that would have sold for over $30,000. A 95% CoW yield on a 16-per-wafer product loses nearly a package per wafer, which is why HBM ships as tested known-good stacks, why CoWoS re-tests them anyway, and why Module 18 exists.

### Interposer yield with stitched reticles

A big simple chip still obeys Poisson statistics: at 2,800 mm² and an interposer defect density of even 0.02/cm², naive yield is exp(−0.56) ≈ 57%. Actual yields are much higher because the RDL is coarse and a 65 nm passive process is very clean, but stitching adds a class of failure (CD mismatch and overlay across field boundaries) that a normal die never sees. CoWoS-L is partly a yield play: the fine-pitch silicon is small and high-yielding, and the big organic interposer has nothing finer than 2 µm on it.

> **What can go wrong: CoWoS-L's own failure modes.** *Die shift*: the LSI bridges are placed on the carrier before molding, and as the mold compound cures and shrinks it drags them by micrometres, so the top RDL, patterned afterwards, no longer lands on the bridge pads where the design expects; the defence is to measure every bridge position after molding and correct the RDL exposure to it. *Bridge-to-die overlay*: a GPU's 45 µm bumps must land on a bridge a few millimetres wide with a placement budget of a few micrometres, and both the bridge's shift and the die's placement error eat it. *Moisture popcorning*: the molded interposer is a polymer that absorbs water from the air, and at the next reflow that water flashes to steam and delaminates the mold from the bridge or the RDL with an audible crack; parts are baked dry before reflow and kept in dry storage between steps. All three are found by X-ray, C-SAM and electrical test of the module, and all three were in play in the 2024 Blackwell redesign.

## Capacity: The Bottleneck of the AI Era

### The numbers, 2023 to 2026

All figures in this section are supply-chain analyst estimates (TrendForce, SemiAnalysis, Silicon Analysts and brokerage research notes) as of 2026; TSMC publishes none of them, and they shift quarterly. TSMC's CoWoS capacity was ~10,000–15,000 **wafers per month (wpm)** at the end of 2023, when H100 lead times reached a year. It doubled to ~30,000–35,000 wpm by end-2024, doubled again to ~75,000–80,000 by end-2025, and the stated plan for end-2026 was 120,000–140,000 wpm, with the supply-demand gap still estimated at 10–20%. Outsourced oS work at ASE, SPIL and Amkor added on the order of 240,000–270,000 wafers per year by 2026 (Amkor ~180,000–190,000, SPIL ~60,000–80,000). NVIDIA takes an estimated 55–65% of all CoWoS; Broadcom, AMD, Google, Amazon (via Marvell and **Alchip**, a Taiwanese design-service house that builds custom AI chips for Amazon) and Microsoft take the rest.

> **Worked example: from wafers per month to GPUs.** Converting wafers to packages is product-specific: ~29 H100 packages per 2.5× reticle wafer, ~16 B200s per 3.3× CoWoS-L wafer, ~9 per 5.5× wafer. So 10,000 wpm of 3.3× capacity is 10,000 × 16 = 160,000 Blackwell packages a month, and the same wafers on the 5.5× generation are only ~90,000. At ~130,000 wpm and a mix averaging ~16–20 packages per wafer, 2026 capacity supports 130,000 × 12 × 16–20 ≈ 25–31 million AI packages a year. Wafer capacity doubling per year roughly kept pace with interposer area doubling per generation (29 → 16 → 9 packages per wafer), which is why accelerator unit counts grew more slowly than the headline wafer figures.

### Fabs and outsourcing

TSMC's advanced-packaging (**AP**) fabs are separate from its wafer fabs, and they are a different kind of building. A wafer fab's capacity is set by a few hundred very expensive, very fast tools (an EUV scanner costs ~$150–400 million and exposes >100 wafers an hour) in ISO Class 3–5 cleanrooms; an AP fab's capacity is set by hundreds of cheap, slow ones (a TC bonder at ~$1–3 million places a few hundred dies an hour; a mold press, a reflow tunnel, a grinder and a debonder each handle one or a few wafers at a time), most of them in less stringent bays (~ISO Class 6–7), with only the interposer lithography and plating and the hybrid-bonding cells kept at fab-grade cleanliness. Capacity therefore scales with floor area rather than with scanner count, which is why "floor space" is the number analysts track for packaging and why TSMC has bought and converted existing buildings rather than wait for new ones. The sites: AP1 (Hsinchu, becoming an R&D fab), AP2 (Tainan, bumping and some CoWoS), AP3 (Longtan, InFO), AP5 (Taichung, the original CoWoS home), AP6 (Zhunan, the 2023–2024 CoWoS expansion), AP7 (Chiayi, a **greenfield** site, one built on empty land, broken ground in 2024 with its first phase producing from 2026, hosting SoIC, **WMCM** (TSMC's fan-out package for Apple's M-series) and the planned **CoPoS** panel line, the rectangular-panel successor to CoWoS described in the Fan-Out section), and AP8 (Tainan, a former Innolux LCD plant bought in 2024 and converted quickly because the building existed; at ~96,000 m² it is reportedly ~9× the floor area of AP6). Total AP floor space reportedly roughly tripled from 2022 to 2026. In Arizona, TSMC announced two AP fabs as part of its 2025 US expansion, and Amkor is building a ~$2 billion plant in Peoria for CoWoS-class oS and InFO on TSMC's Arizona wafers, both targeted at ~2027–2028. TSMC's stated policy is to push oS and, increasingly, whole CoWoS-S flows to OSATs so its own AP fabs concentrate on CoW, CoWoS-L and SoIC.

### What it costs

TSMC does not publish pricing. Analyst estimates (SemiAnalysis, TrendForce, sell-side notes) put a CoWoS-S wafer at ~$6,000–8,000 in 2023 and CoWoS-L at ~$10,000–12,000 or more by 2025, with advanced-packaging prices rising 10–20% per year through 2026. Dividing by 16–29 packages and adding the substrate ($200–500 for a large ABF part), lid and test gives ~$500–1,500 of assembly cost per GPU package, a low-single-digit percent of selling price but comparable to the logic die's silicon cost. The strategic problem was never price; it was that a $10,000 wafer of packaging gated the sale of sixteen $30,000 GPUs.

## SoIC: Hybrid Bonding and True 3D

CoWoS puts dies side by side. **SoIC (System on Integrated Chips)** is TSMC's brand for stacking dies with **hybrid bonding**: no bumps, no solder, no underfill, just copper pads on one die fused directly to copper pads on the other with the surrounding dielectric fused too, at 9 µm pitch in 2022, 6 µm in production since ~2025, and a roadmap to 4.5 µm (which TSMC's 2026 symposium placed in ~2029) and then 3 and ~2 µm. At 6 µm there are 1/(0.006 mm)² ≈ 28,000 connections per mm², about 55× a 45 µm microbump array (~500 per mm²) and ~470× a 130 µm C4 array (~59 per mm²), enough to make the interface between stacked dies indistinguishable from a wide on-die bus at ~0.1 pJ/bit.

> **Intuition: optical contact.** Press two optically flat glass plates together and they stick by themselves, hard enough that they must be slid apart; opticians call it optical contact. It happens because two surfaces flat to a nanometre come within molecular reach over a large area, and the weak attractions between molecules (van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonds), negligible one at a time, add up over billions of them. Hybrid bonding engineers that effect deliberately, on surfaces polished flat to a fraction of a nanometre and chemically primed to bond, and then bakes the weak bonds into strong covalent ones. The copper is the passenger: it is recessed so that the glass-like dielectric makes the first contact, and it is closed up afterwards by heat.

### The mechanism

1. **Surface preparation.** Both surfaces finish with a damascene copper pad layer, then CMP to nanometre flatness with a controlled copper **dishing** of ~2–5 nm: the pads must sit very slightly below the dielectric, which is SiO2 or, increasingly, **SiCN** (silicon carbonitride, a harder dielectric that bonds more strongly and blocks copper diffusion). Too much dishing and the copper will not meet after anneal; too little and the dielectric will not seal.
2. **Plasma activation.** A brief nitrogen, argon or oxygen plasma breaks surface Si–O bonds and, after a deionized-water rinse, leaves reactive **silanol** (Si–OH) groups, dangling hydroxyls on the oxide surface. SiCN activates to higher bond strength than SiO2 and is a better copper diffusion barrier.
3. **Room-temperature pre-bond.** The surfaces are aligned and touched at one point. Hydrogen bonds form between facing OH groups and a **bond wave** propagates across the interface at centimetres per second with no applied force. The recessed copper pads are not yet touching, so the bond can still be inspected and, for wafers, undone.
4. **Anneal** at ~200–300 °C (down to ~150 °C with SiCN) for about an hour. The Si–OH groups condense to covalent Si–O–Si bridges, releasing water that diffuses away, so the dielectric bond becomes as strong as bulk oxide. The copper, expanding far more than the constraining dielectric and silicon (Cu ~17 vs SiO2 ~0.5 and Si 2.6 ppm/°C), grows out of its recess, touches, and is pressed against its partner; copper atoms interdiffuse and grains grow across the interface, leaving a single piece of copper with a faint line under **TEM** (transmission electron microscopy, an electron beam through a slice thin enough to resolve the atomic lattice). No solder, no intermetallics, no gap.

> **Worked example: copper pad expansion versus the dishing budget.** A copper pad ~1 µm thick heated by ΔT ≈ 250 °C grows vertically by α · ΔT · t = 17 × 10⁻⁶ × 250 × 1,000 nm ≈ 4 nm if it were free. It is not free: the surrounding oxide, expanding at ~0.5 ppm/°C, squeezes it sideways, and copper pushed inward has nowhere to go but up, so the real rise is somewhat larger. That is why the CMP dishing budget is 2–5 nm: recess the pad by less and the copper touches before the dielectric has sealed; recess it by more and the anneal cannot close the gap. The whole process lives inside a few nanometres of thermal expansion.

### Chip-on-wafer and wafer-on-wafer

**Wafer-on-wafer (WoW)** bonds two full 300 mm wafers, with the best alignment (~50–100 nm on an EV Group GEMINI or SUSS XBC300) and throughput, but requires identical die layouts and marries good dies to bad ones. It is used where dies are small and yields high: Sony's image sensors (the pixel-array wafer bonded onto the logic wafer, since 2016) and **3D NAND** flash memory (the stacked storage chips inside SSDs), where the chip's control circuits (its **periphery**, the row and column drivers and sense amplifiers that surround a memory array) are made on one wafer and bonded on top of the memory array made on another. **Chip-on-wafer (CoW)** places known-good dies one at a time on a target wafer with a die-to-wafer hybrid bonder (Besi's 8800 Chameo series, ASMPT, and the Applied/Besi Kinex cluster that integrates cleaning, activation, placement and metrology at ~1,600 placements per hour), at ±100–200 nm accuracy. CoW is what logic uses: AMD's 3D V-Cache places a 64 MB SRAM die (static RAM, the fast on-chip cache memory) on a Zen (AMD's CPU core design) core die, and MI300 places its **accelerator complex dies (XCDs)**, the compute chiplets, built on TSMC's N5 (5 nm-class) node, on N6 (6 nm-class) **I/O dies (IODs)**, the chiplets holding memory controllers and interconnect, at 9 µm pitch, then puts the stacks and eight HBM3 on a CoWoS-S interposer, the first product to combine SoIC and CoWoS. TSMC also offers a bumped SoIC-P (~18–25 µm) beside the bumpless SoIC-X.

### Why particles and alignment are everything

A hybrid bond has no solder to absorb a defect. A particle trapped at the interface forces the two surfaces to bend around it, and the unbonded void is hundreds to thousands of times the particle's size, because a stiff plate cannot drape closely over a bump. The void's diameter grows with the particle's height (roughly as its square root in the classic elastic model) and strongly with the stiffness of the wafers, which rises steeply with thickness. So between two full-thickness 775 µm wafers even a ~100 nm particle opens a millimetre-scale void (the figure Module 15 quotes for wafer-to-wafer bonding) and a 1 µm particle one of several millimetres, while between a thinned ~50 µm die and a wafer a 1 µm particle leaves a void of ~1 mm or less. In every case it kills every pad inside it. Cleaning, activation and bonding therefore happen in an **ISO Class 1** mini-environment (the cleanest cleanroom class: no more than ~10 particles of 0.1 µm or larger per cubic metre; typical fab bays are Class 3–5) with minimal exposure time, and inline infrared or acoustic inspection (Onto, Camtek, EVG/SUSS inline metrology) finds voids before the anneal makes them permanent. Alignment budgets follow pitch at roughly ±(pitch/20), the rule of thumb bonder makers quote: ±0.3 µm at 6 µm and ±0.1 µm at 2 µm, which is why the roadmap is gated by bonder alignment as much as by process. A 300 mm silicon wafer grows ~0.8 µm across its diameter per degree (2.6 ppm/°C × 300 mm), a full alignment budget at 4.5 µm pitch, so the temperatures of the two wafers, or of die and wafer, must match to a fraction of a degree.

## The Competitors: Intel, Samsung, and the OSATs

### Intel: EMIB and Foveros

**EMIB (Embedded Multi-die Interconnect Bridge)** embeds a small silicon bridge with 2–4 fine-pitch RDL layers into a cavity cut in the organic substrate itself, directly under the edge where two dies meet. The cavity matters: the bridge sits flush with the substrate surface, so a die's bumps over the bridge and its bumps beside it land on one plane. The dies use ~55 µm (moving to ~45 µm) bumps over the bridge and ordinary C4 bumps elsewhere, straight onto the substrate: no interposer, no TSVs, and a package as large as the substrate allows. The cost is that the substrate maker must embed silicon in a laminate and build flat layers over it, which only Intel's captive lines and a few partners have mastered. EMIB shipped in Kaby Lake-G (2018, a laptop CPU with an AMD graphics tile), Stratix 10 and Agilex (FPGAs with HBM), Sapphire Rapids (the 2023 Xeon server CPU, four ~400 mm² tiles, plus HBM in Xeon Max), its successors Emerald and Granite Rapids, and Ponte Vecchio (a 47-tile data-centre GPU, EMIB plus Foveros). Gaudi 3, Intel's own AI accelerator, is reportedly assembled on TSMC 2.5D, a telling data point. **EMIB-T** adds TSVs through the bridge for power delivery and was readied for external customers in 2025–2026.

**Foveros** is Intel's die-on-die stacking: solder microbumps at 50 µm (Lakefield, 2019, a low-power laptop chip), 36 µm (Meteor Lake, the 2023 laptop CPU: an Intel 4 compute tile and a TSMC N5 graphics tile on a base tile with TSVs built on 22FFL, Intel's 22 nm low-power process), 25 µm (Foveros Omni), and **Foveros Direct** hybrid bonding at 9 µm with a roadmap to ~3 µm. Intel Foundry offers all of these to external customers as the alternative to CoWoS, from packaging fabs in Chandler (Arizona), Rio Rancho (New Mexico), and Penang and Kulim (Malaysia), the largest advanced-packaging capacity outside Taiwan. After NVIDIA's $5 billion investment in Intel in September 2025 and the announced co-developed products, Intel packaging for NVIDIA-designed parts became a live possibility, though as of 2026 NVIDIA's GPU volume remains on CoWoS.

### Samsung: I-Cube and X-Cube

Samsung's **I-Cube** is its 2.5D family, and it has the same shape as CoWoS. **I-CubeS** puts logic and HBM on a passive silicon interposer with TSVs (I-Cube4 with four HBM2E stacks, announced in 2021; I-Cube8 with eight since), on microbumps in the same ~40–55 µm class as CoWoS, because HBM's 55 µm footprint is fixed by JEDEC and every 2.5D flow has to land on it. **I-CubeE** embeds a small silicon bridge in a molded RDL interposer, its CoWoS-L analogue, and **H-Cube** (developed with Amkor) stacks a fine-pitch substrate on a coarser, larger one to reach package sizes beyond what one laminate can hold flat. **X-Cube** is TSV-based 3D stacking of SRAM or logic on logic with microbumps, with a Cu-Cu hybrid-bonded version roadmapped for ~2026–2027. The work is done at Samsung's packaging plants in Cheonan and Onyang, South Korea, alongside its HBM stacking. Samsung is the only company making HBM, logic and packaging in-house and pitches a turnkey AI package; its first announced I-Cube customer was Baidu's Kunlun AI chip (2019–2020, on Samsung's 14 nm-class logic). In practice its 2.5D volume has been a small fraction of TSMC's, because a package is normally assembled by the foundry that made the dies and the accelerator designers overwhelmingly fab at TSMC. This section is short for that reason: the physics is the same as CoWoS, the volume is not.

### The OSATs: ASE, Amkor, SPIL, JCET

The outsourced assembly houses build 2.5D-class flows on fan-out RDL rather than silicon interposers, since they lack 300 mm front-end fabs. ASE's **FOCoS** (Fan-Out Chip on Substrate) puts dies on a ~2 µm line/space fan-out RDL interposer, and **FOCoS-Bridge** embeds a silicon bridge under die edges, a direct CoWoS-L analogue; ASE's **CoWoP** (chip-on-wafer-on-PCB) proposal drops the ABF substrate and mounts the CoW module on a large fine-line PCB. Amkor's **S-SWIFT** and **S-Connect** are the equivalents. ASE (which owns SPIL) and Amkor are also TSMC's designated overflow for CoWoS oS and, from 2025, some CoWoS-S CoW. JCET's **XDFOI** is another heterogeneous-integration platform. In its April 2024 results release, JCET reported stable high-volume XDFOI manufacturing across multiple factories. These factories supply advanced-packaging services to global customers. It is not the same process as CoWoS: interconnect geometry, package dimensions, thermal limits, yield, available capacity and customer qualification must be compared for the particular design. [JCET Q1 2024 production update](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/jcet-q1-2024-revenue-and-net-profit-achieve-double-digit-year-on-year-growth-302126070.html).

## Fan-Out and the Move to Panels

**Fan-out wafer-level packaging (FOWLP)** builds the package wiring directly on the dies, with no substrate at all. The mechanism is reconstitution: good dies are placed face-down on a carrier with gaps between them, molded into a new 300 mm epoxy disc, and released from the carrier. Wiring layers are then built over die and epoxy alike, so the wires can run past the die's edge (fan out) onto the mold, where the solder balls go. Each RDL layer is made the same way. A liquid polyimide is **spin-coated** (poured onto the centre of the spinning wafer, so that centrifugal force spreads it into a film a few micrometres thick), exposed and developed to open the via holes (this polyimide is itself photosensitive, so it needs no separate resist for the vias), and cured; then titanium and copper seed are sputtered over it, a photoresist is patterned with the wiring, copper is plated up in the openings, and the resist and the seed between the lines are stripped away. The result is ~2 µm line/space copper with vias of ~5–10 µm through polyimide a few µm thick, layer on layer (TSMC publishes ranges, not recipes). That is the wiring of the CoWoS-R interposer and of the organic part of the CoWoS-L interposer, which is why those sections point here.

The characteristic problem of any reconstituted wafer is **die shift**. The mold compound shrinks by a fraction of a percent as it cures, and the carrier adhesive lets each die creep, so after molding every die sits some distance (uncorrected, ~10 µm-class, growing towards the wafer edge, where the shrinking disc has pulled furthest) from where it was placed, while the RDL patterned next assumes the design position; a 2 µm line that misses its pad by 10 µm is an open. The cure is **adaptive patterning**: measure the actual position and rotation of every die after molding and adjust that die's RDL exposure to match, which is natural on a maskless direct-write lithography tool and possible on a stepper by shifting each field, and which the CoWoS-L failure box described for LSI bridges. The other failure modes are warpage of the reconstituted wafer (an epoxy disc at 8–12 ppm/°C with silicon islands at 2.6 bows as it cools from the mold cure, and must be flat enough for the lithography's focus) and cracks in the RDL where it crosses a die edge onto mold, the sharpest change in stiffness and expansion anywhere in the structure; polyimide's flexibility is one reason it is the dielectric of choice, and rounded via corners and wider lines at the die-edge crossing are the layout rules. TSMC's **InFO (Integrated Fan-Out)** entered volume with Apple's A10 in the iPhone 7 (2016) as **InFO-PoP** (**package-on-package**: a memory package stacked on top of the processor package): the A10 in mold with two RDL layers beneath and TIVs (the same pillars used in CoWoS-L) up through the mold so the **LPDDR** (low-power phone DRAM) package could sit on top in under 1 mm total height. Every A-series and M-series chip since has used an InFO variant, TSMC's second-largest AP business. **InFO-oS** puts a fan-out package on a substrate for networking and HPC (high-performance computing) parts; **InFO-L / InFO-LSI** adds an embedded bridge (Apple's UltraFusion, joining two M1 Max dies at 2.5 TB/s, is InFO-LSI); **InFO-SoW** (system on wafer) skips dicing and builds a full 300 mm wafer of dies with fan-out RDL, used by Tesla's Dojo D1 tile (25 dies, 2021) and productized as **TSMC-SoW** (2024) and **SoW-X** (CoWoS-based, ~40× the compute of one CoWoS package, announced for 2027).

The next structural change is the substrate shape. A round 300 mm wafer fits 16 of a 3.3× reticle rectangle and only 9 or 4 of the 5.5× and 9.5× sizes, with large edge waste; a rectangular **panel** wastes almost nothing. TSMC's **CoPoS (Chip-on-Panel-on-Substrate)** moves the CoWoS-L flow onto panels: a 310 × 310 mm pilot line was reportedly completed in mid-2026 (at its VisEra/Visionchip subsidiary in Longtan), with a 510 × 515 mm production format (~3.7× the area of a 300 mm wafer, matching the ABF substrate panel standard) planned for volume at AP7 Chiayi in ~2028–2029. The tool set changes: large-field projection or laser-direct-imaging lithography (KLA/Orbotech, Onto JetStep, Canon panel steppers), panel plating and CMP (SCHMID, MKS/Atotech, Ebara), and display-style handling. Intel, Samsung and ASE announced panel lines on similar timelines, and glass-core substrates (Intel, Absolics/SKC, Corning), with CTE tunable to ~3–9 ppm/°C and flatness allowing finer lines than ABF, are expected to arrive with them. Panel-level packaging is where this module's 2.5D flow and Module 16's substrate flow converge.

## The Chiplet Paradigm

A **chiplet** is a die designed from the start as one component of a package, with a defined **die-to-die (D2D)** interface. B200 is two chiplets; MI300X is twelve (8 XCDs, 4 IODs) plus 8 HBM; Ponte Vecchio was 47; AMD's EPYC Genoa server CPU is 13 (12 **CCDs**, core complex dies, AMD's 8-core CPU chiplets, plus an IOD) on a plain organic substrate, proof that chiplets need 2.5D only when the D2D bandwidth demands it.

### Die-to-die PHYs

A D2D **PHY** is the transmit and receive circuitry that drives a die's edge wires; its capacity is counted in **lanes** (one wire or wire pair carrying one bit stream) and its speed in **GT/s** (giga-transfers per second, bits per second on one lane).

- **UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express)**, the open standard published in March 2022 by Intel, AMD, Arm, TSMC, Samsung, ASE, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Qualcomm, and later NVIDIA. UCIe 1.x defines a **standard package** PHY for organic substrates (100–130 µm bump pitch, 16 lanes per PHY module, UCIe's unit of one link and unrelated to the CoW module of the process flow, up to 32 GT/s per lane, ~28–224 GB/s per mm of edge, ~0.5 pJ/bit target) and an **advanced package** PHY for interposers and bridges (25–55 µm pitch, 64 lanes per PHY module, ~165–1,300 GB/s/mm at 45 µm and 32 GT/s, ~0.25 pJ/bit target, ~2 ns latency). UCIe 2.0 (2024) added **UCIe-3D** for hybrid-bonded stacks, spanning roughly 10–25 µm down to 1 µm or less, measured in areal bandwidth; UCIe 3.0 (2025) raised lanes to 48–64 GT/s. The protocol layer carries **PCIe** (the standard board-level interconnect), **CXL** (its memory-coherent extension, which lets devices share memory consistently), or raw streaming.
- **NVIDIA NV-HBI**, the proprietary link between the two Blackwell dies: 10 TB/s aggregate (both directions combined) across a ~30 mm shared edge, i.e. ~330 GB/s per mm of edge, over the CoWoS-L bridge, presenting the pair to software as one GPU with a **coherent L2** (one shared cache that both dies see as a single copy). Rubin uses a scaled successor.
- **AMD Infinity Fabric** on-package: over organic substrate for Ryzen/EPYC CCD-to-IOD links (~2 pJ/bit), and over SoIC and CoWoS in MI300, where the XCD-to-IOD interface is a wide, slow hybrid-bonded bus and IOD-to-IOD links run through the interposer at several TB/s aggregate.
- **Intel AIB (Advanced Interface Bus)**, the 55 µm-pitch, ~2 Gb/s-per-lane parallel interface open-sourced in 2018, a direct ancestor of UCIe's advanced PHY; plus **Apple UltraFusion** (2.5 TB/s over InFO-LSI) and the **OCP Bunch of Wires** open PHY for organic substrates (OCP is the Open Compute Project, the hyperscalers' open-hardware consortium).

> **Worked example: microbumps and I/O for one HBM stack.** An HBM3E stack has a 1,024-bit data interface as 16 channels of 64 bits (32 **pseudo-channels** of 32 bits, each channel split in two halves that can be addressed independently). Each pseudo-channel needs a differential strobe pair and per-byte parity or **data-bus-inversion (DBI)** bits (a scheme that flips a byte when that means fewer wires toggle, saving power), each channel a command/address bus (~14 lines), a clock pair and control lines. Roughly: 1,024 data + ~128 DBI/parity + ~64 strobes + ~220 address and control across 16 channels + ~100 miscellaneous (the **IEEE 1500** test port, a standard for testing embedded cores, plus temperature and status, redundancy and direct-access pins) gives on the order of 1,500–1,800 signals. Power and ground bumps in a wide I/O interface equal or exceed the signal count, for two reasons. Every signal's current must loop back to its source through the nearest ground (its **return path**), and a long loop both radiates and couples into its neighbours, so a ground bump sits beside each small group of signals to keep every loop short. And ~1,000 wires switching in the same cycle draw a current spike that only many parallel power and ground bumps, with low combined resistance and inductance (a low **supply impedance**, the total opposition the supply path offers to a fast change in current), can deliver without the supply voltage sagging. So the JEDEC footprint carries ~5,000–6,000 microbumps per stack at ~55 µm pitch: 6,000 × (0.055 mm)² ≈ 18 mm² of bump array on the base die. HBM4, with 2,048 data bits, roughly doubles the signal count on a denser map. Eight stacks therefore contribute ~45,000 microbumps and ~13,000 signal traces to a Blackwell interposer, all routed at sub-µm pitch into the GPU through ~12 mm of edge per stack. A whole Zen 4 CCD, for comparison, talks to its IOD over about 200 signal pins.

### The economics

Chiplets pay off through yield, reuse and node mixing. The full accounting must also include the package (a CoWoS-L assembly is not free), the D2D PHY area on each chiplet (a UCIe advanced PHY module is ~0.4 mm² per 64 lanes; NV-HBI at 10 TB/s occupies a strip along the whole die edge, a few percent of the die), and the power of moving data off-die. Reuse sells chiplets in CPUs: AMD uses one CCD across desktop, workstation and server parts and one IOD per generation, amortizing mask sets and validation over far more units. Node mixing sells them in accelerators: compute dies go on N3/N2 (TSMC's 3 nm- and 2 nm-class nodes) while the I/O die with its analog SerDes and PHYs stays on N6/N5 (6 nm- and 5 nm-class), where those circuits are cheaper and better characterized. The consequence for this course is that the GPU is no longer a chip TSMC makes and someone else packages; the package is the product, and its cost is dominated by this module and Module 15.

> **Worked example: two 400 mm² chiplets versus one 800 mm² die.** Take a 300 mm N4-class (TSMC 4 nm-class) wafer at $17,000 with D0 = 0.1/cm². Monolithic 800 mm² (26 × 31 mm): ~68 gross candidates; yield exp(−0.8) = 44.9%, so ~31 good dies, ~$550 each. Two 400 mm² chiplets (20 × 20 mm), each with a D2D PHY strip adding ~5% area (420 mm²): ~140 gross candidates; yield exp(−0.42) = 65.7%, so ~92 good dies at ~$185, or $370 per pair. Add the assembly penalty: one more placement and a bridge, and compound yield of 97.5% (99% per placement, 99.5% for the link) versus 99% for one die, i.e. ~$40 more expected scrap on a $2,500 assembly. Net ~$410 for the pair versus ~$550 monolithic, a ~25% silicon saving, before noting that a 1,600 mm² "Blackwell as one die" could not be printed at any yield. Real designs harvest (sell 800 mm² dies with a few **SMs** disabled, an SM being a streaming multiprocessor, NVIDIA's block of ~128 cores), which narrows the gap, and chiplets pay ~0.3 pJ/bit on every D2D bit. The decision is closer than naive yield math suggests, which is why NVIDIA stayed monolithic through Hopper (the H100 generation) and split only when the reticle forced it.

## Two Handoffs: Heat to the Rack, Light to the Fibre

These two topics are side notes here: the package's job ends at the lid and at the fibre, and Module 19 covers cooling and the rack in full. They are included because both are decided by choices made in this module.

### Heat

A 1,000–1,800 W package with an ~80 × 80 mm lid must present a flat, low-resistance face to a **cold plate**, a copper block with water channels bolted to the lid. Thermal resistance in **K/W** is the temperature rise per watt flowing, the heat analogue of electrical resistance. The stack from transistor to coolant is **junction** (the transistor layer, the hottest point) → die silicon (~0.7 mm, ~150 W/m·K) → TIM1 (indium or liquid metal, ~50–80 W/m·K effective across 50–100 µm) → copper lid (2–3 mm) → TIM2 (grease or pad, the weakest link unless the module is engineered as a unit) → cold plate → water at ~25–45 °C inlet. Each interface adds ~0.01–0.03 K/W, for a junction-to-coolant resistance on the order of 0.05–0.1 K/W. The arithmetic is one line: 0.05–0.1 K/W × 1,200 W = 60–120 °C of rise, hence 45 °C water and ~85–100 °C junction targets, with HBM held under ~85–95 °C. **Vapor chambers** in the lid (a sealed flat pocket in which water boils at the hotspot and condenses elsewhere, spreading heat far better than solid copper) even out hotspots; direct-to-chip liquid cooling (the GB200 NVL72 rack, 72 Blackwell GPUs and 36 Grace CPUs, has cold plates on every GPU and CPU, rejecting ~120 kW per rack) is mandatory from Blackwell on. Module 19 picks up at the cold plate.

### Light

Pluggable optical transceivers at the front of a switch burn ~15–30 pJ/bit and cost hundreds of dollars each; a 51.2 Tb/s switch with 64 × 800G ports spends more power in optics than in its ASIC. **Co-packaged optics (CPO)** moves the optical engine onto the switch package. The engine works like this. A **waveguide** is a glass or silicon channel a few hundred nanometres wide that guides light the way a fibre does. A **microring modulator** is a ~10 µm ring of silicon waveguide that traps light of one exact wavelength, the one for which a whole number of waves fits around the ring; a small voltage injects charge carriers into the ring's silicon (or pulls them out), which changes its **refractive index** (how much the material slows light, and so how many waves fit around the ring), shifting the trapped wavelength by a fraction of a nanometre and switching the light passing by on or off, turning an electrical bit into an optical one at 100–200 Gb/s. A **photodetector** does the reverse, turning received light into a tiny current, and a **TIA** (transimpedance amplifier) turns that current into a voltage the logic can read. The lasers themselves are **external**, in replaceable modules that feed light in by fibre, because lasers dislike the heat of a switch package. The power saving comes from the electrical side: the ASIC-to-modulator wire is the SerDes channel, and shortening it from ~10 cm of PCB and a connector to ~5 mm of interposer removes most of the equalization circuitry and its power.

TSMC's **COUPE (Compact Universal Photonic Engine)** hybrid-bonds a 65 nm electrical IC (integrated circuit: drivers, TIAs, control) onto a silicon photonic IC (microring modulators, waveguides, photodetectors) with SoIC-X, then places the engines around the switch die on a CoWoS interposer with fibre attach at the package edge. NVIDIA's **Quantum-X Photonics** InfiniBand switches (InfiniBand is the low-latency network fabric used inside AI clusters) (144 ports × 800 Gb/s, announced for late 2025 and shipping in 2026) and **Spectrum-X Photonics** Ethernet switches (up to 512 × 800 Gb/s, 2026) are the first products: 200 Gb/s per lane microrings, 1.6 Tb/s per engine, external lasers, and NVIDIA's claim of 3.5× better power efficiency and ~4× fewer lasers than pluggables. Broadcom's Tomahawk switch chips with CPO take a similar path. Optical NVLink out of the GPU package is the stated direction for the late-2020s scale-up fabric, which will make this module's flow the home of lasers, waveguides and fibre alignment as well as copper.

## Summary

- CoWoS exists because of three walls: the 858 mm² reticle, Poisson yield Y = exp(−A·D0) that punishes big dies, and HBM's ~1,600 signals per stack that must land on 55 µm bumps and enter the GPU through ~12 mm of edge.
- A CoWoS package is a sandwich: lid / TIM1 / dies on 40–55 µm microbumps / ~100 µm silicon interposer with 0.4–2 µm wiring and TSVs / 130–180 µm-pitch C4 bumps / 2–3 mm ABF substrate / 1 mm-pitch BGA balls.
- Energy per bit tracks wire capacitance: on-die ~0.01–0.1 pJ, 2.5D ~0.3–0.6, HBM ~3, board SerDes 5–10, pluggable optics 15–30; every hop off the package costs ~10×.
- CoWoS-S is a full silicon interposer; CoWoS-R an organic RDL one; CoWoS-L organic RDL with small LSI silicon bridges only under die edges plus TIVs. NVIDIA has been on CoWoS-L since Blackwell, and 5.5× reticle parts shipped in 2026.
- The flow: interposer fab (Bosch-etched 10 × 100 µm TSVs, 4–5 damascene RDL layers in stitched exposures) → chip-on-wafer (TCB or mass reflow, underfill, mold) → carrier bond, grind to 100 µm, reveal TSVs, C4 bumps → dice → module-on-substrate reflow → stiffener, lid, BGA → test; ~6–12 weeks.
- The order is forced by mechanics: a paper-thin interposer survives only after it is molded under its dies, and TSVs are 100 µm deep because a 10:1 hole can be etched and filled while a 77:1 hole cannot.
- A 1,000 W die at 0.8 V draws ~1,250 A, which is why most of its ~100,000 bumps carry power and ground rather than signals.
- The enemies are CTE mismatch (a 100 mm laminate grows ~0.26 mm more than silicon over a reflow, warping the stack by hundreds of µm), bump coplanarity, intermetallic growth, electromigration, and the impossibility of rework, which forces known-good die.
- Capacity went ~15k → 35k → 80k → ~130k wpm from 2023 to 2026 (analyst estimates), but packages per wafer fell 29 → 16 → 9 as interposers grew, so unit growth lagged, and TSMC is moving to 510 × 515 mm panels (CoPoS) around 2028–2029.
- Assembly costs ~$500–1,500 per GPU package on a ~$10,000 CoWoS-L wafer, small against a $30,000 price but the gate on every sale.
- SoIC hybrid bonding fuses oxide to oxide at room temperature and copper to copper during a 200–300 °C anneal with no solder; 6 µm pitch gives ~28,000 connections/mm² (~55× microbumps, ~470× C4), a 1 µm particle kills a millimetre, and alignment must be ~pitch/20.
- Intel EMIB/Foveros is the only full-stack alternative; Samsung I-Cube/X-Cube and the OSATs' fan-out bridges trail; TSMC holds >80% of high-end 2.5D/3D.
- Chiplets pay through yield, reuse and node mixing but cost D2D PHY area, ~0.25–0.5 pJ/bit and compound assembly yield; UCIe is the open link standard and NV-HBI (10 TB/s) the proprietary one.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"CoWoS is just a fancier way of putting a chip in a package."** → CoWoS builds a second, wire-only chip (the interposer) on a 300 mm wafer with front-end damascene and TSV processes, then assembles the product on that wafer. It is a fab process with a back-end finish, which is why OSATs could not simply copy it and why TSMC controls it.
- **"The silicon interposer is the expensive part, so CoWoS-L must be cheap."** → CoWoS-L replaces the big interposer with organic RDL plus small bridges but adds TIV plating, molding, grind-back, and the hardest warpage problem in the industry; its wafer price is higher than CoWoS-S's. It exists because a 3.3×+ silicon interposer would be worse, not because it is easy.
- **"Hybrid bonding is a finer-pitch microbump."** → There is no solder, bump, underfill or gap. Copper pads fuse into single grains by thermal expansion during anneal and the dielectric bonds covalently around them. That is why it reaches 6 µm and below, has no intermetallic reliability limit, and dies from a single micrometre particle.
- **"TSMC's AI bottleneck was wafer capacity."** → Front-end N4/N3 capacity could have supported on the order of ten times the accelerator volume shipped in 2023–2025 (this module's own order-of-magnitude estimate). The constraints were CoWoS wafers per month (15k → 80k), HBM stacks, and to a lesser degree ABF substrates.
- **"Chiplets are always cheaper than a big die."** → Yield math favours them, but D2D PHY area, ~0.25–0.5 pJ/bit of link power, extra assembly steps and compound assembly yield eat much of the gain. NVIDIA stayed monolithic until the reticle forced a split; AMD split Ryzen for reuse across products as much as for yield.
- **"HBM4 uses hybrid bonding."** → As of 2026 production HBM4 still stacks DRAM with microbumps and TC-NCF or MR-MUF; hybrid-bonded HBM is planned for HBM4E/HBM5 (~2027–2029). The HBM-to-GPU interface has always been microbumps on an interposer.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module consumes the outputs of Modules 14, 15, and 16: known-good GPU dies from wafer sort with copper-pillar microbumps already formed, known-good HBM stacks from SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron, and the large ABF build-up substrates from Ibiden, Unimicron, and their peers, plus an interposer that TSMC fabricates itself on 65 nm-class tools using the TSV, damascene, and CMP processes of Modules 09 and 12. It produces the single most valuable component in the chain, a lidded, ball-attached CoWoS package with two GPU dies and eight HBM stacks, worth ~$5,000–7,000 in parts and $30,000+ at sale, and it is the stage whose monthly throughput has set the ceiling on AI accelerator supply since 2023. The package goes to final test, burn-in, and system-level test in Module 18, where yield fallout at each assembly stage is counted, and then to the SXM module, HGX baseboard, and GB200/Rubin NVL rack of Module 19, where the cold plate on this module's copper lid becomes the first component of a 120 kW liquid-cooled rack.
