# Module 00: The Whole Supply Chain on One Page

Start with the object this course follows: a **GPU**, or graphics processing unit, used here to perform the arithmetic for AI. It is built from silicon chips and supporting parts. A **die** is one rectangular chip cut from a round silicon **wafer**, the shared surface on which many chips are made. The **package** joins dies, memory, a supporting carrier, a lid and solder balls into the object that gets soldered to a circuit board.

A single NVIDIA Blackwell GPU package contains two dies of roughly 800 mm² each, about 208 billion **transistors** (electrically controlled switches), and eight stacks of **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)**. HBM stacks **dynamic random-access memory (DRAM)** chips, which hold working data, beside the GPU so data can move between them rapidly. A silicon-and-organic **substrate**, the layered carrier, connects the dies to the circuit board. More than a dozen companies on three continents assemble the parts.

Its silicon was quartz in a mine in Norway, Brazil, or North Carolina roughly a year before the GPU booted. In between, the material passed through something like 1,500 discrete process steps, a **mask set** (the ~100 master patterns used to print the chip's layers) that cost tens of millions of dollars, machines that individually sell for $200–400 million, and a **fab** (a chip factory) that cost more than an aircraft carrier. No single person understands every step at the level of the engineers who own it. That is the problem this module solves: you need a map of the whole territory before you walk any part of it, so that when Module 08 spends 7,000 words on tin droplets you know why they matter and where they sit.

The chain is hard for one reason that recurs at every stage: the tolerances are absurd relative to the scale of production. A chip is built as roughly 100 patterned layers stacked on a silicon disc, each printed on top of the last. A leading-edge fab must place features a few tens of atoms wide across a 300 mm wafer (a disc the size of a dinner plate, 775 µm thick, about the thickness of a credit card), aligned to the previous layers within about 2 nanometers, roughly 10 silicon atoms side by side; a human hair, at ~70 µm, is 35,000 times wider than that alignment budget. It must repeat that on more than 100,000 wafers a month with a **defect density** below 0.1 per square centimeter, where a **killer defect** is any particle or pattern error that stops a die working and the defect density, **D0**, counts them per cm² of wafer: fewer than one killer defect in every 10 cm², or about one for every 1.2 H100-sized dies. Every stage in this module is a place where the industry solved that problem once, expensively, after which a small number of firms became the only ones who could do it. The last third of this module is about what those firms are and why they cannot be replaced quickly.

> **Intuition: the scale of the job.** Scale a wafer up by a million: the dinner plate becomes a disc 300 km across and 775 m thick. A 20 nm transistor feature is now a 2 cm coin, the 2 nm alignment budget is 2 mm, a hair is a road 70 m wide, and a 20 nm dust particle is a pebble that kills whichever coin it lands on. The fab prints ~100 layers of coins across the whole disc, each landing on the last to within 2 mm, a hundred thousand discs a month.

By the end of this module you should be able to do four things. First, name the 15 stages from quartz to data center and say roughly what each costs, how long it takes, and who does it. Second, explain why the value of a chip concentrates *after* the wafer leaves the fab: why a ~$350 die sits inside a ~$3,000 module that sells for ~$25,000, and why memory and packaging, not the logic wafer, dominate that bill. Third, name the three chokepoints (EUV lithography, leading-edge foundry, advanced packaging plus HBM) and give the physical or economic reason each exists, not just the company that holds it. Fourth, use the 60-step process spine near the end as an index: every later module is a magnifying glass on a few of its lines.

## Before you start

This module leans on nine ideas from school science and everyday economics. Modules 01 through 19 define each again, properly and in depth; here you need only the plain-language version.

- **Atoms and crystals.** Silicon is an element whose atoms, in a solid, sit in a repeating three-dimensional pattern, the **lattice**; the repeating unit (the **unit cell**) is 0.543 nm on a side. A **single crystal** means the pattern continues unbroken across the whole piece. (Module 02)
- **Semiconductors and doping.** Pure silicon barely conducts. Replacing about one atom in a million with boron (one electron fewer than silicon, leaving a missing electron or "hole": **p-type**) or with phosphorus or arsenic (one electron more: **n-type**) makes it conduct in a controlled way. The added atoms are **dopants**. (Module 10)
- **A transistor is an electrically controlled switch.** Current flows from a **source** to a **drain** through a thin **channel** of silicon only when the **gate**, an electrode just above the channel and insulated from it, carries the right voltage. (Module 11)
- **Voltage, current, power.** Voltage V is the electrical push, current I is the rate of charge flow, and power P is the rate of energy transfer: P = V × I. A GPU runs its transistors at about 1 V, so a 1,000 W chip draws ~1,000 A. Delivering that much current requires substantial connections even though the switches themselves are tiny. (Module 19)
- **Light and resolution.** Light spreads as it passes a patterned mask, so the image on the wafer is blurred rather than a perfect shadow. Shorter wavelengths help resolve finer detail. Lens design, illumination and chemistry also matter: features can be smaller than the wavelength, as Module 07 explains.
- **Purity notation.** "9N" means nine nines: 99.9999999% pure, one foreign atom per billion silicon atoms. "11N" is one in a hundred billion. (Module 01)
- **The scale ladder.** 1 m → 1 mm (a grain of sand) → 1 µm (a bacterium) → 1 nm (about four silicon atoms in a row). A hair is ~70 µm across. A wafer is 300 mm across and 0.775 mm (775 µm) thick; the smallest features printed on it are ~10–20 nm.
- **Yield and throughput words.** **Yield** is the fraction of parts that work. It measures success, not production speed: a fast factory can still produce many unusable chips. **Wafer starts per month (wpm)** is how many wafers a fab begins processing each month. **Cycle time** is how long one wafer takes from first step to last. (Modules 05, 13)
- **Money words.** **Capex** (capital expenditure) is money spent on plant and tools. **Gross margin** is (price − manufacturing cost) ÷ price. A **bill of materials (BOM)** is the list, and total cost, of everything that goes into a product. (Module 20)

## What a chip is, physically

Before the map, a picture of the thing being made. Everything in this module is either making a silicon disc, printing patterns on it, checking them, or connecting the result to the world.

**The wafer.** Every chip begins as a **wafer**: a disc of single-crystal silicon, 300 mm across and 775 µm thick, polished on its top face to a mirror. It is the unit of work through every fab step; nothing is done to one chip at a time.

**The die.** A **die** is one rectangular chip cut from that wafer at the end. A large GPU die such as the H100's is ~26 × 31 mm, a large postage stamp, and carries ~80 billion transistors; the two Blackwell dies are ~800 mm² each. A few dozen such dies fit on one wafer, all made at once, and the number that fit and the fraction that work are what the economics of the whole chain rest on.

**The transistor.** In the top ~100 nm of the silicon sit the transistors. Each is a switch: two doped regions, the **source** and the **drain**, separated by a short **channel**; above the channel, insulated from it by a few atomic layers of oxide, sits the **gate**. Voltage on the gate pulls electrons into the channel and current flows; remove it and the current stops. In modern chips the channel is a vertical fin (**FinFET**) or a stack of horizontal sheets wrapped on all sides by the gate (**nanosheet**, or **gate-all-around**); the shapes belong to Module 11. A GPU die holds tens of billions of these, spaced ~50 nm apart.

**The wiring.** Above the transistors sit 15–18 levels of copper wiring, each a set of parallel lines in an insulator, joined between levels by short vertical metal plugs called **vias**. Think of a city: the transistors are the buildings, the lowest levels the narrow streets between them, the upper levels the avenues, and vias the on-ramps. The lowest lines are ~25 nm apart; the top ones are micrometers wide and carry power.

**How every layer is made.** The structure is built one layer at a time by repeating six **unit processes**: **deposit** a thin film over the wafer (by **chemical vapor deposition (CVD)**, in which gases react on the hot surface and leave a solid, or a related method); **pattern** it by lithography; **etch** away the film where the pattern says so, or **implant** dopant atoms where the pattern leaves silicon exposed; **polish** the surface flat; and **clean and measure** before the next layer. **Lithography** is a photographic stencil at atomic scale: the wafer is coated with a light-sensitive film, **photoresist** (resist); light is shone through (or, for EUV, reflected off) a **mask**, a plate carrying one layer's pattern at 4× scale; a lens shrinks the image onto the wafer; the exposed resist is chemically changed and **developed** away; the etch then removes film only where the resist is gone. Roughly 70–100 masks, one per patterned layer, make up a leading-edge mask set. A mask does not print the whole wafer at once: one exposure prints one **field**, a rectangle of at most 26 × 33 mm (858 mm², the **reticle limit**; **reticle** is the industry's word for a mask once it is loaded in the scanner, and the two words are used interchangeably). The scanner then moves the wafer and repeats the exposure, ~90 times per wafer including the partial fields at the rim, to cover the disc (**step-and-repeat**), after which the next mask is loaded for the next layer. Any die, or interposer, larger than one field has to be stitched from several exposures, which will matter in the packaging section.

> **Intuition:** a chip is a 100-layer screen print at atomic scale, where each print must land on the last to within about 10 atoms across the whole plate. Where the analogy breaks: a screen printer only lays ink on top; a fab also digs into the layer below (etch) and shoots atoms into it (implant), so the layers interlock rather than stack.

**Node names.** "N3", "2 nm", "18A" are generation labels, not dimensions: no feature in an N2 transistor is 2 nm wide. Read them as model years; Module 11 explains what they track. (See also Common Misconceptions.)

**The package.** A die cannot be soldered to a circuit board: its connection points, the **pads**, are tens of micrometers apart while a board's lines are hundreds of micrometers apart, and the die is fragile, needs cooling, and would crack under the board's thermal expansion. So it is mounted face-down, via **bumps** (small solder or copper bumps on each pad), on a **substrate**, a laminated sandwich of copper wiring and resin that spreads the pad spacing out to solder balls ~1 mm apart. A metal lid, thermal paste, and the solder balls complete the **package**, which is what a robot places on the board. In a large GPU package an **interposer**, a slab of silicon or fine copper wiring, sits between dies and substrate to carry thousands of wires between neighbouring dies; the CoWoS subsection explains why.

**HBM.** A GPU needs to read memory faster than any board wiring can carry, so its memory dies, **DRAM** (dynamic random-access memory, the chips holding the data being worked on), are thinned, stacked 8–16 high, wired through with vertical copper columns called **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, and placed millimeters from the GPU on the same interposer. That stack is **high-bandwidth memory (HBM)**.

> **Why it matters downstream:** every stage in the rest of this module is one of four jobs: making the disc (Stages 1–5 in the table below), printing the layers on it (Stage 6), checking them (Stage 7), or connecting the die to the world (Stages 8–15). When a later module feels disconnected, ask which of the four jobs it belongs to.

A **tap** is one draining of molten silicon from the smelting furnace through a hole in its wall; the furnace itself never stops. **Trichlorosilane** is the liquid silicon compound (SiHCl3) that MG-Si is converted into so that it can be purified by distillation, and the **Siemens reactor** is the bell-jar in which that liquid's vapor is turned back into solid silicon rods. **Ingot**: the ~2 m single-crystal cylinder wafers are sliced from. **Epi**: an extra ultra-pure silicon layer grown on the wafer surface. **Wafer sort** and **bins**: the electrical test of every die on the wafer, and the categories (good, bad, fast, slow) each is sorted into. **OSAT**: outsourced semiconductor assembly and test company, a contractor that packages and tests chips; **KYEC** is a Taiwanese OSAT specializing in test. **Bumping**: depositing solder bumps on each die pad. **Known-good die**: a die that has passed wafer test.

**Rows 9–15 (memory, packaging and the system).** **Bridges**: small pieces of silicon wiring embedded only where two dies meet, an alternative to a full interposer. **SLT**: system-level test, running a real workload on the finished chip in a socket. **Burn-in**: running the part hot and at high voltage for hours to provoke early failures before shipment. **VRM**: voltage regulator module, the circuits that convert the board's supply down to the chip's ~1 V at ~1,000 A. **PCB**: printed circuit board. **SXM**: NVIDIA's mezzanine module, a GPU package on its own small board that plugs into a baseboard. **HGX**: NVIDIA's 8-GPU baseboard; **DGX**: NVIDIA's own server built on it; **"Bianca"**: the GB200 compute board with two Grace CPUs and four Blackwell GPUs. **NIC**: network interface card. **Tray**: one sled-like unit of a rack; **spine**: the bundle of copper cables joining the trays; **NVL72**: NVIDIA's 72-GPU liquid-cooled rack. **Hyperscaler**: a cloud company building data centers at gigawatt scale (Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta); **neocloud**: a newer GPU-rental cloud (CoreWeave and peers). **MW** in row 15: data centers are sized and priced by the electrical power they can deliver to the racks, in megawatts, not by floor area, because power and the cooling to remove it, not space, are what limits how many GPUs a building can hold; "$30–50M per MW" is the cost of the building, power gear and cooling for each megawatt of computing load.

> **Worked example: the value ladder of one wafer.** Follow one 300 mm wafer down the table.
> 1. A polished prime wafer enters the fab worth ~$150 (Stage 5).
> 2. It leaves ~3 months later as a processed N4 wafer priced at ~$16,500 (Stage 6): a hundredfold increase, all of it fab processing.
> 3. It carries ~60–65 gross H100-class dies, of which ~45–50 pass test and harvesting (harvesting: selling dies that have a few defective blocks switched off, as a slightly smaller product) (Stage 7; the arithmetic is in the Wafer sort subsection).
> 4. Each sellable die becomes one SXM module after HBM, packaging, test and board assembly (Stages 8–12), sold at ~$25,000–30,000 in 2023–2024.
> 5. 45 × $25,000 ≈ $1.1 million; 50 × $30,000 ≈ $1.5 million. One wafer's worth of dies becomes ~$1.1–1.5 million of product.
>
> Where does that value sit? Not in the wafer: the ~$16,500 of fab processing is about 1% of it, and each die is ~$350 of a ~$3,000 module bill of materials. Roughly half of that BOM is HBM, a quarter is packaging, and the amount above ~$3,000 must cover costs omitted from that estimated BOM as well as gross profit. A reseller price is not necessarily NVIDIA's realized selling price. The chain's value, and its constraints, sit after the wafer.

The second orientation fact is time. Add up the *Time* column: polysilicon to polished wafer is ~3–4 weeks (3–5 days in the Siemens reactor, ~2.5–3.5 days in the puller, 2–3 weeks of wafering); the fab is ~3 months; HBM stacking, CoWoS and test are ~1–2 months; boards, servers and racks another ~1 month; with shipping and queues between stages the total is roughly 6–9 months from polysilicon to a shipping server, of which the wafer fab is about half. Everything after the wafer is fast; everything before it is cheap.

## Stage by Stage

### Quartz to metallurgical-grade silicon

Silicon is 28% of the earth's crust, so the raw material is not scarce; the *pure* raw material is. The first job is to separate silicon from oxygen, and it is hard because silicon holds its oxygen tightly (the Si–O bond is one of the strongest in chemistry, which is why sand is so stable), so breaking it takes ~2,000 °C and enormous electrical energy, and because the product, even done well, is only 98–99% silicon, carrying everything that was in the ore and the carbon.

The mechanism is reduction: a more reactive element strips the oxygen from an oxide. Smelters charge lump quartz (typically 10–100 mm pieces, >98% SiO2, low in iron, aluminum, boron, and phosphorus) with carbon (coal, petroleum coke, charcoal, wood chips) into a **submerged arc furnace**: a brick-lined pot several meters across in which three graphite electrodes carry tens of thousands of amps into the pile. The arc burns inside the **charge** (the pile of quartz and carbon loaded into the furnace; the word has nothing to do with electric charge here), hence "submerged", at ~2,000 °C at the electrode tips, and the carbon takes the oxygen away as carbon monoxide: SiO2 + 2C → Si + 2CO. Liquid silicon collects at the bottom and is **tapped**, drained through a hole in the furnace wall into ladles, every few hours, while fresh charge is added on top; the furnace runs for years without stopping. Each tonne of silicon consumes ~11–13 MWh of electricity, roughly an American household's electricity for a year, which is why smelters sit next to cheap hydro (Norway, Quebec, Brazil) or coal (Yunnan, Xinjiang).

The product, **metallurgical-grade silicon** (MG-Si) at 98–99% purity, costs $2–3 per kilogram, and around 80–85% of the world's ~4.6 million tonnes per year (USGS, 2024) comes from China. Roughly 40% of MG-Si goes to aluminum alloys and ~25% to silicones; about a third is refined into polysilicon, almost all of it for solar (the solar boom lifted this share from ~10–15% a decade ago), and only a small fraction of that reaches electronic grade. Two impurities get special attention already at the mine: boron and phosphorus. Both are dopants, electrically active in silicon at a few parts per billion, so they change the material's conductivity rather than merely sitting in it, and boron in particular is hard to remove downstream: it neither distils out cleanly in the next stage nor segregates out during crystal growth. **Segregation** is the second, free purification step the chain gets: as a crystal freezes from a melt, most impurity atoms fit the lattice badly and prefer to stay in the liquid (sea ice is fresher than the sea it froze from, for the same reason), so crystal growth itself sweeps impurities toward the last liquid to freeze. Boron is the exception: it fits the silicon lattice well and enters the solid almost as readily as it stays in the melt (Module 02 gives the coefficient, 0.8), so whatever boron is in the melt ends up in the crystal. The ore has to be low in it from the start.

> **Intuition:** the raw material is dirt cheap; the purity is what you pay for. Quartz sells for cents per kilogram, MG-Si for $2–3, electronic-grade polysilicon for $20–40, and a polished wafer for ~$100–150 for its ~130 g of silicon, on the order of $1,000 per kilogram. Every rung is the same element with fewer wrong atoms in it.

A separate, much smaller quartz stream matters just as much: the **high-purity quartz** (HPQ) used to make the crucibles in which silicon crystals are grown (Stage 4), where any impurity in the crucible wall dissolves into the melt. The best deposits, at Spruce Pine, North Carolina (Sibelco and The Quartz Corp), supply most of the inner-layer crucible quartz on earth.

> **What can go wrong: a hurricane in a mountain town.** Hurricane Helene shut the Spruce Pine mines for a few weeks in September–October 2024, cutting the world's main supply of crucible-grade quartz. Production restarted within weeks and stockpiles covered the gap, but the chain has obscure single points of failure well before any chip is made, and every crucible maker now runs a formal second-source program (Module 01).

### Polysilicon: 99% to 99.999999999%

Electronic-grade silicon must be roughly 9N–11N pure (99.9999999% to 99.999999999%), a ten-million- to billion-fold improvement over MG-Si (from one wrong atom in a hundred to one in a billion, or one in a hundred billion): in a cube of silicon a thousand atoms on a side (about a quarter of a micrometer), at most one atom may be something else. No treatment of a solid gets near that. The trick is a liquid intermediate. Liquids can be purified by **distillation**, boiling a mixture and condensing the vapor, because each substance boils at its own temperature; solids cannot. So the strategy is to turn silicon into a liquid compound whose boiling point differs from those of the impurity compounds, distil it many times, and turn it back into solid silicon.

The workhorse route is the **Siemens process**. MG-Si powder is reacted with hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas at ~300 °C in a **fluidized bed**, a bed of powder held floating by upward gas flow so that it stirs like a liquid, to make **trichlorosilane** (TCS, SiHCl3), a liquid that boils at 32 °C: Si + 3HCl → SiHCl3 + H2. Because TCS is a liquid, it can be purified by **fractional distillation** (distillation through a tall column in which the vapor condenses and re-boils many times on its way up, so that compounds whose boiling points differ by only a few degrees still separate), repeated through many columns until metal chlorides, boron, and phosphorus are at parts-per-trillion levels (one in 10¹²). The purified TCS is then decomposed with hydrogen onto electrically heated silicon "U-rods" at ~1,100 °C in a **bell-jar reactor**, an inverted metal dome sealed to a base plate through which the rods and gases pass, growing polysilicon rods over 3–5 days to ~150–200 mm diameter. The deposition is the first reaction run backwards, SiHCl3 + H2 → Si + 3HCl, and the same chemistry can do both jobs because the reaction's direction depends on temperature: making TCS from silicon releases heat, so it is favoured cold (~300 °C), while breaking TCS back into silicon absorbs heat, so it is favoured hot (~1,100 °C). The hydrogen supplies the atoms that carry the chlorine away as HCl, which is recycled to the fluidized bed; only a fraction of the TCS converts on each pass over the rods, and the rest is condensed and sent round again. The alternative **fluidized bed reactor** (FBR) route decomposes silane (SiH4) onto silicon seed granules and produces free-flowing beads that are convenient for crucible charging.

Wacker (Germany and USA), Hemlock (USA), Tokuyama (Japan), and OCI (Korea, with a Malaysian electronic-grade line from 2026) dominate electronic grade; Chinese producers (GCL, Tongwei, Daqo) dominate the far larger solar-grade market, where oversupply pushed prices below $5/kg in 2024–2025. Electronic grade sells for on the order of $20–40/kg, and a 300 mm ingot needs ~350–450 kg of it. The reactors and distillation columns are not a chokepoint: they are custom pressure vessels designed in-house or by a handful of engineering firms (historically GT Advanced Technologies and Centrotherm, now largely Chinese fabricators), and the scarce thing is the operating recipe and the decades of contamination discipline, not the hardware. Module 01 covers all of this at mechanism level.

> **What can go wrong: the impurity you cannot see until it is a crystal.** Polysilicon at 6N and at 11N look identical, and the failure that matters is invisible: a few parts per trillion of boron or phosphorus slipping through because a distillation column ran slightly off its set point, a seal leaked air (and with it dust, which carries boron), or the steel and graphite of the reactor, crusher and handling line shed metal and carbon into the product. So every lot is qualified the slow way: a small test crystal is grown from it by float zone (a crucible-free method, so nothing but the feedstock touches the melt), and its resistivity, which is set by the net dopant concentration, is measured; for the last parts per trillion, the crystal is cooled to ~10 K and its infrared absorption read, since at that temperature each dopant's electron sits on its own atom and absorbs at a wavelength unique to that element (Module 01). A lot that fails is not scrapped but sold down to solar grade. The mechanical failures are the rods: over 3–5 days at ~1,100 °C a power interruption drops the whole batch, a rod that grows lopsided touches its neighbour and shorts, and a rough "popcorn" surface traps chlorine-bearing residue that must be etched off before the chunks can be melted. Two rules follow from all this, and the rest of the chain inherits them: measure the product electrically, because chemistry alone cannot see the level you care about, and treat every touch as a contamination event.

### Crystal growth: the Czochralski puller

Polysilicon is **polycrystalline**: many small crystals meeting at **grain boundaries** where the lattice of one does not line up with the next. A transistor needs a single crystal, and here is why. A grain boundary scatters electrons, so current flows differently on each side of it; it traps dopant atoms, so the doping is wrong near it; and it etches and oxidizes at a different rate from the crystal around it, so every fab step behaves differently there. A transistor straddling a boundary is a different device from its neighbour, and a chip is only possible because a billion transistors on one die are identical. A single crystal, with a known **lattice orientation** (which way the atomic planes face relative to the wafer surface), makes every square micrometer of the wafer the same.

> **Intuition:** the Czochralski process is cotton candy on a stick. A seed crystal is dipped into molten silicon and slowly withdrawn; the liquid freezes onto it as it rises, and because atoms freezing onto a crystal continue its pattern, the whole 2 m ingot ends up with the seed's lattice. Where the analogy breaks: the "candy" is 400 kg of silicon at 1,420 °C under argon, and one stray particle or a moment's temperature wobble can spoil the pattern for the rest of the pull.

The **Czochralski (CZ) process** melts ~400 kg of polysilicon plus a few milligrams of dopant (boron for p-type, phosphorus or arsenic for n-type; ~5 mg of boron in 400 kg is only ~10 parts per billion by weight, far lighter than the one-in-a-million of Before you start, because the wafer's background doping is deliberately light and the fab later implants the parts-per-million and heavier doping only inside the transistor regions, Module 10) in a quartz crucible held inside a graphite **susceptor**, a cup that supports the crucible (which softens at the working temperature) and carries the heater's warmth into it, under argon at ~1,420 °C (silicon melts at 1,414 °C). A small seed crystal is dipped in and slowly withdrawn while rotating; the melt freezes onto it in the seed's orientation. The operator first pulls a thin **Dash neck** a few millimeters in diameter to shed **dislocations**, lines in the crystal where a plane of atoms ends and the lattice around it is strained: the thermal shock of dipping always creates some, and growing thin and fast makes every one run out of the side surface before the crystal widens. That works because a dislocation cannot travel straight down the pull axis: it propagates along the crystal's slanted atomic planes, so in a neck a few millimeters wide each one reaches the side surface within a few millimeters of growth and is gone, and the crystal above the neck starts clean. The crystal is then flared out to 300 mm plus a few mm of grinding allowance, and the body is pulled at ~0.5–1 mm/min. A 2 m body therefore takes ~40–60 hours; with melt-down, seeding, tailing, and cooldown the cycle is ~2.5–3.5 days per crystal.

The quartz crucible slowly dissolves into the melt, so the crystal always contains oxygen, and its amount must be controlled rather than eliminated. Some oxygen is useful: it stiffens the wafer against warping in the fab's hot steps, and oxygen clusters deep in the wafer trap stray metal atoms away from the transistors. Too much, or in the wrong place, and those clusters (precipitates) form near the surface and kill devices. Because the crucible dissolves, anything else in its wall ends up in the crystal too: that is why crucible quartz purity, and the Spruce Pine stream above, matter so much. Separately, **magnetic Czochralski (MCZ)** applies a strong magnetic field across the melt. Molten silicon is an electrical conductor, and a conductor moving through a magnetic field is dragged by it (the physics of an eddy-current brake), so the field calms the melt's churning convection and lets the operator set how much dissolved oxygen the currents carry from the crucible wall to the growing crystal. The crucible is destroyed every run. Module 02 covers the thermodynamics, the defect physics (vacancies, missing atoms; interstitials, extra atoms squeezed between lattice sites; and **COPs**, crystal-originated particles, tiny voids left where vacancies cluster), and why 450 mm wafers never happened.

> **What can go wrong: structure loss.** If a dislocation enters the crystal part-way down the body (a particle off the crucible wall, a temperature wobble), the single crystal is spoiled from that point on. The operator sees the four **growth ridges** vanish (four faint lines running the length of a dislocation-free crystal, at 90° intervals, where flat crystal facets at the growth front meet the surface; they disappear within a few millimeters of a dislocation entering, because the facets need an undisturbed lattice to form), and the only fix is to melt the crystal back below the point of entry, re-neck, and regrow: hours of growth lost and the melt's dopant and oxygen history disturbed (Module 02).

### Wafering: from ingot to a 775 µm mirror

The problem is to turn a 2 m cylinder into ~1,500–2,000 discs, each 775 µm thick, flat to a few tens of nanometers, with a surface clean and smooth enough to print 20 nm features on, without wasting too much of a ~$100–200k crystal. The ingot is cropped, ground to exactly 300 mm, given a notch for orientation, and sliced by a **diamond-wire multi-wire saw** in which a single ~100 km wire loops through the ingot thousands of times. The **kerf**, the material the wire turns to dust with every cut, is ~150 µm per wafer, so roughly a sixth of the ingot is lost in the saw. Each wafer is then **lapped** (rubbed flat between two rotating plates with abrasive slurry between them) or ground, edge-profiled, chemically etched to remove the layer the saw damaged, double-side polished, and given a final **chemical-mechanical polish (CMP)** on the front side to sub-nanometer roughness. CMP is a pad plus a slurry of nanometer-sized silica particles in a mildly corrosive liquid: the chemistry softens the top few atoms and the mechanics wipes them away, which is how a surface becomes smooth to a fraction of an atom over a whole wafer. Cleaning follows the **RCA** sequence, named for the company that published it in 1970 (SC-1 ammonia/peroxide for particles, SC-2 HCl/peroxide for metals).

The spec that matters most to the fab is local flatness. The lithography lens, like a camera, is sharp only within a slab a few tens of nanometers deep (its **depth of focus**), and every part of the wafer surface must sit inside that slab in each exposure field. The metric is **SFQR** (site front-surface least-squares range: the peak-to-valley deviation from a best-fit plane over one site), typically <20–30 nm over a 26 × 8 mm site. Scaled up, 25 nm over 26 mm is ~0.1 mm over the length of a football field. Many logic wafers get an additional **epitaxial** layer (a few µm of ultra-pure silicon grown by CVD, continuing the crystal) to bury crystal defects and provide a controlled doping profile. Five companies (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic, SK Siltron) supply ~90% of 300 mm wafers, and a polished prime wafer costs ~$100–150. Their tools come from a second, mostly Japanese tier: 300 mm wire saws from Komatsu NTC (with Takatori and Precision Surfacing Solutions as alternatives), double-side polishers from Precision Surfacing Solutions (the former Lapmaster Wolters line, built in Germany), Fujikoshi and SpeedFam, and the flatness and particle metrology almost entirely from KLA. Module 03 walks the full sequence; Module 04 covers **SOI** wafers (silicon on insulator: a thin silicon film on a buried glass layer) and **compound semiconductors** (crystals of two or more elements, such as gallium nitride or gallium arsenide), plus every consumable the fab will now burn through.

> **What can go wrong: a broken wire, a failed site, a particle that survived.** The costliest event is a wire break mid-cut: the web goes slack, the partly cut ingot can no longer be re-registered to the old cut planes to a micrometer, and the block is usually scrapped or re-cut at a new pitch, with every partial slice lost. A site that fails SFQR is a subtler failure: the scanner's focus sensor can follow a slow warp of the wafer but not a sharp local step, so in the affected field the lines print blurred and the dies there die months later in the fab. The wafer maker therefore measures every wafer on an interferometric flatness tool (KLA's WaferSight), and an out-of-spec wafer is downgraded to a monitor or test grade rather than shipped as prime. Particles are the third: a particle that survives the RCA clean is buried under the fab's first film and prints as a defect in every layer above it, so each wafer is scanned by a laser surface inspector (KLA's Surfscan) that counts light-scattering points down to a few tens of nanometers, and a prime wafer is allowed only a few tens of them. Metal contamination from a bad chemical batch is caught electrically, by measuring how long excess electrons survive in the silicon (**minority-carrier lifetime**, which metal atoms shorten); a bad batch is traced back to the specific tool and lot, the same discipline the fab will apply at every step.

### The wafer fab: three months and a thousand steps

This is where $150 becomes $17,000, and the primer is the picture to hold: ~100 patterned layers, each made by deposit → pattern → etch or implant → polish → clean and measure. A leading-edge fab is a ~$20–30 billion building (TSMC's Fab 18 in Tainan, Intel's Ohio site, Samsung Taylor) that runs 100,000+ **wafer starts per month** (wpm) through ~1,000–1,500 process steps organized around ~70–100+ photomask layers. Each layer means a lithography pass (coat resist, bake, expose, bake again, develop), an etch or implant, a resist strip and clean, a deposition, often a CMP, and metrology (measurement) between many of them. The rule of thumb is ~1.3–1.5 days of cycle time per mask layer: ~70 layers × ~1.3 days ≈ 13 weeks, which is where the ~3-month (12–14 week) cycle time comes from, and 100+ layers at the slower rate push toward 20 weeks. Most of those days are waiting: a wafer is actually being processed for a small fraction of its life in the fab, and the rest is queueing in front of ~1,000 tools that every wafer revisits dozens of times. Module 05 shows why running those tools near full utilization makes the queues explode. Wafers travel in 25-wafer **FOUPs** (Front Opening Unified Pods, sealed plastic boxes) on an overhead transport system that makes millions of moves per day.

The flow divides into three regimes: the switch, the plug into the switch, and the wiring above it.

- **FEOL** (front end of line): everything that makes the transistors. Isolation trenches between devices, **wells** (doped regions in which each kind of transistor sits), the gate stack, source/drain growth, the replacement metal gate. This is where the ~2 nm alignment tolerances and the ~20–25 EUV layers live.
- **MOL** (middle of line): the contacts, the metal plugs that connect each transistor's source, drain and gate up to the wiring, at the tightest **pitches** (center-to-center spacing of repeated features) in the chip, ~25–30 nm, using tungsten, cobalt, or ruthenium.
- **BEOL** (back end of line): ~15–18 levels of copper wiring built by **dual damascene**: etch **trenches** (the grooves that become lines) and **vias** (the holes that become vertical connections) into a **low-k dielectric** (the **dielectric constant**, k, measures how much an insulator lets charge on one wire attract charge on its neighbour; the higher k is, the more charge the two wires store between them, the more **capacitance**, and the longer every switch takes to charge them, so a low-k insulator makes neighbouring wires slow each other less), line them with a thin tantalum nitride (TaN) **barrier** that stops copper diffusing into the insulator, fill with electroplated copper, and polish flat. Pitches go from ~24–28 nm at **M1** (the first, finest metal level) to microns at the top.

Modules 05 through 13 cover this in detail: the building (05), deposition (06), lithography (07, 08), etch (09), doping (10), the transistor (11), the wiring (12), and the metrology and yield discipline that holds it together (13). The equipment is where most of the money goes: ~70–80% of a fab's capex is tools, from ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, Tokyo Electron, and KLA.

> **Worked example: how many EUV tools a 100k wpm N3 fab needs.** Two definitions first. **Dose** is the light energy per unit area the resist needs, in millijoules per square centimeter; a higher dose means the scanner dwells longer on each field, so fewer wafers per hour. **Availability** is the fraction of hours the tool is actually able to expose wafers rather than being down for maintenance, calibration or repair.
> 1. An N3-class flow has on the order of 20–25 EUV layers. At 100,000 wafer starts per month that is 100,000 × 20–25 ≈ 2.0–2.5 million EUV wafer-passes per month.
> 2. ASML's current EUV scanner, the NXE:3800E, is rated at ~220 wafers per hour at a 30 mJ/cm² dose. Run at a realistic ~75–80% availability and with typical doses above 30 mJ/cm² on critical layers, it delivers roughly 100–120k wafer-passes per month.
> 3. 2.0–2.5 million ÷ 100–120k ≈ 20–25 EUV scanners for the fab.
> 4. At ~$200–220 million each, that is ~$4–5.5 billion of EUV scanners alone, roughly 15–20% of the fab's capex. Module 05 counts ~30 scanners after adding ~20% headroom for **tool dedication** (assigning each layer to specific scanners so that it always prints the same way, which leaves some tools idle while others queue) and ramp, and its ~$6–8 billion "EUV fleet" figure is the all-in lithography number including High-NA tools, spares and reticle infrastructure (the sealed pods, storage, inspection and cleaning tools that keep hundreds of masks defect-free): the same estimate at a higher level of contingency.
>
> Multiply by the ~1,000-tool count of a full fab (etch, deposition, CMP, implant, metrology, wet clean, track) and you get to $20–30 billion without much effort.

> **What can go wrong: one particle, one overlay miss, one bad week.** A single ~20 nm particle landing on a wafer before a critical layer shadows the pattern and can kill the die under it. An **overlay** miss, one layer printed 3 nm off the layer below instead of 2 nm, opens or shorts vias across a whole exposure field. And a **D0 excursion** (an **excursion** is any period in which a process drifts outside its normal range; here, a week in which one tool quietly starts adding defects) removes a few percent of the fab's output until someone notices. Fabs catch all three the same way: inline inspection and metrology after every critical layer (Module 13) find the defects days or weeks before wafer sort would find the dead dies, and statistical process control flags which tool to fix.

> **Why it matters downstream:** the defect density D0 the fab achieves, in killer defects per cm², sets how many dies per wafer work and therefore what each die costs. The worked example at Wafer sort turns a D0 of 0.1/cm² into ~45–50 sellable H100 dies and ~$350 per die; at 0.2/cm² the perfect-die yield of an 8.14 cm² die falls from ~44% to ~20%.

### Wafer sort, thinning, dicing

A finished wafer contains dozens of dies and there is no way to know from the outside which of them work, so every die must be exercised electrically before anyone spends more money on it. The wafer is probed on a **wafer prober** with a **probe card** (FormFactor, Micronics Japan, Technoprobe) whose thousands of needles contact the die's pads while an **automatic test equipment (ATE)** system (Advantest V93000, Teradyne UltraFLEX) runs **structural tests** (which check that the wiring and memory arrays are physically intact by shifting known patterns through them and reading them back) and **functional tests** (which run real instructions). The result is a wafer map: each die is marked good, bad, or binned by speed and by which redundant blocks work. For a large GPU die, this **harvesting** is everything: an H100 SXM shipped with 132 of 144 **streaming multiprocessors** (SMs, the identical compute blocks that tile the die) enabled precisely so that a wafer with several defects per die still yields sellable parts.

> **Worked example: die per wafer and yield for the H100.** Name the symbols: d = wafer diameter = 300 mm; A = die area = 814 mm² = 8.14 cm² (about 26 × 31 mm) on TSMC's 4N process (an N5-family node), with 80 billion transistors; D0 = killer defect density = 0.1 defects per cm².
> 1. *How many dies fit?* A 300 mm wafer has π(d/2)² ≈ 70,700 mm² of area, but a rectangular die cannot use the curved edge. A standard estimate subtracts an edge term: N ≈ π(d/2)²/A − π·d/√(2A) = 70,686/814 − 942.5/40.35 = 86.8 − 23.4 ≈ 63 gross dies. A real placement with a ~3 mm **edge exclusion** (the ring at the wafer's rim where no die is placed, because films are non-uniform there and handling equipment grips the wafer by its edge) lands in the same place, ~60–65 gross dies.
> 2. *How many are perfect?* Assume killer defects land at random with an average of A × D0 per die. The probability that a die has none is the Poisson zero term, Y = e^(−A·D0) = e^(−8.14 × 0.1) = e^(−0.814) ≈ 44%, or ~28 perfect dies per wafer.
> 3. *Harvesting.* A defect in one of the 144 SMs kills only that SM, and NVIDIA sells the part with 132 enabled. What decides how much that helps is the share of die area that is redundant. On the H100 die photo the SM arrays cover roughly ~50–60% of the area (an estimate; NVIDIA does not publish it), and the 60 MB L2 cache, of which 50 MB is enabled, is also partly spare, so somewhat more than half of all defects land somewhere that can be switched off. Call that fraction X ≈ 0.5–0.6. A die is sellable if it is perfect or if its defects all hit disableable area: sellable ≈ 44% + (1 − 44%) × X ≈ 44% + 56% × 0.5–0.6 ≈ 72–78%, the ~70–80% used here, or ~45–50 sellable dies per wafer. (The sum assumes at most a few defects per die, true at A × D0 ≈ 0.8, and that no die loses more than 12 SMs.) To reuse this for another chip, put in its own X: a chip with no redundant blocks sells only its perfect dies.
> 4. *Cost per die.* At ~$16,500 per 4N wafer, $16,500 ÷ 45–50 ≈ $350 per sellable die.
>
> The fab's defect density, the die's area, and the designer's redundancy together set the price of the silicon. Module 13 gives the yield models (Poisson is the simplest; real fabs use models in which defects cluster) and Module 14 the test economics.

Before packaging, three things happen to the wafer. It gets **bumps**: the die's pads are tens of µm across and no soldering process can join them to a substrate directly, so each pad receives a solder microbump or copper pillar 20–100 µm across, in a mini-fab process (sputter an **under-bump metallization** layer, a metal stack that solder will wet and that stops it attacking the pad; pattern it; electroplate the bumps). It is **thinned**, ground from 775 µm to a few hundred µm, because a thick die is a poor heat path and too tall for its package, and to tens of µm for dies that will be stacked, whose vertical wires can only be so long. And it is **diced** into individual dies by a diamond blade saw (wet, and it chips the edges), by **stealth dicing** (a laser focused *inside* the silicon creates a weakened layer that is cracked along by stretching the tape under the wafer; clean and dry), or by **plasma dicing** (etching the streets between dies with fab plasma chemistry; slowest but gentlest). DISCO of Japan supplies ~70–80% of the world's dicing and grinding equipment. Module 14 covers test; Module 16 covers backgrind, dicing, and the classic packaging flows.

### HBM: a memory stack built like a skyscraper

> **Intuition: why memory is stacked.** Bandwidth is the number of wires multiplied by the speed of each wire. Ordinary DRAM sits centimeters from the processor on the board and talks over a few dozen wires per module, each driven very fast, because long board wires are expensive to add and hard to drive. HBM takes the other approach: ~1,000–2,000 wires per stack, each driven slowly, which only works if the wires are a few millimeters long. Short wires mean the memory must sit beside the GPU on the same interposer, and a GPU needs far more memory than fits in a few square centimeters of single-layer dies. So the dies are stacked, and the wires run vertically through them.

An H100 carries 80 GB of HBM3 in five stacks; a B200 carries 180 GB of HBM3E in eight; Rubin carries 288 GB of HBM4. Each stack is 8, 12, or 16 DRAM dies, each thinned to ~30 µm, wired vertically by **through-silicon vias** (TSVs): copper columns a few µm wide running straight through the thinned die, so a signal passes from one die to the next without leaving the stack. The data path to the GPU is 1,024 bits wide for HBM3 and 2,048 for HBM4, and once power, ground, command and spare lines are added each die carries several thousand TSVs. The DRAM dies sit on a **logic base die**, a bottom die made on a logic rather than a memory process, which manages the stack and drives the interface to the GPU; for HBM4 it is made at a foundry (TSMC or Samsung) rather than in the DRAM fab.

Stacking is a second manufacturing process with its own yield, and the three makers bond differently. SK hynix pioneered **mass-reflow molded underfill** (MR-MUF): dies are placed with solder microbumps between them, the whole stack is heated so all the joints melt at once, and the gaps are filled with a molded resin that also carries heat out. Samsung and Micron use **thermo-compression bonding with non-conductive film** (TC-NCF): a thin adhesive film goes between dies and each die is pressed on hot, one at a time. HBM4 pushes toward **hybrid bonding**, polished copper faces pressed directly together with no solder, for 16-high stacks: each die's face is a flat mosaic of copper pads in oxide, polished to a fraction of a nanometer; when two such faces touch, the oxide areas stick at room temperature by the same surface forces that make two clean glass plates cling together, and a bake at a few hundred °C then makes the copper pads expand and grow into each other, atom to atom, until each joint is a single piece of metal with no solder and no gap. HBM is a full DRAM fab flow (itself ~1,000 steps and increasingly EUV-patterned) plus this stacking flow, and a stack is worth several hundred dollars, roughly 3–5x the price per bit of ordinary **DDR5** (the standard memory modules in servers and PCs) on the contract-price comparisons made in 2023–2024. Revenue-share and bit-share estimates for 2025 differ by source and accounting basis; this course has not reconciled them into a verified price-per-bit multiple. The extra cost includes the base die, stacking and testing that an ordinary DDR5 die does not carry. Treat the multiple as "several-fold to an order of magnitude", depending on year and basis, not as a fixed number. As of Q2 2026 (Counterpoint), SK hynix holds ~50% of the HBM market, Samsung ~33%, and Micron ~18%. HBM was sold out for both 2025 and 2026 before those years began. Module 15 covers DRAM, NAND, and HBM manufacturing.

> **What can go wrong: one dead die in twelve.** A 12-high stack has twelve chances to fail plus eleven bonded joints, and one dead die or one bad joint scraps the whole stack, base die included. That is why every DRAM die is tested before stacking, why the finished stack is tested and burned in again as a **known-good stacked die** (KGSD) before it ships to the packager, and why bonding yield, not DRAM yield, limits HBM output. The second failure mode is **warpage**: twelve dies bonded with resin and solder bow as they cool, and a stack that is not flat to a few micrometers cannot be bonded to the interposer.

### Advanced packaging: CoWoS

The GPU die and its HBM stacks must sit within a few millimeters of each other on something that can carry thousands of wires between them, and no circuit board can.

> **Intuition: the wire-pitch ladder.** Every carrier in electronics can route wires only so close together. A printed circuit board manages lines ~50–100 µm apart; a package substrate built from **ABF** (Ajinomoto Build-up Film, an epoxy film laminated one layer at a time; more below) ~10 µm; a silicon interposer, made with fab lithography, ~1 µm; the chip itself, <0.1 µm. The GPU-to-HBM link needs thousands of wires in a strip a few millimeters wide, which only silicon-class patterning can route. So a slab of silicon wiring with no transistors on it, the **interposer**, sits between the dies and the substrate: silicon for the fine wires between dies, organic substrate for the coarse wires to the board.

**CoWoS** (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate) does this in two bonding steps. In **chip-on-wafer** (CoW), the logic and HBM dies are **flip-chip** bonded (turned face-down so their bumps land on the carrier) at ~40 µm microbump **pitch** (bump-to-bump spacing) onto a full 300 mm interposer wafer. The interposer is a silicon interposer with TSVs and a few copper layers (CoWoS-S); an organic **redistribution layer (RDL)** interposer, a few layers of fine copper wiring built on a wafer or mold compound with no transistors (CoWoS-R); or, for Blackwell, a molded RDL interposer with small embedded silicon **bridges**, pieces of silicon interposer placed only where two dies meet (**CoWoS-L**). The interposer wafer is thinned to expose the TSVs, bumped, and diced. In **wafer-on-substrate** (WoS), each CoW module is bonded at ~130–150 µm **C4** pitch (**controlled collapse chip connection**, IBM's 1960s name for the classic flip-chip solder bump, which slumps to a controlled height when melted) to a 10–20-layer organic build-up substrate made with ABF (the Ajinomoto epoxy film introduced above, laminated one layer at a time with laser-drilled via holes) by Ibiden, Unimicron, Shinko, or AT&S. The module is then **underfilled** (a resin drawn by capillary action under each die and cured, so the solder joints survive the different expansion of silicon and substrate), **lidded** (a copper lid glued on over thermal paste to spread heat), and **balled** (a grid of ~1 mm solder balls attached to the bottom for the board). The finished B200 package is ~100 × 100 mm and sits on an interposer of more than three **reticle limits** in area: the reticle limit is the largest area one lithography exposure can print, 26 × 33 mm = 858 mm², and anything larger must be stitched from several exposures, which is why big interposers are slow and expensive to make.

TSMC does effectively all of the CoW step for NVIDIA and AMD, and its capacity, ~35k wafers per month at end-2024, ~70–80k at end-2025, targeting ~120–140k by end-2026 (as of mid-2026), has been the single tightest constraint on AI accelerator supply. The WoS step and some CoWoS-S volume are increasingly outsourced to ASE/SPIL and Amkor. Module 17 covers the interposer process, hybrid bonding (SoIC), Intel's Foveros and EMIB, and the warpage physics that make 100 mm packages hard.

> **Worked example: CoWoS wafers to packages.** This is the arithmetic behind "CoWoS is the bottleneck."
> 1. TSMC's total capacity exceeded 17 million **12-inch-equivalent** wafers in 2025 (capacity counted in 300 mm wafers; a 200 mm wafer counts as 0.44 of one), i.e. ~1.4 million wafer starts per month across every node from 0.18 µm to N2. Of that, the N5/N4 family runs on the order of 150k wpm and N3 on the order of 100–130k wpm.
> 2. Suppose NVIDIA takes ~40k N4-class wafers per month for Hopper- and Blackwell-family dies (a plausible 2025 figure; customer allocations are not disclosed at this granularity). At ~45–50 sellable H100-class dies per wafer that would be ~1.8–2.0 million dies per month, far more than were actually shipped. This hypothetical allocation does not establish which stage constrained actual shipments: total foundry capacity spans many nodes and customers, and cannot be treated as available N4 capacity.
> 3. TSMC's CoWoS capacity at end-2025 was ~70–80k wafers per month, of which NVIDIA is estimated to take ~60%, or ~45–50k.
> 4. Packages per wafer depend on interposer size. An H100 interposer is ~2.5 reticle fields, and a CoWoS-S wafer yields ~29–30 H100-class packages. A CoWoS-L interposer for Blackwell is ~3.3 reticle fields (~2,800 mm²), so after edge loss a 300 mm interposer wafer holds only ~16 of them. Do not try to reproduce these with the die-per-wafer formula from the Wafer sort example: it gives ~19 and ~12, because its edge term was derived for dies much smaller than the wafer and over-penalizes very large rectangles, and for objects this size only an explicit placement drawing gives the count, which depends on the exact rectangle shape and on how the stitched exposures are laid out. The ~29–30 and ~16 are trade-press placement estimates, not TSMC disclosures; treat them as ±20%.
> 5. ~45k CoWoS wafers per month at, say, ~16–20 packages each gives ~700k–900k packages per month, roughly 8–10 million accelerator packages per year: about right for the 2025–2026 shipment estimates and an order of magnitude below what the front-end wafer supply could support.

> **What can go wrong: a 100 mm package that will not stay flat.** Silicon, copper, resin and solder expand at different rates, and a 100 × 100 mm assembly carrying ten dies bows by tens of micrometers as it cools from the reflow oven. Too much bow and corner solder joints lift or crack, or the lid no longer touches every die. And the assembly is the most expensive object in the chain before it is even tested: one bad HBM stack or one lifted joint scraps a ~$6,000+ assembly of two logic dies and eight HBM stacks, which is why every die and stack is tested before it goes on and every CoW module is tested again before substrate attach.

### Final test, module, server, rack, data center

The packaged GPU goes through package-level ATE test, often a **burn-in** at elevated temperature and voltage, and increasingly **system-level test** (SLT) in a socket that runs real workloads. Burn-in exists because of the **bathtub curve**: parts fail at a high rate in their first hours (**infant mortality**, from marginal defects that were not quite fatal at test), then at a low rate for years, then at a rising rate as they wear out. Heat and voltage accelerate the first phase because the mechanisms behind infant mortality (metal atoms migrating along a thin wire, a marginal gate oxide breaking down, a hairline crack in a solder joint opening) are chemical and mechanical processes whose rates rise exponentially with temperature and steeply with the electric field across the oxide, so a few hours at ~125 °C and ~10–20% over nominal voltage stand in for weeks of ordinary service (the exact acceleration is fitted per product; Module 18). A part that would have died in a customer's rack in week two can be made to die on the test floor in hour six. An **escape** is a bad part that passed test. Fallout here is a few percent, but each unit is worth tens of thousands of dollars, so test cost per part (tens to hundreds of dollars) is small relative to escapes. Module 18 covers this.

The tested package is soldered to an **SXM** module PCB (NVIDIA's mezzanine format: the GPU on its own small board that plugs into a baseboard) with its voltage regulators. An H100 SXM draws up to 700 W, a B200 up to 1,000 W, a GB300 up to 1,400 W, and the chip runs at ~1 V, so a 1,000 W GPU draws ~1,000 A. Why that is hard: at 1,000 A, every milliohm of resistance between regulator and die drops 1 V, the entire supply, and turns 1,000 W into heat (P = I²R), so the current has to be carried over millimeters, not centimeters, through thick copper planes in the PCB, and delivered by dozens of **VRM** (voltage regulator module) phases in parallel, each converting the board's supply down to ~1 V and handling tens of amps, packed as close to the package as they will fit. And the load is not steady: when a kernel starts, the GPU's current can rise by hundreds of amps in nanoseconds, faster than any regulator can respond, so hundreds of capacitors on the module and inside the package supply the charge for the first nanoseconds and are refilled before the next swing. That is why the copper planes in the PCB are as much a power-delivery problem as a wiring one, and why the power stages (from Monolithic Power Systems, Infineon and Renesas) are a named line in the bill of materials.

Eight SXM modules go onto an **HGX** baseboard with **NVSwitch** chips, NVIDIA's switch chips that connect every GPU to every other over **NVLink**, its GPU-to-GPU link; or, in the Grace-Blackwell era, a **compute tray** (one sled-like unit of a rack) carries two **Grace** CPUs (NVIDIA's Arm-based server processor) and four Blackwell GPUs. Eighteen compute trays and nine NVSwitch trays connected by a copper NVLink **spine** of ~5,000 cables make a **GB200 NVL72** rack: 72 GPUs, 36 CPUs, ~120–140 kW (the peak draw of roughly a hundred homes, in one cabinet), selling for ~$3–4 million. At that density air cooling is physically out: air-cooled racks top out around ~30–40 kW, and a 1,000 W die of ~8 cm² runs at ~125 W/cm², roughly ten times the heat flux of an electric stovetop element, which no fan blowing over a heatsink can carry away. So the rack is liquid-cooled through **cold plates** (metal plates with water channels clamped onto every chip, so the water is millimeters from the silicon) fed by a rack-level **coolant distribution unit** (CDU, the pump-and-heat-exchanger box that circulates the water; from specialists such as CoolIT, Vertiv and Motivair). ODMs (original design manufacturers: Foxconn/Hon Hai, Quanta, Wistron/Wiwynn, Inventec) build these in Taiwan, Mexico, and increasingly Texas.

> **Worked example: cooling one NVL72 rack.** Name the symbols: P = heat to remove = 130 kW; c = specific heat of the coolant (water 4,180 J per kg per °C; air 1,005 J per kg per °C); ρ = density (water 1,000 kg/m³; air 1.2 kg/m³); ΔT = temperature rise allowed in the coolant = 10 °C. The mass flow needed is P ÷ (c × ΔT).
> 1. Water: 130,000 ÷ (4,180 × 10) ≈ 3.1 kg/s, about 3 liters per second or ~190 liters per minute through one cabinet, the flow of roughly ten garden hoses.
> 2. Air: 130,000 ÷ (1,005 × 10) ≈ 13 kg/s, which at 1.2 kg/m³ is ~11 m³ of air per second through one cabinet, a gale, and it would still have to reach a die running at ~125 W/cm².
> 3. The ratio: per liter, water carries 4,180 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 3,500 times more heat than air for the same temperature rise. That number, not fashion, is why every rack above a few tens of kilowatts is plumbed.

A 100,000-GPU cluster is ~1,400 such racks, ~150–200 MW of **IT load** (the power drawn by the computing equipment itself, before cooling), and on the order of $10 billion of hardware, plus a network of **InfiniBand** or **Ethernet** switches (the two competing high-speed networking standards) and hundreds of thousands of **optical transceivers** (the modules that convert electrical signals to light for the fiber links between racks). The building is priced per megawatt (~$30–50M per MW all-in, table row 15) because every megawatt of IT load has to be delivered by transformers, switchgear and busbars, backed by batteries and generators, and then removed again by chillers and cooling towers; that electrical and cooling plant, not the racks, is most of the construction cost, and it is why a build takes 12–24 months: a grid connection and its large transformers had lead times of years as of 2025–2026, so power, not chips, is the next constraint in line. Module 19 traces the H100, Blackwell, and Rubin product path from **RTL** (register-transfer level, the code that describes a chip's logic; step 1 of the master flow) to rack.

> **Why it matters downstream:** a $3–4 million rack with one dead GPU out of 72 does not run at 71/72 speed; the training job that spans the rack stops, someone pulls the tray, and the replacement takes days. That is why the test chain spends hundreds of dollars per part and days of burn-in per rack: an escape costs a thousand times what the test did.

## Money Flow and Industry Structure

The rest of the module is about companies and money, so here are the business words it uses. **EDA** (electronic design automation) is the software that turns a chip description into the mask drawings. **IP** (intellectual property) here means pre-designed, reusable circuit blocks licensed to chip designers (a CPU core, a memory controller). A **SerDes** (serializer/deserializer) is the circuit that sends data over one very fast wire; a **PHY** (physical-layer interface) is the circuit that drives a memory or network interface. A **fabless** company designs chips and owns no factory; a **foundry** manufactures wafers for others; an **IDM** (integrated device manufacturer) designs and makes its own; an **OSAT** packages and tests for others. **Gross margin** is (price − manufacturing cost) ÷ price; **capex** is money spent on plant and tools; **process control** is the family of measurement and inspection tools that tell a fab whether each step worked; a **sub-tier** supplier is a supplier's supplier.

The semiconductor industry sold roughly $790 billion of chips in 2025 (WSTS/SIA: $791.7 billion, up 25.6%), and WSTS's Spring 2026 forecast (May 2026) projects ~$1.51 trillion for 2026, driven overwhelmingly by memory prices (the memory segment alone is forecast up ~250%, to about 3.5x its 2025 revenue, almost all of it price rather than volume: DRAM and HBM contract prices rose several-fold from 2025 into 2026 as AI demand outran supply, for the reason Chokepoint 3 gives, while bit shipments grew far less), with AI accelerators and HBM the fastest-growing slices (as of mid-2026; treat the 2026 figure as a price-driven peak rather than a new floor). Beneath that revenue line sit seven distinct kinds of company, and the whole industry is best understood as money and wafers flowing between them.

| Layer | What they do | Business model | Leading firms (share where meaningful) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EDA and IP** | Design software; reusable blocks (CPU cores, SerDes, memory PHYs) | Software licenses, royalties | Synopsys, Cadence (together ~70% of EDA), Siemens EDA; Arm, Synopsys/Cadence IP, Rambus |

The dollar flow for a GPU runs like this. NVIDIA pays Synopsys and Cadence for design tools and Arm for the Grace CPU cores; pays TSMC ~$17,000 per N4 wafer plus a separate per-package price for CoWoS; pays SK hynix, Micron, or Samsung on the order of $15–20 per gigabyte for HBM (estimates vary; sometimes purchased by TSMC on NVIDIA's behalf for packaging); pays Ibiden or Unimicron for substrates; pays KYEC or ASE for test; and pays Foxconn or Quanta to build boards and racks. TSMC in turn spent ~$41 billion of capex in 2025 and has guided $60–64 billion for 2026 (as of mid-2026; the January 2026 guidance quoted in Module 20 was $52–56 billion), most of it with ASML, Applied, Lam, TEL, and KLA, and buys wafers from Shin-Etsu and SUMCO, resist from JSR and TOK, gases from Linde and Air Liquide, and mask blanks from Hoya and AGC. Every one of those suppliers has its own sub-tier: ASML buys optics from Zeiss and lasers from Trumpf; Zeiss buys ultra-low-expansion glass from Corning.

Note where the cost is: the die is only ~10–15% of the BOM; HBM is ~40–50%; packaging is ~20–25%. That ranking is why Modules 15 and 17 exist.

> **Worked example: why a fab needs every customer.** The number that explains foundry concentration is depreciation per wafer.
> 1. A leading-edge fab costs ~$30 billion. Depreciate it over ~5 years (roughly the accounting life foundries use for tools): $30B ÷ 5 ≈ $6 billion per year, owed before a single wafer, chemical, or engineer is paid for.
> 2. At 100k wpm the fab makes 100,000 × 12 = 1.2 million wafers a year. $6B ÷ 1.2M ≈ $5,000 of depreciation per wafer at full load.
> 3. At half load (50k wpm, 600k wafers a year) the same $6B ÷ 0.6M ≈ $10,000 per wafer.
> 4. Compare with the price of an N3 wafer, ~$18–20k. A full fab spends ~25–30% of its wafer price on depreciation; a half-empty one spends ~50%, and the ~55–60% gross margin that funds the next node is gone.
>
> This is why nobody builds a leading-edge fab without the customers to fill it, why TSMC, with hundreds of customers, can build one node after another while Samsung and Intel struggle to fill theirs, and why the foundry business concentrates.

The most important structural fact is the split between *design* and *manufacturing* that TSMC's founding in 1987 made possible. Morris Chang's idea, the **pure-play foundry**, was a company that would manufacture for anyone and compete with no one: it would never design its own chips, so a customer's design was safe with it. Before that, you needed a fab to be a chip company. After it, NVIDIA could become the most valuable company on earth while owning no factories, and TSMC could spread the ~$30 billion cost of a node across hundreds of customers, which the worked example above shows is the only way the arithmetic closes. The consequence is that manufacturing has concentrated into a handful of firms at each layer, which brings us to the chokepoints.

> **Why it matters downstream:** the split has a cost that the rest of the course keeps meeting. A fabless company's output is capped by whatever capacity its suppliers allocate to it, so NVIDIA prepays TSMC and the HBM makers years ahead and still cannot buy more CoWoS than exists; every one of the three chokepoints below is a step it buys rather than controls; and a week of disruption in Hsinchu reaches every fabless company on earth at once, because they all queue at the same fabs. Module 20 puts numbers on this: cost per transistor by node, wafer price by node, the fab cost curve, and what a Taiwan disruption or a decade of export controls would do to each layer of the table above.

## The Three Chokepoints

Ask "what would stop the world from making more Blackwells next quarter?" and the answer is always one of three things.

### 1. EUV lithography: one company, one tool, one supply chain

Start with the physics. The smallest feature a lens can print scales with the wavelength of the light (Module 07 gives the equation). The industry used 193 nm **deep-ultraviolet (DUV)** light for two decades, stretching it with water between lens and wafer (**immersion**: water bends light more steeply than air, its refractive index at 193 nm being 1.44 against 1.0, so the lens can collect a wider cone of light from the mask and resolve finer lines) and by printing one layer as two or four interleaved exposures (**multiple patterning**), until features reached a tenth of the wavelength and the tricks ran out. **Extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** light at 13.5 nm is 14 times shorter. The price is that every material absorbs it, including air and glass. There is no lens, because nothing is transparent at 13.5 nm; the whole optical path is in vacuum; and the image is formed by mirrors, each coated with dozens of alternating molybdenum and silicon layers and reflecting only ~70% of what hits it. No single surface reflects 13.5 nm light usefully; each Mo/Si interface returns only a fraction of a percent. The trick is spacing: the layer pairs are laid down at half a wavelength apart, so the ~40–50 weak reflections from successive interfaces travel distances differing by whole wavelengths and add in phase, like echoes timed to reinforce one another, and the stack as a whole reaches ~70%. After the ~10 reflections from source to wafer only a few percent of the light survives. Two consequences follow. The light source must be enormously powerful, which is why it is a tin plasma: 50,000 droplets per second of molten tin are hit by a CO2 laser and flash into a plasma that radiates at 13.5 nm. Tin is the fuel because tin atoms stripped of roughly 8–13 electrons happen to radiate strongly in a band centred at 13.5 nm, which is the wavelength Mo/Si mirrors reflect best; in effect the wavelength was chosen for the mirror and the fuel chosen to match it. And the mirrors must be almost perfect, because at this wavelength a bump a few atoms high distorts the image.

Every leading-edge node from TSMC's N7+ and Samsung's 7 nm-class equivalent (2019) onward, and the Intel equivalents, uses EUV for its finest layers; SMIC reaches 7 nm-class with DUV multi-patterning at lower yield. Every EUV scanner on earth is made by ASML in Veldhoven, Netherlands. The current workhorse NXE:3800E costs ~$200–220 million, weighs ~180 tonnes, ships in ~40 freight containers (three 747 freighters or ~20 trucks), and exposes ~200–220 wafers per hour. The High-NA EXE:5000/5200 raises the **numerical aperture** (NA: how wide a cone of light the optics can accept; larger NA prints finer features) from 0.33 to 0.55 with **anamorphic** optics (different magnification in the two directions, 4× one way and 8× the other, so the mirrors can accept the wider cone without becoming impossibly large; the price is a half-size exposure field). It costs ~$380–400 million and, as of mid-2026, is in early production use at Intel (on some 18A layers; 14A, the first node designed around it, targets **risk production**, early low-volume production while yield is still being learned, in 2027), under evaluation at Samsung and SK hynix, committed by TSMC for its A14P generation (~2028) after skipping it at A16, and being qualified at **imec** (the Belgian semiconductor research institute where ASML and the chipmakers share early tools). Module 08 has the customer-by-customer status. ASML recognized system sales for 44 EUV systems in 2024 and 48 in 2025; shipments and revenue recognition can fall in different periods; ~350 had shipped cumulatively by end-2025, over half of them to TSMC. Inside ASML's tool sit a Zeiss projection optics box (mirrors polished to ~50 pm **RMS surface figure**, the root-mean-square height error of the mirror surface; 50 picometers is about a quarter of one silicon atom, achieved in one Zeiss factory in Oberkochen), a Trumpf drive laser, a Cymer (ASML-owned) source, and frames and modules from VDL and others. EUV has never been licensed for China (since 2019); since September 2023 the most capable immersion DUV scanners (NXT:2000i and above) also require Dutch licences, with metrology and further immersion tools added in 2024–2025, while older immersion tools still ship to non-sanctioned Chinese fabs. Module 08 is entirely about this machine.

> **What can go wrong: the source stops.** The tin plasma coats the **collector**, the large mirror that gathers its light, and the droplet generator and laser run at the edge of their ratings; when any of them fails, a $200 million tool stops exposing and, with it, the ~100k+ wafer-passes a month it was delivering. Every lot waiting for that layer queues, and because a wafer visits EUV 20–25 times, an outage anywhere in the fleet slows the whole fab. That is why fabs spread each EUV layer across several qualified tools and why ASML keeps resident engineers and spare collectors at every EUV fab.

### 2. Leading-edge foundry: TSMC, and Taiwan

TSMC manufactures more than 90% of the world's most advanced logic wafers (5 nm-class and below), essentially all of it in Taiwan: Fab 18 (N5/N3) in Tainan, Fab 20 (N2) in Hsinchu, Fab 22 (N2) in Kaohsiung. Its capacity exceeded 17 million 12-inch-equivalent wafers in 2025 (12-inch means 300 mm; a 200 mm wafer counts as 0.44 of one), or ~1.4 million per month across all nodes; N3 alone runs on the order of 100–130k wpm and N2 is ramping through 2026. Samsung's SF3/SF2 and Intel's 18A (their 3 nm- and 2 nm-class node names) are the only alternatives, and both have struggled to win external customers at volume; Intel 18A is in high-volume production for **Panther Lake** (Intel's 2026 laptop processor) but external foundry volume remains small as of mid-2026. TSMC's Arizona Fab 21 began N4 production in late 2024, is slated for N3 volume in the second half of 2027, and will bring N2 later, but Arizona output is a few percent of Taiwan's (as of mid-2026). Rapidus in Japan targets 2 nm in 2027. The concentration is not an accident: a leading-edge node costs ~$20–30 billion of fab plus several billion in R&D, and the amortization worked example above shows why only the firm with the most customers can afford it: an empty fab pays ~$10,000 of depreciation per wafer, a full one ~$5,000.

Money is only the first of three reasons the position is so hard to attack; the other two are physical. The second is **yield learning**. A new node does not start at D0 = 0.1/cm²: it starts several times higher, and D0 falls only as wafers are run, failing dies are cut open, each defect mechanism is traced to a tool or recipe and removed, one mechanism at a time (Module 13). That learning needs volume, volume needs customers, and customers only commit to a node whose yield is already known, so the firm with the most wafers learns fastest and pulls further ahead each generation; TSMC's ~1.4 million wafer starts a month are a learning rate as much as a revenue line. The third is that a node is not a blueprint but ~1,000 tuned recipes and the several thousand engineers who tuned them. Moving it to a new site means copying every recipe and re-qualifying every tool ("Copy Exactly", in Intel's phrase; Module 05) with a workforce that has not yet seen the failure modes, which is why Arizona trails Tainan by years on the same node, and why the design side is tied to the same place: designers only design for a node with a proven **PDK** (process design kit, the rulebook describing what the fab can make; step 2 of the master flow) and a mature library of tested circuit blocks, and those exist first, and sometimes only, for the node with the most customers.

> **What can go wrong: a node that does not ramp, and an island that shakes.** The best-documented failure is a node that misses its yield schedule: Intel's 10 nm process, planned for 2015–2016, did not reach healthy yields until 2019, and its foundry ambitions and product roadmap paid for the gap for years, and Samsung's 3 nm-class node, the first to use gate-all-around nanosheets, was widely reported to have struggled with yield in its first years, which is a large part of why it struggled for external customers (Module 05). The geographic failure mode is seismic. Taiwan's fabs sit on base isolation and every tool shuts down on a seismic trigger, scrapping only the wafers mid-process; after the magnitude 7.4 Hualien earthquake of April 2024 TSMC reported most tools recovered within a day and full production within ~3 days, at a cost well under 1% of a quarter's revenue (Module 20). That is the manageable case. The tail case, a larger quake near Tainan or a blockade, has no recovery time to quote, which is why customers and governments fund fabs in Arizona, Japan and Germany that cost more per wafer than Taiwan's.

Module 05 and Module 20 explain how a **GIGAFAB** (TSMC's name for a site of 100k+ wpm) is organized and what the concentration risk means.

### 3. Advanced packaging and HBM: the AI-era bottleneck

For AI accelerators specifically, the tightest constraint from 2023 through 2026 has been neither EUV nor wafers but the two steps *after* the wafer: CoWoS capacity and HBM supply. Both are hard for the same reason: they are new, high-value process flows with **yield learning curves**, and the demand for them roughly quadrupled in two years. A new flow starts with failure modes nobody has seen, and yield improves only by running material and fixing what fails, one mode at a time; so capacity that exists on paper, as installed tools, is not the same as capacity that yields, and the gap closes over quarters, not weeks. TSMC's CoWoS ramp from ~35k to ~120–140k wafers per month (end-2024 to end-2026 target, as of mid-2026) required entire new fabs (AP7 in Chiayi, AP8 in Tainan), and even so analysts estimated a supply gap of ~20% entering 2026, narrowing toward ~10% as the new sites ramp.

On the memory side, published HBM bit-share and revenue-share estimates are not directly interchangeable, and SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron each diverted a large slice of DRAM wafer capacity to HBM. An HBM die needs more wafer area per stored bit than an ordinary DRAM die: each die gives up area to TSV columns and the keep-out zones around them, carries a much wider interface and more redundancy, and the stack needs a base die that stores nothing at all. As a rule of thumb an HBM bit consumes roughly 3x the wafer area of a DDR5 bit (Module 15 puts the die-area part alone at ~1.5–1.8x, with the base die, stacking losses and spares making up the rest), so a hypothetical 5% of bits at three times the wafer area per bit would consume about 14% of a two-product wafer mix, and more at SK hynix, which is why ordinary DRAM prices rose sharply in 2025–2026. HBM4, with its 2,048-bit interface and logic base die made at TSMC or Samsung foundry rather than in the DRAM fab, ships in volume for Rubin in 2026. Modules 15 and 17 cover the mechanisms.

> **What can go wrong: capacity on paper versus capacity that yields.** A CoWoS line or an HBM stacking line is specified in wafers per month of tool capacity, but its output is that number times a yield that starts low. A new interposer size, a new bonding film, or a twelve-high stack instead of eight-high each reset part of the learning curve, and the difference between 60% and 90% stacking yield is a third of the world's HBM. Analysts' capacity numbers are tool counts; the shipment numbers that matter lag them by the time it takes to learn.

There is a fourth candidate chokepoint that is cheaper but no less absolute, and it is a side note here and Module 20's subject: Japanese materials. Japan supplies ~90% of **photoresist** (the light-sensitive coating that lithography patterns), ~55–60% of silicon wafers, most **mask blanks** (the flat, ultra-pure quartz plates, carrying a reflective multilayer for EUV, on which each mask's stencil is written; Hoya and AGC), and 100% of ABF (Ajinomoto).

None of these is expensive, and none is protected by a monopoly on the underlying chemistry. What protects them is **qualification lock-in**. A resist or a blank is not a commodity bought by specification; it is tuned into each layer's recipe over months of test wafers, and its exact formulation, batch behaviour and defect statistics are baked into that layer's **process window** (the range of dose, focus and etch settings over which the layer still prints and yields; a new material shifts the window, and every setting tuned to the old one has to be found again). Changing supplier means re-qualifying every affected layer, which for a resist used on dozens of layers is a multi-year program with yield risk at every step, so no fab does it unless forced. Modules 04 and 20 return to it.

## Master Process Flow: Sand to Rack in ~60 Steps

This is the spine of the course. Each step is at the granularity of "STI formation" or "M1 dual damascene," the level at which fab engineers talk about the flow; later modules expand each into its unit processes (litho, etch, deposition, clean, metrology). Where a step repeats many times it appears once with a note. Each step has a first line naming it the way engineers do and a second line in plain language saying what physically happens and why, with every acronym expanded at first use. You are not expected to retain the sub-items on first read; the step names and their order are the point.

**Vocabulary for this list**, recapped from the primer:

- The six unit processes: deposit a film, pattern it by lithography, etch it, implant dopants, polish flat (CMP), clean and measure. Every fab step below is a loop of these.
- Transistor parts: source, drain, channel, gate; the gate is insulated from the channel by a thin dielectric (an insulator).
- A **via** is a vertical hole filled with metal joining two wiring levels; a **trench** is the groove that becomes a wire.
- A **hard mask** is a tougher stencil layer (a nitride or carbon film) into which the resist pattern is first transferred, because resist alone is too soft and thin to survive a deep etch.
- An **anneal** is a heat treatment that repairs crystal damage and activates dopants (settles them into lattice sites where they conduct).

**Stage 0: Design and masks (Module 19, Module 04)**

1. Architecture and RTL design; verification (Synopsys, Cadence tools; ~2–4 years for a new GPU)  
   Architects decide what the chip does; designers write it as **RTL** (register-transfer level code, a text description of every storage element and the logic between them); verification engineers simulate it for years, because a bug found after the masks are written costs tens of millions of dollars and months.
2. Synthesis, place and route, timing closure, DRC/LVS signoff against the foundry PDK  
   Software turns the RTL into transistors and wires (synthesis), decides where each of billions of them sits (place) and how they connect (route), adjusts until every signal arrives on time (timing closure), and checks the drawing against the foundry's rulebook, the **PDK** (process design kit: the files describing what the fab can make), with **DRC** (design rule check: are all shapes wide enough and far enough apart?) and **LVS** (layout versus schematic: do the drawn shapes form the intended circuit?).
3. Tape-out: GDSII/OASIS layout; OPC and mask data prep (compute-intensive, weeks)  
   **Tape-out** is the moment the finished layout (a **GDSII** or **OASIS** file, the two standard formats describing every polygon on every layer) goes to the foundry. **OPC** (optical proximity correction) then pre-distorts every polygon so that, after the blurring of the lens, what prints is what was drawn.
4. Mask making: e-beam write (multi-beam, ~10–20 h per critical layer), etch, inspect, repair, pellicle; a full N3/N2 mask set costs ~$20–30M+  
   Each layer's corrected pattern is written into an absorber film on a quartz plate by an electron beam (**multi-beam**: a writer such as IMS Nanofabrication's splits the beam into ~262,144 beamlets that write in parallel, because a single beam flashing the pattern one rectangle at a time would take days per mask), etched, inspected pixel by pixel, repaired where a defect is found, and covered with a **pellicle** (a transparent membrane that keeps dust out of the focal plane). Why a set costs tens of millions: the mask is drawn at 4× the wafer scale, but after OPC its pattern is far denser than the drawn layout, with correction features smaller than anything that prints, and every feature over the ~100 cm² of the plate must be right, since a single defect prints on every field of every wafer; an EUV mask blank (Hoya, AGC) carries the same Mo/Si multilayer as the scanner mirrors, and a buried bump a few nanometers high in that stack prints as a defect, so blanks are inspected with 13.5 nm light itself (Lasertec) and blanks with no printable defect are rare and priced accordingly; and an EUV pellicle must be a membrane tens of nanometers thick that passes ~90% of the light while surviving the source's heat. Masks are made in the foundries' own mask shops and by Photronics, Tekscend (formerly Toppan) and DNP; the writers come from IMS Nanofabrication (Austria, Intel-owned) and NuFlare (Japan). Module 04 covers the flow.

**Stage 1: Raw material to wafer (Modules 01–03)**

5. Quartz mining and beneficiation  
   Quartz rock is blasted, crushed, washed and sorted (**beneficiation**: raising the ore's grade by physical separation) into lumps of >98% SiO2; the deposit's trace boron and phosphorus decide what the lumps are good for.
6. Carbothermic reduction to MG-Si (submerged arc furnace)  
   Quartz and carbon are heated by an electric arc at ~2,000 °C; the carbon takes the oxygen away as CO, and liquid silicon of 98–99% purity is tapped from the furnace bottom.
7. Hydrochlorination of MG-Si to trichlorosilane  
   Ground MG-Si reacts with HCl gas at ~300 °C in a fluidized bed to make TCS, a liquid; the silicon is now in a form that can be distilled.
8. Multi-column distillation of TCS to 9N–11N  
   The TCS is boiled and recondensed through a series of tall columns; each pass leaves behind impurities with higher or lower boiling points, until the wrong atoms are down to parts per trillion.
9. Siemens CVD deposition of polysilicon rods (or FBR granules)  
   Pure TCS and hydrogen flow over silicon rods heated to ~1,100 °C in a bell jar; the gas decomposes and silicon deposits on the rods for 3–5 days (or, in an FBR, onto floating seed granules that grow into beads).
10. Rod harvest, crushing, etch-clean, packaging  
    The rods are broken into chunks, etched to strip surface contamination picked up in handling, and sealed in bags; from here on, every touch is a contamination risk.
11. Crucible charge and melt-down (Czochralski puller, Ar, ~1,420 °C)  
    ~400 kg of chunks are loaded into a quartz crucible, the chamber is filled with argon so nothing oxidizes, and the heater melts the charge over several hours.
12. Seed dip, Dash neck, shoulder, body growth (MCZ), tail, cooldown  
    A seed crystal touches the melt, a thin neck is pulled to shed dislocations, the crystal is widened to 300 mm (the shoulder), the 2 m body is pulled at ~0.5–1 mm/min under a magnetic field that steadies the melt, the diameter is tapered off (the tail) to avoid thermal shock, and the ingot cools for hours.
13. Ingot crop, diameter grind, notch, resistivity/orientation test  
    The seed and tail ends are cut off, the body is ground to exactly 300 mm, a notch is ground along its length to mark the crystal orientation for every later machine, and resistivity (which reveals the dopant concentration) and orientation are measured.
14. Diamond-wire multi-wire sawing into wafers  
    A single ~100 km diamond-coated wire wound into a web of parallel strands is drawn through the ingot, cutting ~1,500–2,000 slices at once over many hours.
15. Lapping/grinding, edge rounding, damage etch  
    The saw leaves the slices wavy and cracked in their top microns; lapping between abrasive plates makes them flat and parallel, the sharp edge is rounded so it will not chip, and a chemical etch dissolves the cracked layer.
16. Double-side polish and front-side finish CMP  
    Both faces are polished to remove the last roughness, then the front face gets a final chemical-mechanical polish to an atomically smooth surface, the one the fab will print on.
17. RCA clean, laser mark, flatness/particle inspection; epitaxial layer growth if specified  
    The two-step RCA chemistry strips particles and metals, a laser scribes an identification code, the wafer is measured for flatness and scanned for particles, and if ordered a few µm of ultra-pure silicon are grown on top by CVD to bury crystal defects.
18. Ship in cassettes/FOUPs to fab  
    Wafers are sealed in cassettes or FOUPs under nitrogen, and the wafer maker's job ends. Value so far: ~$100–150.

**Stage 2: FEOL, transistor formation (Modules 05–11; each step includes its own litho/etch/clean/metrology loop)**

19. Incoming clean; zero-layer alignment mark etch  
    The wafer is cleaned on arrival and the first pattern ever printed on it is a set of alignment marks, etched into the silicon, to which every one of the ~100 later layers will be positioned.
20. Channel material prep: for GAA nanosheets, epitaxial Si/SiGe superlattice (3–4 pairs); for FinFET, none  
    For a **gate-all-around (GAA)** transistor, alternating layers of silicon and silicon-germanium (**SiGe**), each a few nm thick, are grown on the surface; the SiGe layers are sacrificial and will later be removed to leave the silicon sheets that become channels. A FinFET carves its channel from the bulk wafer.
21. Fin/nanosheet-stack patterning: hard-mask deposition, EUV or SAQP mandrel/spacer patterning, high-aspect-ratio Si etch  
    A hard mask is deposited, the fin pattern is printed by EUV or by **SAQP** (self-aligned quadruple patterning: print one line, the **mandrel**, coat its sides with a spacer film, remove the mandrel, repeat, so one printed line becomes four at a quarter of the pitch), and a deep **high-aspect-ratio** etch (aspect ratio is height divided by width; the fins are many times taller than they are wide, which is hard because the etching gas must reach the bottom of a narrow slot and the etched by-products must get back out) cuts tall thin fins or stacks into the silicon. (See the worked example on EUV tool count in the wafer fab section.)
22. **STI formation**: liner oxidation, flowable/HARP oxide fill, STI CMP, oxide recess (fin reveal)  
    **STI** (shallow trench isolation) fills the gaps between fins with oxide so neighbouring transistors cannot leak into each other: a thin oxide liner is grown, the gaps are filled with an oxide that flows in as a liquid-like film (**flowable** CVD oxide) or with **HARP** (high-aspect-ratio process, Applied Materials' name for a CVD oxide that fills narrow deep gaps without leaving voids), the excess is polished off, and the oxide is etched back to expose the top of each fin.
23. Well and Vt implants (n-well, p-well, anti-punch-through), anneal  
    Ions of boron or phosphorus are fired into selected regions to create the **wells** (the p-type or n-type background each kind of transistor sits in), to set each device's **threshold voltage (Vt)** (the gate voltage at which it switches on), and to add a buried doped layer that stops current sneaking beneath the channel (**anti-punch-through**); an anneal then repairs the damage and activates the dopants.
24. Dummy gate stack: dummy oxide, amorphous-Si deposition, hard mask, gate patterning (EUV), gate etch  
    A temporary **dummy gate** of **amorphous silicon** (silicon deposited with no crystal order, its atoms jumbled like a glass, which deposits smoothly and etches cleanly) is built where the real gate will go, because the real gate materials would not survive the high-temperature steps that follow (the **gate-last** flow); it is printed with EUV and etched. (See the worked example on EUV tool count.)
25. Gate spacer: ALD low-k SiOCN, anisotropic spacer etch  
    A thin insulating film of silicon oxycarbonitride (**SiOCN**, chosen for its low dielectric constant) is deposited by **ALD** (atomic layer deposition: one atomic layer per gas pulse, perfectly conformal) over everything, then etched straight down (an **anisotropic** etch) so it remains only on the gate's vertical sidewalls, as the **spacers** that set the distance from gate to source/drain.
26. Source/drain recess etch; inner-spacer formation for GAA (SiGe lateral recess, ALD fill, etch-back)  
    The silicon either side of the gate is etched away to make room for the source and drain; in a GAA device the SiGe layers are also recessed sideways under the spacers and the pockets refilled with insulator (**inner spacers**), which will insulate the future gate from the source and drain.
27. Embedded epitaxial S/D: SiGe:B for PMOS, Si:P for NMOS (selective epi, ~600–700 °C)  
    The source and drain are regrown as crystal, boron-doped SiGe for p-type (**PMOS**) transistors and phosphorus-doped silicon for n-type (**NMOS**), deposited only on exposed silicon (selective epitaxy); the SiGe also squeezes the channel (strain), which makes holes move faster.
28. Contact etch-stop liner (SiN) and ILD0 (flowable oxide) deposition; CMP to expose dummy gate top  
    A silicon nitride liner is laid down as the layer later contact etches will stop on, the surface is buried in the first **inter-layer dielectric (ILD0)**, an oxide insulator, and CMP planarizes it until the tops of the dummy gates show.
29. **Replacement metal gate** (gate-last): dummy (poly)silicon removal; for GAA, channel release (selective SiGe etch); interfacial SiO2; ALD HfO2 high-k; work-function metals (TiN, TiAlC, TiN); W or Co fill; gate CMP  
    The dummy gate is dissolved out, leaving a trench (it is the amorphous-silicon film of step 24, which has crystallized into polysilicon during the anneals since, so engineers call it "dummy poly"); in a GAA device the SiGe between the silicon sheets is etched away too, so each sheet hangs free (**channel release**); a few atomic layers of SiO2 are grown, then **hafnium oxide (HfO2)**, a **high-k** dielectric (high dielectric constant: it insulates like a very thin oxide while being physically thicker, which stops gate leakage), is deposited by ALD around every sheet; **work-function metals** (titanium nitride and titanium aluminum carbide, whose choice sets the threshold voltage) are deposited; the trench is filled with tungsten or cobalt and polished flat. The transistor is now complete.
30. Gate cut; gate recess and self-aligned contact cap (SiN)  
    The long gate lines running across many fins are cut into individual gates, and each gate's metal is recessed and capped with nitride so that a later contact etch landing slightly off-target hits insulator rather than gate metal (a **self-aligned contact**).

**Stage 3: MOL, contacts (Modules 11–12)**

31. Trench-silicide/contact patterning (EUV), etch to S/D epi  
    The holes and slots for the contacts are printed by EUV and etched down through ILD0 to the source/drain crystal.
32. Contact metallization: Ti/TiN liner, TiSix formation, Co/W/Ru fill, CMP  
    Titanium and titanium nitride are deposited into the holes and heated so the titanium reacts with the silicon into **titanium silicide (TiSix)**, a low-resistance junction between metal and semiconductor; the holes are filled with cobalt, tungsten or ruthenium and polished.
33. Gate contact and V0 via: patterning, etch, fill, CMP  
    A second set of holes connects to the gates, and **V0**, the zeroth via level, brings every contact up to the height of the first wiring level.

**Stage 4: BEOL, interconnect (Module 12; steps 34–35 repeat ~15–18 times with loosening pitch)**

34. **M0/M1 formation**: low-k dielectric deposition, EUV single-expose or SALELE patterning, trench etch, TaN barrier/Ru or Co liner or Cu seed, Cu electroplate (or direct Ru/Mo for M0), CMP, dielectric cap  
    The first wiring levels are the finest in the chip: low-k insulator is deposited, the line pattern is printed by a single EUV exposure or by **SALELE** (self-aligned litho-etch-litho-etch: two interleaved EUV exposures in which a spacer defined by the first aligns the second), trenches are etched, a TaN barrier and a ruthenium or cobalt liner are deposited, a copper seed is sputtered and the trench is electroplated full of copper (or, at M0, filled directly with ruthenium or molybdenum, which need no barrier), the excess is polished off, and a thin dielectric cap seals the copper. (See the worked example on EUV tool count.)
35. **Mx dual damascene** (via + trench in one fill), ~24–28 nm pitch at M1–M2, DUV immersion multi-patterning at intermediate levels, single-expose DUV at global levels  
    Each subsequent level is built by dual damascene: the via holes and the trenches above them are etched first, then both are filled with copper in one plating step and polished. M1–M2 are printed with EUV; intermediate levels use DUV immersion with two or more exposures per layer; the wide top levels use a single DUV exposure. This block repeats ~15–18 times.
36. Top thick metals and RDL (Al or thick Cu), MIM capacitors if used  
    The top levels are thick aluminum or copper lines carrying power across the die, plus a **redistribution layer (RDL)** that fans the connections out to the bump positions and, where used, **metal-insulator-metal (MIM) capacitors**, thin sandwiches of metal and dielectric that smooth the on-chip power supply.
37. Passivation (SiN/SiO2 stack), polyimide, bond-pad opening  
    The finished wiring is sealed under nitride and oxide (**passivation**) and a soft polyimide layer against moisture and scratches, and windows are etched open over the pads where the bumps will go.
38. (A16/PowerVia-class nodes) backside power delivery: bond to carrier wafer, flip, thin to ~<1 µm over devices, nano-TSV etch and fill, backside power rail metallization  
    On the newest nodes the power wires move to the *back* of the wafer: it is bonded face-down to a carrier, flipped, thinned until less than a micrometer of silicon remains under the transistors, tiny **nano-TSVs** are etched through to the transistors' contacts, and power rails are built on the backside, freeing the front-side wiring for signals. Intel's version is **PowerVia**.

**Stage 5: Wafer test and preparation (Modules 13–14, 16)**

39. Inline metrology and inspection at every critical layer (CD-SEM, OCD, overlay, bright-field/e-beam defect inspection); parametric test on scribe-line structures  
    After each critical layer, sample wafers are measured: a **CD-SEM** (critical-dimension scanning electron microscope) measures line widths; **OCD** (optical critical dimension, or scatterometry) infers feature shapes from how light scatters; overlay tools measure how well the layer landed on the one below; optical and electron-beam inspectors scan for particles and pattern errors. Test transistors in the **scribe lines** (the strips between dies that will be cut away) are probed to check the electrical parameters of the process itself.
40. **Wafer sort**: probe-card contact, ATE test, wafer map, redundancy repair/e-fuse, binning  
    Every die is contacted and tested, spare blocks are switched in for dead ones by blowing **e-fuses** (tiny on-chip fuses that permanently record a configuration), and each die is assigned a bin. (See the worked example on die per wafer and yield in the Wafer sort section.)
41. Bumping: UBM sputter, resist pattern, Cu pillar/microbump plating (~40 µm pitch for CoW; C4 ~130–150 µm for direct-on-substrate)  
    An **under-bump metallization (UBM)** stack is sputtered over the pads, a resist pattern opens holes over each pad, and copper pillars or solder microbumps are electroplated up through the holes.
42. Backgrind (with tape/protective film), stress-relief etch, backside metal if required  
    The front is protected with tape, the back is ground from 775 µm to the target thickness, a light etch removes the grinding damage (which would otherwise weaken and warp the die), and a metal film is added on the back if the package needs it for heat or electrical contact.
43. Dicing (laser groove + blade, stealth laser, or plasma), die pick and place into trays  
    The wafer is cut along the scribe lines, and each good die, identified from the wafer map, is picked up by a vacuum nozzle and placed in a tray.

**Stage 6: HBM (Module 15; runs in parallel in the memory maker's fab)**

44. DRAM wafer fab (~1T1C cell, 1β/1γ node, EUV on 1–5 layers) with via-middle TSV formation  
    The DRAM wafers are made in a memory fab: each bit is a **1T1C** cell (one transistor and one capacitor, the capacitor holding the charge that stores the bit), on the makers' **1β** or **1γ** generations (the current DRAM node names), with EUV on a few of the finest layers. The TSVs are formed **via-middle**, etched and filled after the transistors but before the wiring, so the wiring can be built over them.
45. Base (logic) die fab, on DRAM process for HBM3/3E or on foundry logic node (TSMC N12/N5, Samsung) for HBM4  
    The base die at the bottom of each stack is made separately: on the DRAM process for HBM3 and HBM3E, and for HBM4 on a real logic node at a foundry, so it can carry the faster and more complex interface.
46. TSV reveal: wafer thinning to ~30 µm on carrier, backside bump  
    Each DRAM wafer is glued to a carrier, ground to ~30 µm so the buried TSV columns poke out of the back, and given bumps on that back face too, so it can be stacked on and under.
47. Die-to-wafer stacking: 8/12/16-high by TC-NCF, MR-MUF, or hybrid bonding  
    Thinned DRAM dies are placed one on another on a base-die wafer, 8, 12 or 16 high, and bonded by one of the three methods described in the HBM section.
48. Stack test and burn-in; **known-good stacked die** (KGSD) shipment  
    Each stack is tested and burned in as a unit and ships only if it passes (the KGSD guarantee), because a bad stack found later scraps a whole GPU package.

**Stage 7: Advanced packaging (Module 17)**

49. Substrate fabrication (parallel): core drilling and plating, sequential ABF build-up (~10–20 layers, laser vias, SAP plating), solder mask, surface finish  
    In a separate factory, a glass-fibre core is drilled and plated, then ABF layers are laminated one at a time, each laser-drilled for vias and patterned by **SAP** (semi-additive process: a thin seed metal, a resist pattern, plating only where wanted, then seed removal), to build ~10–20 layers of copper wiring; a solder mask and a surface finish protect the pads.
50. Interposer fabrication: Si interposer with TSVs and 3–4 Cu layers (CoWoS-S), or RDL interposer with embedded LSI bridges (CoWoS-L)  
    The interposer is made in the fab with the same tools as a chip but with no transistors: for CoWoS-S, a silicon wafer with TSVs and three or four copper wiring layers; for CoWoS-L, a molded wafer with fine RDL wiring and **LSI bridges** (local silicon interconnect: small silicon dies carrying only wiring, embedded where the GPU and HBM meet).
51. **Chip-on-wafer**: flip-chip TC bonding of GPU die(s) and HBM stacks onto interposer wafer; underfill; molding; overmold grind  
    The GPU dies and HBM stacks are placed face-down on the interposer wafer and bonded by **thermo-compression (TC) bonding** (heat and pressure applied to each die so its bumps fuse to the interposer pads), the gaps are underfilled, the wafer is encased in mold compound (**overmold**) and the top is ground flat. (See the worked example on CoWoS wafers to packages.)
52. Interposer wafer thinning and TSV reveal; C4 bumping; CoW dicing  
    The interposer wafer is thinned from the back until its TSVs are exposed, given the larger C4 bumps that will connect to the substrate, and cut into individual modules. (See the worked example on CoWoS wafers to packages: this is where ~16 or ~29 packages per wafer is decided.)
53. **Wafer-on-substrate**: CoW module reflow onto ABF substrate; underfill  
    Each module is placed on its substrate and passed through a reflow oven that melts the C4 bumps; the gap is underfilled.
54. Lid or stiffener attach with thermal interface material; BGA ball attach; laser mark  
    A copper lid (or a **stiffening ring**, a metal frame glued around the edge of the substrate to hold it flat when the package has no lid and will be cooled through the bare die) is glued on over a **thermal interface material (TIM)**, the paste or soft metal that carries heat from die to lid; a grid of solder balls (**BGA**, ball grid array) is attached to the bottom; the part is laser-marked. This is the packaged GPU.

**Stage 8: Final test and system (Modules 18–19)**

55. Package final test (ATE); burn-in; speed/power binning  
    The package is tested in a socket by ATE, burned in, and sorted by the clock speed it reaches and the power it draws.
56. System-level test in socket with real workloads  
    The GPU runs actual software, with real memory and drivers, for hours, to catch failures that pattern tests miss.
57. SXM module assembly: GPU package onto module PCB with VRMs; reflow; test  
    The package and its voltage regulators are soldered to the SXM board in a reflow oven and the module is tested.
58. Board integration: HGX baseboard (8 GPU + NVSwitch) or GB200/GB300 compute tray (2 Grace + 4 Blackwell), cold plates  
    Modules are mounted on a baseboard or tray with the NVSwitch chips, CPUs, and the cold plates that will carry the water cooling.
59. Rack integration: 18 compute trays + 9 NVSwitch trays, NVLink copper spine, busbar, CDU/manifolds; rack burn-in and cluster test  
    Trays slide into the rack, ~5,000 copper NVLink cables connect them through the spine, a **busbar** (a thick copper bar running the height of the rack) distributes DC power to every tray, the CDU and its **manifolds** (the pipes that split the coolant to each tray) are connected, and the whole rack runs for days under load before shipping.
60. Data center deployment: power, liquid cooling, InfiniBand/Ethernet fabric and optics, bring-up, cluster validation  
    Racks are installed, connected to power, water, and the network fabric of switches and optical links, and the cluster is brought up and validated as a single machine.

## Summary

- A chip is ~100 printed layers on a 300 mm, 775 µm-thick single-crystal silicon disc; each layer is deposit → pattern → etch or implant → polish → clean and measure, and each must land on the last to within ~2 nm.
- Silicon is abundant; purity is not. Quartz at cents per kg becomes 9N–11N polysilicon at ~$20–40/kg via the Siemens route (a liquid intermediate, TCS, that can be distilled), then a single-crystal 300 mm ingot (Czochralski, ~2.5–3.5 days), then ~1,500–2,000 polished wafers at ~$100–150 each.
- The fab turns a ~$150 wafer into a ~$17,000 one over ~3 months and ~1,000–1,500 steps; ~70–80% of its ~$20–30B cost is tools, and most of a wafer's three months is spent queueing for them.
- Value and constraint both sit after the wafer: an H100 die is ~$350 of a ~$3,000 module BOM; HBM is ~40–50% of it and packaging ~20–25%; one wafer's dies become ~$1.1–1.5 million of product.
- Elapsed time is ~6–9 months from polysilicon to a shipping server; the fab is about half of it.
- Chokepoint 1: EUV. One company (ASML), ~$200–220M per tool, ~350 shipped by end-2025, a mirror-and-vacuum machine because nothing transmits 13.5 nm light, fed by a tin plasma because ~10 reflections at ~70% each leave only a few percent of the light.
- Chokepoint 2: TSMC in Taiwan makes >90% of ≤5 nm-class logic wafers because only the firm with the most customers can amortize a ~$20–30B fab: ~$5,000 of depreciation per wafer full, ~$10,000 half-empty.
- Chokepoint 3: CoWoS (~70–80k wpm end-2025, ~120–140k target end-2026) and HBM (sold out for 2025–2026 in advance) gated AI accelerator supply from 2023 through 2026, because both are new flows whose yield has to be learned.
- Japan's materials (resist, wafers, mask blanks, ABF) are a quieter fourth chokepoint, protected by qualification lock-in rather than cost.
- Seven kinds of company (EDA/IP, fabless, foundry, IDM, OSAT, equipment, materials) pass wafers and money to each other; the 1987 pure-play foundry split is why NVIDIA owns no fab.
- The 60-step master flow is the course's index; each later module expands a few of its lines to unit-process depth.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The chip is the expensive part of an AI GPU."** → The 814 mm² H100 die costs on the order of $350 out of a ~$3,000 bill of materials; HBM and packaging together are roughly two-thirds of it. The die is the hardest part, not the priciest.
- **"TSMC's constraint is how many wafers it can make."** → For AI accelerators, front-end wafer capacity has exceeded demand by roughly an order of magnitude; the binding constraints from 2023 to 2026 were CoWoS packaging capacity and HBM supply.
- **"A 2 nm node has 2 nm features."** → No dimension in an N2 transistor is 2 nm. Gate pitch is ~45–50 nm, metal pitch ~23–25 nm, nanosheet thickness a few nm. Node names have been marketing labels since the ~22 nm era; Module 11 explains what they actually track.
- **"A fab is a building full of robots that runs itself."** → A GIGAFAB employs several thousand engineers and technicians, and the ~3-month cycle time is dominated by queueing at ~1,000 tools, not by the processing itself. Cycle time and yield are management problems as much as physics problems.
- **"Silicon is scarce or strategically controlled."** → Quartz and MG-Si are cheap, abundant commodities. The strategic scarcity is in *purity* (electronic-grade polysilicon, HPQ crucible quartz) and in *capital and know-how* (EUV, leading-edge fabs, advanced packaging).
- **"China cannot make advanced chips because it lacks silicon or engineers."** → China dominates MG-Si and solar polysilicon and has a large engineering base. What it lacks is EUV lithography (export-controlled since 2019) and, increasingly, the most capable immersion DUV tools and the metrology, resist, and substrate sub-tiers that sit in Japan, the Netherlands, and the US. SMIC's 7 nm-class output on DUV multi-patterning is real but yield- and volume-limited.
- **"ASML is the only EUV maker because of patents."** → Patents are the smallest part of it. EUV is a 25-year, multi-billion-euro supply chain (Zeiss optics with no second source, Trumpf lasers, the Cymer source, thousands of sub-suppliers) plus thousands of tool-years of learning about sources, mirrors and stages that exist nowhere else. A competitor holding every patent would still be a decade and tens of billions away.
- **"HBM is just DRAM chips stacked on top of each other."** → The dies are designed differently (TSV columns with keep-out zones, a 1,024- or 2,048-bit interface instead of a few dozen pins, a logic base die at the bottom), and the stacking flow is a second manufacturing process with its own bonding chemistry, yield, and test step. That is why HBM costs several times more per bit (reported 3–5x on 2023–24 contract comparisons; later estimates vary) and why only three companies make it.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module is the map; it consumes nothing and produces the mental model every other module assumes.
