# Module 05: Inside a Leading-Edge Fab

A leading-edge logic **fab**, a factory that builds computing circuits, must coordinate repeated work on the same wafers. At the scale considered here, it runs ~100,000 wafers per month through 1,000–1,500 sequential process steps, on tools that cost more than airliners. It does that in an environment where a single 15 nm particle in the wrong place kills a **die** (one chip, cut from the wafer at the end) worth hundreds of dollars. And it does it 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, because the $20–30 billion asset loses value at roughly $10 million per day whether or not it is running: the fab is depreciated over five years, and the economics section does the arithmetic. This module is about the *factory* rather than any single process: the building, the air, the boxes the wafers ride in, the robots that move them, the software that decides what runs where, the people, the utilities, and the economics that make all of it non-negotiable.

The previous modules delivered a polished 300 mm wafer and the consumables; here that wafer becomes a manufacturing object. It is one of 25 in a **lot**, the batch of wafers that travels together in one box and shares one history. It counts as one of the fab's **wafer starts per month (wspm)**, the number of blank wafers entering the line each month (Module 00 wrote this as "wpm", the same quantity). And it is routed for ~3 months through a **re-entrant flow**, a route that returns the wafer to the same machines dozens of times rather than moving it once down a line.

By the end of this module you should be able to explain four things that surprise most first-time visitors. Why the box the wafer rides in matters more than the room around it, so that the enormous ballroom cleanroom can be dirtier than the inside of a tool. How ~6,600 deliveries an hour happen with no person carrying anything, and why that number, not cleanliness, is what forces full automation. Why the three months a wafer spends in the fab are mostly spent waiting rather than being processed, and why a formula from queueing theory sets how hard every tool can be run. And why accounting creates strong pressure to use the fab continuously when there is demand: five-year depreciation spreads a $20–30 billion purchase into a fixed daily charge, and producing more working wafers spreads that charge across more output.

Module 04 delivered the fab's inputs (wafers, gases, chemicals, resists, masks); Modules 06 through 12 walk through the process steps in the order the wafer meets them; this module is the container in between, the factory those steps live in.

## Before you start

- A **wafer** is a 300 mm diameter, 775 µm thick disc of single-crystal silicon; a **die** is one chip cut from it at the end (Module 03).
- **Node names** (N7, N5, N3, N2, A16, A14) are TSMC's technology generations, each ~1.5–2 years apart; a smaller number means newer and denser, and the names stopped corresponding to any physical dimension years ago (Module 00).
- Each chip is built as ~70–100+ patterned layers. Each layer is printed by **photolithography** (light shone through a **photomask**, also called a **reticle**, onto a light-sensitive film called **photoresist**), then etched, deposited on, or implanted (Module 00; detail in Modules 07–09).
- The first half of the flow builds the transistors in the top ~100 nm of the silicon (the **front end of line, FEOL**); the second half stacks 15–18 levels of copper wiring above them (the **back end of line, BEOL**) (Module 00; detail in Modules 11–12).
- **Yield** is the fraction of dies on a wafer that work; a **killer defect** is a particle or flaw large enough to break a feature; **D0** counts fatal defects per cm² (Module 00; the math is in Module 13).
- Wafers arrive from the wafer maker 25 to a shipping box (a **FOSB, front-opening shipping box**) and are transferred into the fab's own **FOUPs** (front-opening unified pods, the sealed 25-wafer boxes this module describes); the fab's nitrogen comes from an on-site **air separation unit** that liquefies and distils air (Modules 03–04).
- School physics you will use: pressure (1 atm ≈ 101,000 Pa ≈ 760 Torr), vacuum as "very low pressure", thermal expansion, resonance (an object vibrates most at its natural frequency), ions as charged atoms, and resistivity as the inverse of conductivity.
- Two ideas from outside engineering: **depreciation** (spreading a purchase cost over its useful years as a fixed annual charge, whether or not the thing is used) and **queueing** (why a checkout that is busy 95% of the time has a long line even though it is idle 5% of the time).

## The Building: Why a Fab Is a Vertical Sandwich

The problem a fab building solves is not "keep the dust out". It is that every one of ~1,000 process tools needs clean air delivered from directly above it and drained from directly below it, needs a floor whose residual motion while trucks pass outside, a fraction of a micrometre, is small and smooth enough for the scanner's own isolators to cancel down to the ~1 nm it needs, and needs dozens of pipes, cables, and exhaust ducts arriving from underneath: vacuum lines, ten or more gas lines, chilled water, electrical power, chemical feeds, and toxic exhaust. Put all of that in one flat shed and the pipes block the airflow, the pumps shake the floor, and the exhaust ducts cross the clean air. The answer everyone converged on is to stack the functions vertically: air on top, wafers in the middle, everything dirty and noisy underneath.

A modern 300 mm fab is therefore a purpose-built, vertically layered structure in which each level exists to serve the cleanroom level above or below it. From top to bottom, a typical leading-edge fab (TSMC's GigaFab phases, Intel's Ocotillo fabs, and Samsung's Pyeongtaek lines all follow the same logic) has:

1. **Fan deck / air-handling level.** The top floor houses the **make-up air units (MAUs)**, which take in outside air, filter it, dehumidify or humidify it, cool it, and chemically scrub it. **Make-up air** is the fresh outside air that replaces what the exhaust stacks remove; it is only a small fraction of the air moving through the cleanroom, because most of the air recirculates. The MAUs feed the **supply plenum**. A **plenum** is a large sealed air space that acts as a manifold: one duct fills it, and it feeds hundreds of filters at equal pressure (a supply plenum), or it collects return air from a whole floor (a return plenum). Recirculation fans (or, in most modern fabs, the ceiling-mounted fan-filter units themselves) push the air down into the cleanroom.
2. **Interstitial / ceiling plenum.** Between the fan deck and the cleanroom ceiling is a service space ("interstitial" simply means "in between") where ducting, sprinkler mains, cable trays, and the supports for the **overhead hoist transport (OHT)** rails run (the OHT is the ceiling railway that carries wafer boxes; it has its own section below). The cleanroom ceiling itself is a grid of **fan-filter units (FFUs)**, each a fan and a filter in one box (explained in the next section), and blank panels hung from this structure.
3. **Cleanroom level.** The production floor: the **ballroom** (one large open cleanroom in which the tools stand in rows, as opposed to the older **bay-and-chase** layout, in which narrow tool bays were separated by dirtier service corridors called chases) where the process tools stand on a **raised perforated floor** (floor tiles with holes, on legs, so air can fall through). A single GigaFab phase has on the order of 40,000–50,000 m² of cleanroom, ~6–7 football pitches under one roof; a full multi-phase GigaFab is well over 100,000 m².
4. **Waffle slab.** The cleanroom floor is not a normal concrete slab; it is a **waffle slab**: a 1–1.5 m thick grid of concrete ribs with square openings (seen from below it looks like a waffle), typically supported on a dense column grid and isolated structurally from the outer building shell. It is as thick as a person is tall. The openings let return air fall through into the sub-fab and give a place to pass pipes, cables, and exhaust ducts to each tool. Its thickness and stiffness are what give the floor its vibration performance.
5. **Sub-fab.** Directly beneath the cleanroom sits the "dirty half" of every tool, at ISO 7–8 (i.e. 100–1,000× dirtier than the production floor but still far cleaner than an office; the classes are explained in the next section): vacuum pumps, **radio-frequency (RF)** generators and **match networks** (plasma tools sustain a glowing ionized gas with RF power; the match network continuously tunes the generator to the plasma's changing electrical load so the power goes in rather than reflecting back), chillers, gas cabinets and **valve manifold boxes (VMBs)** (a VMB is a cabinet of valves that splits one incoming gas line to several tools, with leak detection on every branch), chemical delivery cabinets, **point-of-use abatement** (units that burn or scrub toxic and greenhouse exhaust gases right at the tool, before they reach any shared duct), and power distribution. Every process chamber upstairs has a rack of equipment downstairs connected through the waffle slab.
6. **Utility level(s) / basement.** Bulk gas distribution, the **ultrapure water (UPW)** plant (water purified until almost nothing but H₂O is left; the utilities section explains how), waste treatment, exhaust scrubbers, switchgear and transformers, chiller plants, and the **air separation unit (ASU)** for on-site nitrogen (usually built and operated by Linde, Air Liquide, or Air Products on the fab site).

### Vibration: why the slab matters

The problem is easy to state and hard to solve. An **ArF immersion scanner** (the 193 nm **deep-ultraviolet (DUV)** projection tool of Module 07; ArF is the argon fluoride laser that makes the light, and "immersion" means a film of water fills the gap between the lens and the wafer, which lets the lens print finer features than it could through air) positions a wafer stage to within ~1 nm, and an **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** scanner (Module 08) must hold **overlay**, the accuracy with which one layer's pattern lands on the previous layer, in the ~1–2 nm range. Meanwhile trucks pass on the road, hundreds of pumps run in the sub-fab, and thousands of vehicles roll on the ceiling rails. Every one of those pushes on the building, and the building moves.

> **Intuition:** Vibration is a tiny back-and-forth motion. For a sine-wave vibration the displacement is the velocity divided by 2π times the frequency, x = v / (2πf), where x is the peak displacement, v the peak velocity, and f the frequency. Because of that division, one velocity limit means a displacement allowance that shrinks as frequency rises. Take the VC-D curve below, 6.25 µm/s: at 10 Hz, x = 6.25 / (2π × 10) ≈ 0.1 µm, i.e. 100 nm of floor motion, but at 80 Hz only ~0.012 µm. The industry specifies floors in velocity rather than displacement because Colin Gordon's measurements in the early 1990s, which produced the VC curves, showed that precision tools tolerate roughly the same velocity at any frequency across the ~8–80 Hz band, so one number covers the band. Even so, 100 nm at 10 Hz is 100× the ~1 nm the scanner must hold, so a floor built to the tightest common specification is nowhere near good enough on its own; the scanner has to add its own isolation on top. The floor's job is to give the scanner's isolators a problem they can solve.

One idea from school physics matters here: a floor rings like a drum at its **natural frequency**, and a push at that frequency (resonance) produces a much larger motion than the same push at any other frequency. Fab floors are therefore designed so that their natural frequencies sit above the ~10–30 Hz band where the sources (pumps, footfalls, and vehicles) push hardest and where scanner stages are also most sensitive.

Floor vibration is expressed as velocity in one-third octave bands (frequency bins each a factor of 2^(1/3) ≈ 1.26 wide, so three bins span a doubling of frequency), and the industry uses the **vibration criterion (VC) curves**, a ladder in which each step halves the allowed velocity:

| Curve | Velocity limit | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| VC-A | 50 µm/s | General microelectronics areas, older-node fabs |
| VC-B | 25 µm/s | Non-critical process areas |
| VC-C | 12.5 µm/s | Most process tools at leading nodes |
| VC-D | 6.25 µm/s | Lithography and electron-beam (e-beam) metrology areas |
| VC-E | 3.1 µm/s | The most sensitive scanner and e-beam areas |

Lithography and **e-beam (electron-beam) metrology** areas are designed to VC-D or VC-E, which corresponds to displacements of a fraction of a micrometre at low frequencies. An e-beam metrology tool measures or inspects a wafer by scanning a focused beam of electrons across it; its spot is a few nanometres wide, so any floor motion smears the image, which makes it even more vibration-sensitive than a scanner. The waffle slab achieves this in three ways: mass (a thick slab has low response to a given force, the way a heavy table barely moves when you lean on it), stiffness (the closely spaced columns push the slab's natural frequencies above that same ~10–30 Hz band where the sources push hardest and scanner stages are most sensitive), and isolation (the slab is separated from the building shell and from the sub-fab floor so that footfalls, OHT vehicles, and pumps transmit little energy into it). Scanners additionally sit on their own active pneumatic isolation frames: air springs that carry the machine, with sensors and actuators that cancel residual motion. Fabs are sited away from railways and highways for the same reason, and heavy rotating equipment in the sub-fab sits on its own isolated pads.

Fabs are designed and built by a small group of specialist engineering contractors: Exyte (formerly M+W, Germany), Jacobs (USA), CTCI and United Integrated Services (both Taiwan) design the cleanroom and utilities and manage construction. Roughly two years pass from ground-breaking to a weather-tight shell with a working cleanroom, followed by months of **hook-up** (connecting each tool's pipes, power, exhaust, and data; the timing varies with site and scale, so treat "~2 years" as an order of magnitude). Only then can tools move in, and they move in for a year or more as the phase ramps.

> **What can go wrong:** A waffle slab that settles unevenly over its first years tilts the rails and floors that scanners were levelled to, so fabs survey their slabs continuously and design foundations for the local soil. A roof leak during Taiwan's monsoon season drips into the ceiling plenum and can contaminate hundreds of filters at once. A neighbouring construction site (a new phase going up next door, or pile driving for a highway) injects low-frequency vibration that the isolation was never designed for, so scanner areas are monitored with accelerometers and heavy civil works nearby are scheduled around the most sensitive tools. And during commissioning, engineers sometimes find that an OHT rail section resonates at a vehicle-passing frequency; the fix is a stiffening bracket or a speed limit on that stretch, found only by measuring.

## Cleanroom Classes and Airflow

Ordinary city air contains tens of millions of particles per cubic metre. A wafer surface with tens of billions of transistors on it can tolerate almost none of them, and a person standing still emits a hundred thousand particles a minute. The cleanroom is the machinery that keeps those two facts apart: it specifies how clean the air must be, filters it to that level, and moves it so that anything released in the room is carried away from the wafers rather than toward them.

### ISO 14644 classes

Cleanroom cleanliness is specified by **ISO 14644-1**, the international standard that defines the maximum permitted number of airborne particles per cubic metre at a given size. The class number N is the exponent: an ISO class N room may contain at most 10^N particles ≥ 0.1 µm per m³, with the limit at other sizes following C = 10^N × (0.1 / D)^2.08, where C is the maximum count per m³ and D is the particle diameter in micrometres. The formula says that the allowed count falls steeply as the size threshold rises, because large particles are rarer than small ones. For ISO 5 at 0.5 µm: 10⁵ × (0.1/0.5)^2.08 = 100,000 × 0.0352 ≈ 3,520 particles per m³, the number in the table.

| ISO class | ≥ 0.1 µm per m³ | ≥ 0.5 µm per m³ | Old FED-STD-209E name | Where in the fab |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ISO 1 | 10 | (not specified) | (none) | Wafer-handling mini-environments (the cleanest air in the fab; the EUV exposure chamber itself is a vacuum, cleaner than any air class) |
| ISO 2 | 100 | 4 | (none) | EFEM (the tool's front-end enclosure, next section) / mini-environments |
| ISO 3 | 1,000 | 35 | Class 1 | Mini-environments, litho tracks |
| ISO 4 | 10,000 | 352 | Class 10 | Litho bay ballroom (some fabs) |
| ISO 5 | 100,000 | 3,520 | Class 100 | Ballroom in a FOUP-based fab |
| ISO 6 | 1,000,000 | 35,200 | Class 1,000 | Ballroom / less critical areas |
| ISO 7 | (not specified) | 352,000 | Class 10,000 | Sub-fab, gowning rooms |
| ISO 8 | (not specified) | 3,520,000 | Class 100,000 | Sub-fab, utility areas |
| ISO 9 | (not specified) | 35,200,000 | (room air) | Ordinary office / outdoor urban air |

**FED-STD-209E** was the pre-2001 US standard that counted particles ≥ 0.5 µm per cubic foot, so "Class 100" meant at most 100 such particles per ft³ (a cubic foot is 0.0283 m³, so Class 100 ≈ 3,530 per m³ ≈ ISO 5); engineers still say "Class 1" and "Class 100" out of habit. Note the scale: ordinary city air is about ISO 9, so an ISO 5 ballroom is 10,000 times cleaner than outdoors, and an ISO 1 mini-environment is another 10,000 times cleaner than that. A 1990s Class 1 fab was clean everywhere; the modern strategy is to make the small volumes that actually touch bare wafers (the mini-environments, ISO 1–3) extremely clean and let the big ballroom relax to ISO 5–6 because the wafers are never exposed to it. That relaxation is only possible because of the FOUP, covered in the next section.

### Fan-filter units and unidirectional downflow

The ballroom ceiling is a grid of **FFUs**, each typically a 1.2 m × 0.6 m (4 ft × 2 ft) or 1.2 m × 1.2 m module containing a low-noise **electronically commutated (EC)** fan (a brushless motor that sheds no brush dust and can be speed-controlled) and a **ULPA (ultra-low penetration air) filter**. A **HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air)** filter, the kind in a good vacuum cleaner, is rated 99.97% efficient at 0.3 µm; a ULPA filter is rated ≥ 99.9995% at its **most penetrating particle size (MPPS)** of ~0.1–0.2 µm, so essentially nothing that enters the fan passes into the room.

Why does a filter have a worst size? A fibre filter is not a sieve; its gaps are far larger than the particles it stops. Big particles have too much momentum to follow the air around a fibre and slam into it (**impaction**), or brush against it while passing (**interception**). Very small particles are knocked about so violently by air molecules (**Brownian diffusion**) that they wander into a fibre within a few fibre spacings. Particles of ~0.1–0.2 µm are too small for the first two mechanisms and too large for the third, so that is the size a filter catches least well, and it is the size at which the rating is quoted.

In the cleanest areas, filter ceiling coverage is close to 100%; in lower-class areas 30–50% coverage with blank panels in between is sufficient. A GigaFab has tens of thousands of FFUs, each drawing on the order of 100–300 W: roughly 30,000 × 200 W ≈ 6 MW for the fans alone, only ~1% of a 500 MW site. The fans are the visible part of cleanroom air handling; the chillers, the make-up-air conditioning, and the dehumidification that feed them are what bring cleanroom HVAC to the 20–30% of site electricity given in the utilities section.

Air leaves the filters as a **unidirectional downflow** at ~0.3–0.5 m/s (the classical design number is 0.45 m/s). Unidirectional means the air moves in parallel sheets, all in the same direction at the same speed, so a particle released at a point is carried straight down instead of wandering sideways; the industry used to say **laminar**, and still often does, but true laminar flow is not achieved around tools and people, so the standards now say unidirectional. The physics is simple: particles generated at any point in the room (a tool, a person, a robot) are swept straight down past the wafer level and out through the **perforated raised floor** (steel or aluminium tiles with ~15–25% open area, sometimes with dampers) before they can diffuse sideways to a neighbouring tool. Below the raised floor the air drops through the waffle slab openings into the sub-fab, which acts as the return plenum, and is pulled back up return shafts to the fan deck. With a ~3.5–4.5 m ceiling and 0.45 m/s downflow, the air crosses the room in about 4 m / 0.45 m/s ≈ 9 s, so the entire room volume is replaced every ~8–10 s, i.e. 3,600 / 9 ≈ 400 **air changes per hour** (roughly 350–450 depending on ceiling height), versus ~10 in an office. Only 10–20% of the circulating air is fresh make-up air; the rest recirculates, because conditioning outside air to cleanroom specifications is expensive.

### Temperature, humidity, pressure

Litho areas are held to ±0.1 °C (some tool enclosures to ±0.01 °C) around a set point near 22–23 °C, because a 1 °C change in a 300 mm silicon wafer expands it by ~0.8 µm edge to edge (silicon's coefficient of thermal expansion is 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin, so 300 mm × 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 0.78 µm), and overlay budgets at N3/N2 are ~2 nm. That 0.8 µm is 400× the overlay budget, so 1 °C is not a small error; it is a scrapped layer. To keep expansion under 2 nm the scanner's own wafer stage holds the wafer to a few thousandths of a degree, which is why the litho bay at ±0.1 °C is only the outer shell of a nested temperature control: bay, then tool enclosure, then the stage and the water-cooled **chuck** (the plate that holds the wafer), each an order of magnitude tighter than the one outside it. The rest of the fab typically runs ±0.5–1 °C. Relative humidity is held at ~40–45% ± a few percent: too dry and electrostatic discharge and particle attraction rise; too wet and chemically amplified resists (explained under AMC below) change sensitivity, water adsorbs on wafer surfaces, and corrosion accelerates. The cleanroom runs at a slight positive pressure (~10–15 Pa over the adjacent gowning and service areas, with a cascade of decreasing pressure outward) so that any leak is outward. Vibration, temperature, and humidity are all measured continuously and trended in the facility monitoring system.

### Airborne molecular contamination (AMC)

Particles are only half of the contamination problem. **Airborne molecular contamination (AMC)** is gas-phase chemical contamination at **ppb** and **ppt** levels (parts per billion and parts per trillion by volume; 1 ppb is about one drop in a 50,000-litre swimming pool). Molecules are far too small to filter with fibres, they pass through any gap, and they do their damage chemically rather than by sitting on the surface.

> **Intuition:** The most AMC-sensitive thing in the fab is the photoresist, the light-sensitive film that records each layer's pattern. In a **chemically amplified resist** one absorbed photon releases one acid molecule, and that acid then triggers hundreds of chemical reactions during a bake, which is how the resist can be sensitive enough to print with very little light. The catch is that a whiff of base (ammonia is the common one) at the surface neutralizes the acid before it has done its work, so the top of each printed line stays unexposed and undeveloped: a **T-shaped profile ("T-topping")** and a wider **critical dimension (CD)**, the width of the smallest printed feature. A part-per-billion of ammonia in the litho bay is therefore a CD error, which is why the bay is a chemically filtered island inside the cleanroom.

**SEMI F21** classifies AMC (SEMI is the industry standards body that publishes the specifications almost every fab follows): **MA** (acids: HF hydrofluoric acid, HCl hydrochloric acid, SO₂ sulfur dioxide, NOx nitrogen oxides), **MB** (bases: ammonia, amines), **MC** (condensables: organics with boiling points above ~150 °C such as siloxanes and phthalate plasticizers, which settle on surfaces), and **MD** (dopants: boron, phosphorus; a **dopant** is an impurity deliberately added to silicon to set its conductivity; boron makes silicon **p-type**, conducting by positively charged **holes**, i.e. missing electrons, and phosphorus makes it **n-type**, conducting by extra electrons). Each does specific damage:

- Ammonia and amines at ~1 ppb neutralize the photoacid at the surface of chemically amplified DUV/EUV resists, producing T-topping and CD shifts; litho bays use chemical filters (impregnated activated carbon, ion-exchange media) and keep ammonia-based cleaning chemistry away.
- Boron is a p-type dopant; borosilicate glass fibres in older HEPA media outgas boron that dopes silicon surfaces during high-temperature steps, so modern filters use low-boron or **PTFE** (polytetrafluoroethylene, the non-stick polymer) media.
- Siloxanes (silicone sealants, cosmetics, lubricants) crack on hot surfaces and under DUV/EUV light into SiO₂ (silicon dioxide) haze on optics and reticles; fabs ban silicone-containing materials, and EUV scanners run a hydrogen background to clean carbon and tin off the mirrors (EUV light is made by vaporizing tin droplets with a laser, Module 08, and some of that tin reaches the mirrors).
- Sulfates and ammonium salts grow **haze** crystals (a fine crystalline film that scatters light) on reticle (photomask) surfaces that print as defects, which is what drove nitrogen-purged reticle pods and tighter AMC control after the 2000s haze crisis.

AMC is monitored with **ion-chromatography samplers** (air is bubbled through water for hours and the dissolved ions are separated and counted), **cavity ring-down spectrometers** (a laser bounces thousands of times between mirrors and the rate at which its light dies away reveals ppt levels of a specific gas), and **witness wafers** (bare wafers left exposed in a bay and then analysed for what landed on them), and controlled by chemical filtration in the make-up air, in critical-bay FFUs, and inside tools and FOUPs.

Filters and FFUs come from a few specialist makers: Camfil (Sweden), AAF (USA), and Nippon Muki (Japan) supply the ULPA and HEPA elements, the FFUs, and the chemical filter media (activated carbon impregnated with acids or bases to capture the opposite species, and ion-exchange fibres). Exyte and Jacobs design the airflow, pressure cascades, and AMC zoning as part of the cleanroom.

> **What can go wrong:** Every filter is scan-tested on installation and annually: an aerosol is released upstream and a probe is swept across the downstream face to find pinhole leaks, and a leaking element is patched or replaced. An FFU whose fan stops is caught by its own tachometer alarm, because a dead FFU becomes a hole in the ceiling through which unfiltered plenum air can fall. AMC excursions are sneakier: a construction sealant outgassing amines during a tool install has shown up not on any air monitor but as a resist CD shift on the litho bay's **statistical process control (SPC)** charts (explained in the software section), and the source was found by walking the bay with a sampler. And a propped-open door can reverse the pressure cascade so that gowning-room air flows into the ballroom; the pressure monitors alarm within seconds.

> **Why it matters downstream:** AMC control in the litho bay is the reason Module 08's EUV resist doses (the amount of light energy per unit area each exposure must deliver) and Module 07's CD budgets can be met at all. Those modules take for granted that the resist sees ppt-level base concentrations between coat, exposure, and bake; this section is where that assumption is bought.

## The FOUP and the Mini-Environment

A wafer spends ~3 months in the fab and is handled at ~1,300 steps. Between steps it sits, sometimes for hours, and if it sat in open air even ISO 5 air would drop particles on it at a rate the contamination section computes later, while ammonia and water would adsorb onto it continuously. The room cannot be made clean enough to leave wafers out, and even if it could, a person walking past would undo it. The 300 mm industry's answer was to stop trying to protect the wafer with the room and to protect it with a box instead.

> **Intuition:** The cleanroom is inside the box. The FOUP is a spacesuit for wafers: a sealed, nitrogen-flushed container that carries its own tiny clean environment through a room that would otherwise be too dirty. The tool's front end is the airlock: it docks with the box, opens it only into an even cleaner enclosed volume, and closes it again before the box leaves. The ballroom outside is there to protect the tools, the people, and the cables, not the wafers.

Between tools, a 300 mm wafer normally travels in a **front-opening unified pod (FOUP)**, which docks to a controlled tool environment for transfer. A common version holds **25 wafers** at 10 mm pitch, though other capacities and pitches exist. Materials depend on the carrier design: Entegris A300 documentation lists carbon-filled polycarbonate or barrier materials for wafer contacts. **PEEK** and **PEI** (polyether ether ketone and polyetherimide) are engineering plastics used in semiconductor handling, but they are not universal FOUP contact materials. Plastics are chosen because they are light (an OHT vehicle must lift the box), can be moulded to 0.1 mm precision, do not shed particles when wafers slide against them, and can be made static-dissipative so the box does not build up charge. Twenty-five was inherited from the 200 mm-era cassette and was kept because furnace batch sizes and lot economics were already built around it, so a full 25-wafer lot is a useful planning unit. Split lots, monitor wafers, sampling and partially filled carriers mean that actual counts need not be multiples of 25. Major FOUP suppliers are Entegris (the leader, with an estimated ~half or more of the market), Shin-Etsu Polymer, Miraial, and 3S Korea; a FOUP costs a few thousand dollars, and a GigaFab has tens of thousands of them in circulation. The standard is defined by SEMI E47.1 (the FOUP), E15.1 (the load port), and E1.9 (the wafer cassette), which support standardized interfaces; the carrier and load port still need matching specifications and qualification.

The mechanism at the tool is the interesting part:

1. An OHT vehicle lowers the FOUP onto a **load port** on the front of the tool. Three **kinematic coupling** pins on the load port mate with three V-grooves on the FOUP bottom. A rigid body can move in exactly six ways (three shifts and three rotations), and three balls seated in three V-grooves take away exactly those six freedoms and no more, so the box lands in the same place to within a fraction of a millimetre every time with no adjustment and no over-constraint. An **RFID** tag (radio-frequency identification, a small chip read wirelessly) on the FOUP identifies the carrier; the tool's host interface (SECS/GEM, explained in the software section) matches it to the lot the **MES** expects (the fab's central tracking computer, also covered later).
2. The load port clamps the FOUP, advances it against a sealed opening in the tool's front wall, and the **FOUP opener** unlatches the FOUP door from inside, pulling door and latch mechanism into the tool. Only now are the wafers exposed, but only to the interior of the **equipment front-end module (EFEM)**.
3. The EFEM is a small cabinet with its own FFU on top blowing ULPA-filtered air downward across the wafers and out the bottom: the **mini-environment**, kept at ISO 1–3 and at slight positive pressure relative to the ballroom, so ballroom air cannot enter even through the door seam. Inside are a wafer-handling robot (with vacuum or edge-grip **end effectors**, the "hands" on the robot arm), a **pre-aligner** that spins the wafer to find the **notch** (a small V-shaped cut on the wafer edge that marks the crystal direction) and centre it, and often a camera that reads the laser-scribed wafer ID (the SEMI M12/M13 alphanumeric mark on the front near the edge, or the T7 matrix code on the back).
4. The robot moves wafers one at a time from the FOUP into the process module: into a **load lock** for a vacuum tool (a small chamber that is pumped from air down to vacuum with the wafer inside, so the process chamber itself never sees air), or onto a chuck for a track or wet tool. When processing is done the wafers return to the same slots, the door is replaced, and the FOUP is released to the next OHT vehicle.

Two refinements matter at the leading edge. **Nitrogen purge**: load ports and stockers have nozzles that mate with check valves in the FOUP base and flow dry N₂ through it, driving internal humidity to a few percent and displacing oxygen. This slows regrowth of **native oxide** on cleaned silicon (the ~1 nm skin of SiO₂ that bare silicon grows by itself in air; the process-flow section gives the rate), slows copper and cobalt oxidation between BEOL steps (the wiring half of the flow, the process-flow section), and stops the FOUP's polymer walls from feeding adsorbed HF or ammonia back onto the wafers. **FOUP hygiene**: the polymer absorbs process chemicals, so FOUPs are washed in dedicated washers, tracked as assets with maintenance schedules, and often dedicated to a process segment (a "BEOL FOUP" never carries FEOL wafers, to keep copper away from the transistor-building half of the flow). Photomasks travel the same way: each reticle rides in its own sealed, purged pod (the SEMI E152 EUV dual pod, an inner and an outer box, for EUV reticles; Entegris and Gudeng of Taiwan make them).

> **Worked example: how many FOUPs a GigaFab owns.** The process-flow section will show that a 100,000 wspm fab with a 90-day cycle time holds ~296,000 wafers in process. At 25 per box that is 296,000 / 25 ≈ 11,800 loaded FOUPs at any instant. Add the empties waiting at wafer start and at wafer out, the boxes in the washers, the boxes dedicated to monitor and dummy wafers, and spares to cover the wash cycle, and the fleet is plausibly ~15,000–20,000. At a few thousand dollars each, that is tens of millions of dollars of plastic boxes, each one tracked by RFID as a maintained asset.

> **What can go wrong:** A load port whose purge line has failed leaves a lot sitting in humid air; if the lot is inside a queue-time window (the process-flow section) the wafers may have to be scrapped or reworked. A wafer that has been **cross-slotted** (sitting at an angle across two slots) or two wafers in one slot are caught by the load port's **slot-mapping** sensor, a light beam swept past the open door before the robot reaches in; without it the robot would crash into the wafer. An RFID read that does not match the lot the MES expects makes the tool refuse the carrier, and the lot is held until a person checks. And a copper-contaminated BEOL FOUP that reaches an FEOL tool by mistake can contaminate a furnace and every lot that follows through it, which is why carrier dedication is enforced in software as well as by labels.

The consequence for the building is profound: because wafers live in ISO 1–3 mini-environments, the ballroom can be ISO 5–6 and the humans and robots in it are far less dangerous. This is the 300 mm answer to the 200 mm-era **standard mechanical interface (SMIF)** pod, a smaller sealed box that opened from the bottom into a tool's enclosure, and it is what makes full automation and the ballroom layout possible.

## AMHS: How 100,000 Wafers a Month Actually Move

In a leading-edge fab no human carries wafers. The **automated material handling system (AMHS)** moves every FOUP for every step. The dominant suppliers are Daifuku (Japan, the leader with the majority of leading-edge fabs) and Murata Machinery (Muratec, Japan); both build the vehicles, rails, and stockers as a system. SEMES (Samsung's in-house equipment subsidiary) supplies Samsung's fabs, and Mirle (Taiwan) supplies parts of the Taiwanese market.

### Components

- **OHT vehicles** run on aluminium rails hung from the ceiling structure. They take **contactless inductive power** from the rail: the rail carries a high-frequency current, and a pickup coil on the vehicle draws power from its magnetic field with no sliding contacts, because contacts would wear and shed particles over the tools. They talk over wireless links to the **material control system (MCS)**, the traffic-control computer. Each carries one FOUP under its body and lowers it onto a load port with a **belt hoist**: a set of belts unwinds to lower the gripper several metres to the load port, then rewinds to lift the box back up to rail height. Vehicles travel at up to ~5 m/s on straights and slow for turns and merges; a GigaFab has on the order of 1,000–3,000+ of them on tens of kilometres of track laid out as a mesh of **intrabay** loops (one per row of tools) joined by **interbay** trunks, with shortcuts and bypasses to route around blockages.
- **Stockers** are automated towers whose crane stores and retrieves a few thousand FOUPs on shelves. A FOUP in a stocker is **work in process (WIP)**, wafers that have been started but not finished, waiting for a tool, so modern fabs push storage out to the tools: **side-track buffers (STBs)** and **under-track storage (UTS)** shelves hang from the rail beside a tool so the next lot is in reach when the current one finishes, and **overhead buffers (OHBs)** hold FOUPs on the ceiling. Stockers and buffers provide nitrogen purge for lots under humidity-sensitive queue-time limits.
- **Lifters and conveyors** connect floors and phases; TSMC's phases are linked by bridges so a lot moves between phases without leaving the AMHS.
- **Reticle transport** runs on the same rails. Reticles live in a **reticle stocker** (a purged automated library beside the litho bay), ride in their sealed pods (the EUV dual pod of the previous section) on OHT vehicles to the scanner's reticle load port, and are tracked by the MES with the same rigour as lots, because a scanner waiting for a reticle is idle exactly as if it were waiting for a wafer. The handshake between a vehicle and any load port, wafer or reticle, is standardized as **SEMI E84** (a parallel optical signalling protocol in which port and vehicle confirm that the port is ready, the load is placed, and the gripper has let go), which is why a Daifuku vehicle can serve a TEL track and an ASML scanner alike.

### Scale: why automation is forced

The number of moves is what forces automation rather than the cleanliness.

> **Worked example: deliveries per hour and vehicles needed.** Consider a 100,000 wspm fab at ~1,200 steps: 4,000 lots per month, each visiting 1,200 tools, is ~4.8 million tool visits per month, or ~6,600 lot deliveries per hour, every hour of every day. Each delivery is a pickup, a transport averaging a few hundred metres, a drop, and a repositioning run to the next job. Call the whole cycle ~5 minutes. Then 6,600 deliveries per hour × 5 minutes / 60 minutes ≈ 550 vehicles are busy at any instant. A fleet cannot run its vehicles flat out, for the same queueing reason that applies to tools (next section): at a healthy ~50–60% vehicle utilization the fleet is ~1,000 vehicles, the low end of the 1,000–3,000 range quoted for GigaFabs. The rest of a big fab's fleet is surge margin, empty vehicles repositioning toward where lots will finish next, and coverage for the rail sections closed for maintenance. No plausible human workforce could make 6,600 correct, timed, particle-free deliveries an hour.

Average delivery time is typically kept to a few minutes, and the MCS's job is to have a vehicle there before the tool finishes so that the tool never waits for material (a delivery delay on a $200 M EUV scanner costs more than the vehicle fleet). Dispatching follows rules that are simple to state and hard to run at this scale: the nearest free vehicle takes a new job; empty vehicles are pre-positioned near tools whose current lot is about to finish, using the tool's reported remaining time; **hot lots** (expedited lots, next section) get priority and sometimes a reserved vehicle; and the traffic controller spaces vehicles at merges and reroutes around congestion. A **deadlock** is two vehicles each waiting for the space the other occupies, so that neither can move; on a mesh with a thousand vehicles it can happen in ways no one designed, and the MCS must detect and break it. Vehicle dispatching, traffic control, and deadlock avoidance on this scale are a serious real-time-systems problem, and the AMHS software is typically as important as the hardware in a fab's selection.

> **What can go wrong:** A stalled vehicle blocks its loop until it is towed or bypassed, which is why loops have shortcuts and why the MCS reroutes within seconds. A deadlock that the software misses freezes a bay and a person has to walk in and move a vehicle by hand. A dropped FOUP is the nightmare case: 25 wafers with up to ~$10,000 of accumulated processing each, on the order of a quarter of a million dollars, plus the particle cloud and the shutdown of the bay while the mess is cleared; gripper interlocks, redundant belt sensing, and a vehicle speed profile that slows before every hoist all exist to keep this rare. Rail sections need preventive maintenance windows, planned so that no bay loses its only route. And in an earthquake the vehicles are commanded to stop and hold their loads on the rail; after the April 2024 Hualien earthquake TSMC reported that most tools were recovered within a day and full recovery took several days, with the AMHS inspection and restart a large part of the work (approximate, from press reports).

The result is a **"lights-out"** fab in the sense that the production floor needs no operators for normal wafer movement; people in the ballroom are engineers and technicians doing maintenance, qualification, or troubleshooting. Photographs of TSMC's Fab 18 show long aisles of tools with OHT vehicles flowing overhead and few if any people.

## The Process Flow: 1,000–1,500 Steps, Revisited Dozens of Times

This is the module's central idea, and everything else in the fab exists to serve it: a wafer goes through on the order of 1,300 steps on ~20 kinds of tool, returning to each kind dozens of times, and the way that flow behaves as a queueing system determines the fab's cycle time, its inventory, and how hard its most expensive tools can be run.

### FEOL, MOL, BEOL

> **Intuition:** A transistor is a switch built in the top ~100 nm of the silicon. The first part of the flow makes the switches (FEOL). The middle part wires each switch's three terminals up to the first layer of metal (MOL). The last part builds a 15–18-storey city grid of copper wires above them, so that billions of switches can be connected into a circuit (BEOL). Everything is built from the bottom up, one patterned layer at a time, and each layer is printed, etched, filled, and flattened before the next. Modules 06–12 do each step in detail; here the point is only that the three eras have different rules.

The flow divides into three eras, each with its own contamination rules and tool sets:

- **Front end of line (FEOL)**: everything that builds the transistors: **shallow trench isolation** (trenches etched between transistors and filled with oxide so they do not short to each other), **well implants** (ions fired into the silicon to set the background doping of each region), **fin or nanosheet formation** (etching or growing the thin silicon channels the current flows through, Module 11), the **gate stack** (the **high-k dielectric**, an insulator with a high dielectric constant that lets a thicker film act like a thinner one, plus **work-function metals**, the gate metals chosen to set the switching voltage), **source/drain epitaxy** (growing doped crystalline silicon or silicon-germanium on the transistor's two terminals), and **activation anneals** (heating to move implanted atoms into crystal sites where they become electrically active). Temperatures reach 1,000 °C+ in anneals and ~600–700 °C in epi (the fab's short form for epitaxy). No copper or other fast-diffusing metals are permitted anywhere near FEOL tools. Copper atoms move through silicon in minutes at process temperatures, and a copper atom sitting in the crystal creates an allowed electron energy in the middle of silicon's **band gap** (the range of energies electrons cannot normally occupy, which is what makes silicon a semiconductor rather than a conductor). That state is a stepping stone for electrons across a **junction** (the boundary between p-type and n-type silicon that a transistor relies on to block current when it is off), so the transistor leaks. Anything that has touched copper therefore never touches FEOL.
- **Middle of line (MOL)**: the contacts and local interconnect that connect transistors to the wiring: **contact etch** (holes down to each terminal), **silicide** or contact metal (a metal-silicon compound, or titanium/titanium nitride, cobalt, and now increasingly ruthenium or molybdenum, that makes a low-resistance connection to the silicon), gate contacts, and the first tight-pitch metal levels (**M0/M1**, the lowest and narrowest wires). MOL is now a major yield and resistance limiter because contact dimensions are ~15–20 nm.
- **Back end of line (BEOL)**: 15–18 levels of copper (and, at the bottom, cobalt or ruthenium) wiring built by **dual damascene** (etch trenches and holes into an insulator, fill both with metal at once, polish the excess off; Module 12), with **low-k dielectrics** (insulators of low dielectric constant that reduce the capacitance between wires), **barrier/seed deposition** (a thin wall that stops copper diffusing, plus a starter film of copper), **electroplating** (growing the copper fill from a liquid bath), and **chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)** (a slurry and a pad that flatten each layer before the next). BEOL is thermally limited to ≤ ~400 °C so the low-k films and the copper survive. It ends with **passivation** (a protective top layer) and the aluminium or copper pad layer, and for advanced packaging, the **bump** or bond-pad layers (the solder or copper contacts the package attaches to).

### Step counts and mask layers

A "step" is one visit of a lot to one tool (including metrology and cleans). Approximate public estimates: N28 ~ 600–700 steps; N7 ~ 1,000; N5 ~ 1,100–1,300; N3 and N2 ~ 1,300–1,500+ depending on how metrology and cleaning are counted. Mask layer counts have grown similarly: ~40 for a 90 nm process, ~60 for 28 nm, ~80 for N7 (where multi-patterning inflates the count), and roughly 70–100+ for N3/N2, of which ~20–30 are EUV (each EUV layer replacing 2–4 DUV multi-patterning masks; N7+ introduced ~4–5 EUV layers, N5 ~14, N3 ~20–25, N2 ~25–30, with A16 similar to N2 plus backside-power layers). **Multi-patterning** splits one dense pattern across several coarser exposures because 193 nm light cannot print the pitch in one shot (Module 07); EUV prints it in one, which is why an EUV layer replaces several masks. The step count grows faster than the layer count because each layer adds more non-litho work: more **ALD** liners (atomic layer deposition, films grown one atomic layer at a time, Module 06), more selective depositions, more inline inspection.

Each lithography layer is a mini-sequence of ~10–15 steps. In order:

1. **Pre-clean**: a wet or vapour clean removes particles and residue from the previous step so the next film adheres.
2. **Hard mask or film deposition**: the layer to be patterned (or a tougher intermediate mask film) is deposited.
3. **Anti-reflective coating**: a thin film that stops light reflecting back up from the substrate and blurring the image.
4. **Resist coat and bake** (on a **track**, the robotic coater/developer that stands next to the scanner): resist is spun on and baked to drive off solvent.
5. **Exposure** (scanner): the mask pattern is projected onto the resist, field by field.
6. **Post-exposure bake and develop** (track): the bake completes the resist chemistry; the developer dissolves the exposed (or unexposed) regions, leaving the pattern.
7. **After-develop inspection (ADI)**: CD and overlay metrology on a sample of wafers checks that the resist pattern is the right size and in the right place.
8. **Rework loop if out of spec**: strip and recoat. Litho is the only reworkable step in the flow, because resist can be stripped and recoated without touching the film beneath, whereas an etch or an implant changes the wafer irreversibly.
9. **Etch**: the resist pattern is transferred into the film beneath by plasma.
10. **Strip**: the used resist is removed.
11. **Post-etch clean**: residues are removed.
12. **After-etch inspection (AEI)** and sampled defect inspection: the etched pattern is measured and a sample of wafers is scanned for defects.

Multiply by ~90 layers and you are at ~1,000 steps before counting implants, anneals, CMP, and the rest.

### Re-entrant flow

The fab is a **job shop** (a factory in which each job follows its own route among shared machines, the way a machine shop does, rather than an assembly line in which every product moves through a fixed sequence of dedicated stations): there are only ~15–25 distinct tool families (scanners, tracks, etchers of several types, CVD/ALD/PVD deposition platforms (chemical vapour, atomic layer, and physical vapour deposition, Module 06), implanters, furnaces and RTP (rapid thermal processing, lamp-heated anneals lasting seconds), wet and single-wafer cleaners, CMP, plating, and the metrology/inspection tools), and every lot returns to the same families dozens of times. A single lot visits lithography ~90 times, etch 200+ times, cleaning 200+ times. This **re-entrant flow** is the defining property of semiconductor factory physics: a lot at BEOL metal 12 competes for the same immersion scanner as a lot at FEOL fin cut, and a hiccup at any tool family ripples through every lot in the fab at every stage.

### Cycle time, Kingman's formula, and the operating curve

The industry rule of thumb is ~1.3–1.5 days of **cycle time** (elapsed time from wafer start to wafer out) per mask layer, with ~1.0 achievable in a mature, lightly loaded fab; a ~90-layer N3 flow therefore takes ~3–4 months from wafer start to wafer out, and TSMC has publicly described N5/N3 cycle times in that range. The **raw process time (RPT)**, the sum of actual processing durations, is only ~25–50% of that; the rest is waiting: in stockers for a tool, in batches for a furnace to fill, for metrology results, for a scanner's reticle change. The ratio cycle time / RPT is the **X-factor**, typically 2–4 in a well-run fab: X = 4 means the wafer is being processed a quarter of the time and waiting three-quarters, X = 2 means half and half.

Queueing theory connects those waits to uneven arrivals and uneven processing times. A tool needs spare time to catch up after a burst of arrivals or a slow job. If almost all of its time is already committed, a small disruption can leave a long queue. For a single tool with **utilization** u (the fraction of time it is busy processing), the **Kingman approximation** estimates the average wait before processing begins:

W ≈ (u / (1 − u)) × ((cₐ² + cₑ²) / 2) × tₑ

where W is the mean waiting time and tₑ is the mean process time per lot. The quantities cₐ and cₑ describe how uneven the gaps between arrivals and the processing times are. Each is a **coefficient of variation**: standard deviation, a measure of spread, divided by the mean. Zero means perfectly regular; 1 means the spread equals the mean, as in the random-arrival example below. The formula says three things: waiting scales with the process time, it scales with how irregular arrivals and processing are, and it explodes as u approaches 1, because the term u/(1 − u) goes to infinity.

> **Intuition:** Think of a supermarket whose checkouts are busy 95% of the time. On average they have 5% spare capacity, but customers do not arrive on average; they arrive in bursts, and during a burst the line grows. With 5% slack, the line drains only 5% faster than it fills, so a burst that formed in a minute takes twenty minutes to clear, and the next burst arrives before it has. At 50% utilization the same burst clears in a minute. Slack is what absorbs randomness, and near full utilization there is none.

> **Worked example: Kingman at a real tool.** Take a single-chamber tool with a mean process time tₑ = 1 hour per lot and fully random arrivals and processing, cₐ = cₑ = 1, so the variability term is (1 + 1)/2 = 1. At u = 0.8, W ≈ 4 × 1 × 1 h = 4 hours of waiting for 1 hour of processing: the lot spends 5 hours at the tool, an X-factor of 5. At u = 0.9, W ≈ 9 h and X = 10. At u = 0.95, W ≈ 19 h and X = 20. Raising utilization from 80% to 95% gains 15 percentage points of output from the tool and multiplies the wait by almost five.

| Utilization u | u / (1 − u) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 1 | Wait ≈ one process time (with cₐ = cₑ = 1) |
| 0.70 | 2.3 | Comfortable for non-bottleneck tools |
| 0.80 | 4 | Typical target for expensive non-bottleneck tools |
| 0.90 | 9 | Bottleneck territory |
| 0.95 | 19 | Only the scanners, and only with variability driven down |

This table is the **operating curve** every fab manager lives on: push utilization of the $200 M scanners toward 95% to amortize them, and cycle time balloons; run them at 80% to keep lots flowing, and the depreciation per wafer rises. How, then, do real fabs achieve X ≈ 2–4 when the single-server formula gives 10 at 90%? Two ways. First, variability is driven far below 1: arrivals are smoothed by the dispatcher, process times are nearly deterministic, and the biggest sources of cₐ (tool downtime, batch formation, hot-lot disruptions) are attacked relentlessly, because reducing variability is the only way to move the curve itself rather than slide along it. Second, several matched chambers share one queue; a lot waiting for "an etcher" can take whichever of eight chambers frees up first, and pooling cuts the wait roughly in proportion to the number of servers sharing the queue. That is a second reason, beyond vacuum and footprint, for the cluster tools and the tool matching described in the Tools section: a chamber that is not matched cannot join the pool.

### Little's law and WIP

If wafers arrive steadily but spend a long time inside, many must be inside at once. **Little's law** makes that relationship precise: WIP = throughput × cycle time. **WIP (work in process)** is the average number of wafers inside the fab, throughput is the average number finished per day, and cycle time is their average time inside, in days. The units cancel to leave wafers. For a stable system, use the same counting boundary and time period for all three quantities; the formula relates long-run averages, not an exact inventory at every instant. That is why it is the first thing an industrial engineer computes.

> **Worked example: WIP in a GigaFab.** A fab starts 100,000 wafers per month with a 90-day cycle time. Throughput = 100,000 / 30.4 ≈ 3,290 wafers per day. WIP = 3,290 wafers/day × 90 days ≈ 296,000 wafers, i.e., ~11,800 lots of 25 sitting in tools, FOUPs, buffers, and stockers at any instant. Those wafers each carry ~$5,000–15,000 of accumulated processing cost depending on their stage, so the fab has on the order of $2–3 billion of unfinished inventory in the building. Cutting cycle time from 90 to 75 days at the same output would release ~50,000 wafers of WIP and roughly $400–500 million of working capital, which is why fabs pay for cycle-time reduction even when they have no shortage of tools.

### Hot lots and dispatching

Not all lots are equal. Engineering lots for a new product's first silicon, yield-learning split lots, and customer expedites are flagged as **hot lots** (and, above them, **super hot lots** that preempt everything). A hot lot jumps every queue and may have a dedicated OHT vehicle and reserved tool slots; it can run the flow in ~40–60% of normal cycle time. The price is paid by everyone else: each hot lot adds variability (it is exactly the kind of burst the supermarket intuition describes) and typically the number is capped at a few percent of WIP. Below hot lots, the **real-time dispatcher (RTD)** ranks every waiting lot at every tool by rules combining due date, remaining steps, queue-time limits, batch-forming opportunities for furnaces, reticle availability on scanners, and line balance targets, recomputed continuously. (RTD is also the product name of Applied Materials' dispatcher, which is how the term entered the vocabulary.)

> **Worked example: one dispatch decision, and what a hot lot costs everyone else.** An etch chamber with a 1-hour process time frees up with three lots waiting. Lot A is ordinary production, 410 steps from the end and 3 days ahead of its plan. Lot B is ordinary production but was cleaned 2 hours ago against a 3-hour Q-time to this etch (queue-time limits are the next subsection), so it has 1 hour left. Lot C is a hot lot. The RTD applies its rules in a fixed order. Hard constraints first: if B waits one more process time it breaks its Q-time and is reworked or scrapped, so B runs now, ahead of the hot lot; a Q-time miss is a near-certain loss, a hot lot's delay costs an hour. Priority class next: C runs second. Then the ordinary lots are ranked by a rule such as **critical ratio** (time remaining to the due date divided by processing time remaining; a lot below 1.0 is already late), and A, with 3 days of slack, goes last, 2 hours later than it would have with an empty queue. That is the mechanism of a hot lot's cost: every time it preempts a queue, each lot behind it waits one more process time. Count it over the flow. A hot lot visits ~1,300 steps; say 3 lots are waiting at each tool it reaches (a queue of that depth is roughly what an X-factor of 4 implies), each delayed by one process time of ~1 hour on average (minutes at a metrology tool, hours at a furnace). That is ~3 lot-hours per step and ~4,000 lot-hours per hot lot over its ~45-day run. Spread over the ~11,800 lots of the WIP example above, that is ~0.3 hours each; 100 hot lots at once (~1% of WIP) add ~34 hours, about 1.5 days, to every ordinary lot's ~90-day cycle time. Treat that as a floor: it counts only the direct delay, not the extra burstiness each preemption injects into arrivals downstream, which is the cₐ term in Kingman's formula and grows without limit as utilization rises. That nonlinear part is why the What-can-go-wrong box below warns that letting 10% of WIP run hot does far worse than ten times this.

### Queue-time constraints

Many step pairs carry a maximum allowed elapsed time, a **Q-time**, because a surface changes chemically while it waits. Representative examples (exact limits are fab-specific):

- After an **HF-last clean** (a final rinse in dilute hydrofluoric acid that strips the oxide and leaves a bare silicon surface terminated with hydrogen atoms, which is stable for a while) and before **gate oxidation** or **epitaxy** (growing the gate insulator, or growing new crystal on the surface): ~1–4 hours. Oxygen and water attack the Si–H surface at random sites, so oxide growth in air is almost nothing for the first hour, ~0.3–0.5 nm after ~10 hours, and ~1 nm after about a week, slowing as the oxide thickens; the surface also adsorbs organics from the moment it is exposed. The Q-time is set so that the lot reaches the next tool while the surface is still effectively clean.
- After **PVD** (physical vapour deposition: sputtering metal atoms off a target in vacuum) of the TaN/Ta (tantalum nitride and tantalum) **barrier** and copper **seed** (a ~2 nm wall that stops copper diffusing into the insulator, plus a thin copper starter film for plating) and before electroplating: a few hours, because the 2–5 nm Cu seed oxidizes and the plated film then voids and delaminates; seed and plating tools sit adjacent for this reason.
- After copper CMP and before the SiCN dielectric **cap** (a thin silicon-carbon-nitride film that seals the exposed copper): hours, to limit corrosion of the exposed lines.
- After resist develop and before etch: hours to a day, against resist outgassing and CD drift; after etch and before strip, against polymer residue hardening.

The dispatcher must not start the first step of a Q-time pair unless capacity for the second is guaranteed; a lot that violates a Q-time is scrapped or reworked, so Q-time management is core MES logic.

> **Why it matters downstream:** These Q-times are why Module 06's gate-oxide section and Module 12's copper-seed section both talk about clustering and adjacency. Gate oxide is grown in the same cluster tool as the clean where possible, and seed and plating tools are placed side by side, because the chemistry of a waiting surface would otherwise set the fab's dispatching rules rather than the other way round.

> **What can go wrong:** A furnace that waits to fill its batch of 100+ wafers can push a lot past a Q-time; the dispatcher has to choose between running a half-empty furnace and reworking a lot. A scanner that goes down for a day leaves 20 lots that have already been coated waiting for exposure; the ones that miss their ADI window are stripped and recoated, which costs track time and resist. And overusing hot lots is a classic self-inflicted wound: a fab that lets 10% of WIP run hot has doubled everyone else's cycle time by injecting exactly the variability Kingman's formula punishes.

Of that, ~70–80% is process equipment; the building, cleanroom, and facilities are ~20–30%. | Electroplating | ~20–40 | $3–6 M | Lam, Applied |
| Metrology / inspection | ~150–300 | $1–30 M | KLA (~50%+), Applied, Hitachi, ASML (its HMI electron-beam inspection line), Nova, Onto |

Lithography (scanners plus tracks) is ~25–30% of tool capex, and the EUV fleet alone is $6–8 billion. Metrology and inspection are ~10–15%. Etch and deposition together are ~35–40%.

> **Worked example: how many EUV scanners does the fab need?** Suppose 100,000 wspm at N3 with 25 EUV layers: 2.5 million EUV wafer-exposures per month. An NXE:3800E is rated at ~220 wafers per hour (wph) at a 30 mJ/cm² reference **dose** (millijoules per square centimetre of light energy delivered to the resist, the amount it needs to switch from unexposed to exposed), but real layers run 30–60 mJ/cm². At fixed source power, a higher dose means each field must be scanned more slowly to collect more light, so throughput falls roughly as dose rises, and a fab-weighted average of ~150–180 wph is realistic; take 160. Availability (the fraction of time the scanner is up and not in reticle change, calibration, or maintenance) of ~85% gives 160 × 24 × 30.4 × 0.85 ≈ 99,000 exposures per scanner per month. 2,500,000 / 99,000 ≈ 25 scanners, and a prudent fab adds ~20% for dedication constraints (some layers are qualified only on certain scanners), engineering time, and ramp headroom: ~30. At ~$200 M each that is ~$6 billion of scanners consuming ~30 × 1–1.4 MW ≈ 35–40 MW of electricity continuously. Module 00 ran the same example with a more conservative 75–80% availability and got 20–25 scanners before margin; the difference between the two answers is exactly the margin argument, and a fab planner's choice of availability assumption is a bet on how well the fleet will be run.

### Utilization and the SEMI E10 states

Every tool's time is accounted for in the six **SEMI E10** states: productive, standby (up but idle, often waiting for WIP), engineering (running tests or qualifications), scheduled downtime (preventive maintenance, **PM**), unscheduled downtime (failures), and non-scheduled. Two ratios follow and are easy to confuse. **Availability** is up-time over total time: the fraction of the calendar during which the tool *could* run product. **Utilization** is productive time over total time: the fraction during which it *is* running product. A scanner that is 90% available and runs product 95% of its up-time is 0.90 × 0.95 ≈ 85% utilized. **Overall equipment effectiveness (OEE)** multiplies availability by performance (actual vs. theoretical throughput) and quality (good vs. total wafers).

A **bottleneck** is the tool family whose capacity is smallest relative to the demand on it; it sets the fab's output, and it is the only family worth running near the steep end of the operating curve. Leading-edge fabs run bottleneck tools (scanners) at 85–95% availability and utilization; non-bottleneck tools deliberately lower, ~70–85%, because the operating curve punishes high utilization with cycle time and the tools are cheaper. A fab's capacity is set by the bottleneck, so a fab is specified as "N wspm" precisely when its scanner fleet, running at its target utilization, can expose that many wafers across all layers.

Preventive maintenance is the largest planned loss: an etch chamber is wet-cleaned every few hundred to few thousand **RF-hours** (hours the plasma has been lit) as polymer builds up on its walls, a PVD **target** (the slab of metal being sputtered, consumed like a bar of soap) and its shield set are replaced after a set kWh of sputtering, an EUV **collector** mirror (the ~650 mm mirror that gathers light from the tin plasma, Module 08) is swapped after months as tin and hydrogen damage cut its reflectivity. After a PM the chamber is **seasoned** (dummy wafers condition the walls) and **qualified** (particle and film monitors in spec) before product returns. Shrinking PM frequency and duration is a permanent campaign, because every hour of scanner downtime is ~$4,500 of unrecovered depreciation (see the economics section).

### Clustering: the multi-chamber platform

> **Intuition:** A cluster tool is a sealed kitchen with one robot chef in the middle and four to eight ovens around it, all under vacuum so the food never sees air between courses. The wafer goes in through an airlock, the robot passes it from oven to oven in whatever order the recipe says, and it comes out through the airlock only when the whole sequence is done.

Almost no leading-edge process tool is a single chamber. The standard architecture is the **cluster tool**: a central vacuum transfer chamber with a robot, two load locks connected to the EFEM, and 4–8 process chambers arranged around it. Applied Materials' **Endura** (PVD, also degas, preclean, and ALD chambers), **Centura** (CVD, epi, etch, RTP), and **Producer** (plasma-enhanced CVD, with twin chambers) platforms; Lam's **Kiyo**, **Flex**, **Versys**, and **Vector** platforms; and TEL's **Telius**, **Tactras**, and **Trias** platforms all follow this design. Clustering delivers three things:

1. **Vacuum integrity between steps.** The copper barrier/seed sequence (**degas** at ~300 °C, which bakes adsorbed water out of the wafer; an argon or reactive **pre-clean**, which sputters or chemically strips the native oxide from the underlying **via** (the vertical metal plug that connects one wiring level to the one below; the pre-clean exposes clean metal at the bottom of the hole so the new copper bonds to it electrically); TaN barrier; Ta liner; Cu seed PVD) runs across four chambers of one Endura without seeing air; the same holds for a high-k/metal gate stack. Air exposure between these steps would form oxides and ruin adhesion and resistance.
2. **Throughput and footprint.** Four chambers on one mainframe process four wafers in parallel while sharing one EFEM, one set of load ports, and one footprint in a cleanroom that costs on the order of $10,000+ per m² to build.
3. **Redundancy.** If one chamber goes down for PM the platform keeps running at reduced capacity, and, as the queueing section showed, chambers that share a queue cut waiting time.

The trade-off is that identical chambers are never quite identical. **Tool matching** is the discipline of making every qualified chamber produce the same thickness, etch depth, CD, and defect count so that a lot can be dispatched to any of them. It is done by matching hardware (the condition of the **showerhead**, the perforated plate that distributes gas evenly over the wafer; the **electrode gap**, the spacing between the wafer and the powered plate above it that sets the plasma's density profile; the RF match), matching sensor traces via FDC (below), and running periodic matching wafers on a shared metrology tool. Where matching fails, a chamber is **dedicated** to particular layers, and a fab's dispatching flexibility is limited largely by how many chambers are qualified for each recipe.

> **What can go wrong:** A **bimodal** CD distribution (two humps in the histogram of measured line widths) across a week of lots is the classic signature of one mismatched chamber in a pool; the fix is to find which chamber, pull it from the pool, and re-match it. The **first-wafer effect** is the drift on the first wafer or two after a chamber has sat idle, because the walls have cooled or lost their conditioning; fabs run a warm-up wafer or keep an idle plasma lit to avoid it. A particle excursion after a PM is often traced to a shield that was reinstalled with a flake of old film still on it. And an FDC interdict (below) can take a chamber down in the middle of a lot, leaving 12 wafers done and 13 waiting for a different chamber to be matched to it.

## The Lot, the Recipe, and the Software Stack

### The lot

The unit of manufacturing is the **lot**: 25 wafers in one FOUP, sharing one lot ID, one product, one **route** (the ordered list of operations for that technology and product), and one history. Historically a paper **run card** (or traveler) rode with the lot and each operator stamped it; today the run card is entirely electronic and the physical lot is identified by the FOUP's RFID and the wafers' laser-scribed IDs. Wafers within a lot may be split (a **split lot** for engineering experiments, with different wafers receiving different conditions), merged, or scrapped individually; the MES tracks each wafer's slot and history.

### MES, SECS/GEM, and GEM300

Strip away the standards and the software stack does something simple. Every tool needs to say "carrier X arrived", "wafer 7 finished", "alarm 12", and a central computer needs to reply "run recipe Y on slots 1–25" and record what happened. The **manufacturing execution system (MES)** is that central computer, the fab's central nervous system: it holds every lot's current operation, next operation, history, holds, and metrology data; it enforces the route (a tool will refuse a lot whose MES state does not match); it manages Q-times, sampling plans, and rework; and it feeds the dispatcher and the reporting systems. A **hold** is a lot frozen in place until an engineer decides what to do with it, and that decision (release, rework, scrap) is its **disposition**. TSMC runs its own in-house MES and manufacturing data platform; commercial systems include Applied Materials SmartFactory (built on the Brooks Software business Applied bought in 2006), Siemens Opcenter (the former Camstar), and IBM SiView (widely used in Japanese and some Korean fabs), with connectivity layers from companies like PEER Group and Cimetrix.

The shared vocabulary in which tools and the MES talk is **SECS/GEM** (SEMI Equipment Communications Standard / Generic Equipment Model), a 1980s-era message standard carried over ordinary TCP network connections that still works, extended for 300 mm automation by the **GEM300** suite, which adds the ideas of a carrier (the FOUP), a process job (one recipe applied to a list of wafers), and per-wafer tracking. What matters is the handshake, which happens ~6,600 times an hour across the fab:

1. **Carrier arrives.** The OHT vehicle lowers the FOUP; the load port reads its RFID and reports "carrier X at port 2" to the MES.
2. **Verify.** The MES looks up which lot is in carrier X and checks that the lot's next operation is one this tool is qualified to run, that no hold is set, and that any Q-time is still open; if anything fails, the tool refuses the carrier and the MES routes it away.
3. **Slot map.** The load port opens the door, sweeps its light beam across the slots, and reports which slots hold wafers; the MES compares that to the lot's expected wafer list and stops on any mismatch.
4. **Recipe and job.** The MES sends the recipe name (with any APC parameter tweaks, below) and the wafer list; the tool creates a process job for those wafers, and a control job that sequences several process jobs if the lot is split.
5. **Run and report.** The tool processes the wafers, sending an event per wafer (start, end, chamber used) and every alarm, while a separate higher-bandwidth channel streams sensor traces for FDC.
6. **Close.** The tool reports the job complete; the MES advances the lot's state to the next operation, records the history (tool, chamber, recipe, times, alarms) against every wafer, and the dispatcher releases the carrier to the next vehicle.

Each of those messages is a transaction the MES commits, which is what the People section means when it says that every action on the floor is a transaction against the MES. For reference, the standard numbers engineers quote: SECS/GEM is SEMI E5 (message format) and E30 (equipment model); GEM300 is E87 (carriers), E40 (process jobs), E90 (substrate tracking), and E94 (control jobs); the streaming sensor channel is **EDA / Interface A** (Equipment Data Acquisition, SEMI E120–E164), at up to ~10 Hz.

### Recipes

A **recipe** is a tool-level program: a sequence of steps, each with setpoints and tolerances. The setpoints are written in the tool's own units: gas flows in **sccm** (standard cubic centimetres per minute, the volume the gas would occupy at standard temperature and pressure), pressure in **mTorr** (a millitorr is 1/760,000 of an atmosphere, ~0.13 Pa; plasma etch typically runs at 5–100 mTorr), RF power in watts, the temperature of the chuck (in a vacuum tool usually an **electrostatic chuck (ESC)**, which clamps the wafer with an electric field because a suction chuck has nothing to suck against in a vacuum), time, and **endpoint** criteria (how the tool knows to stop, for example by watching the plasma's glow change colour as the film underneath is reached). Leading-edge recipes are locked in a recipe management system; the MES selects which recipe (and which parameter tweaks, below) to download for each lot, and any change is under formal change control because a mis-set flow of a few sccm changes CD by nanometres. The full set of recipes for a technology across all ~1,300 steps, plus the route and the design rules, is what a fab means by the **process of record (POR)**.

### APC: run-to-run and feed-forward control

No process is stable enough to run open loop. **Advanced process control (APC)** wraps every critical step in a feedback loop:

- **Run-to-run (R2R) control** measures the output of lot n (post-etch CD, post-CMP thickness) and adjusts the recipe for lot n+1 (etch time, polish time, dose). The usual estimator is an **exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA)** of the process offset: new estimate = λ × latest measurement + (1 − λ) × previous estimate, where λ (typically 0.2–0.4) is how much the controller trusts a single lot. The equation says the controller believes the trend, not the last data point, and it is combined with outlier rejection and step-size limits.
- **Feed-forward control** uses incoming measurements: a resist CD 0.5 nm wide at ADI makes the etch controller trim the etch; a thick incoming dielectric makes the CMP controller polish longer.
- **Scanner corrections** are the most elaborate: overlay is corrected per scanner, reticle, wafer chuck, and **field** (the ~26 × 33 mm area printed in one scanner shot) with tens of terms fitted from overlay metrology on previous lots (increasingly per wafer, from on-scanner alignment data), plus dose and focus maps for CD uniformity.

> **Worked example: run-to-run etch control.** An etch step targets a CD of 20.0 nm. Three successive lots measure 20.6, 20.5, and 20.7 nm, i.e. offsets of +0.6, +0.5, and +0.7 nm. With λ = 0.3 and a starting estimate of 0, the EWMA after each lot is 0.3 × 0.6 = 0.18 nm; then 0.3 × 0.5 + 0.7 × 0.18 = 0.28 nm; then 0.3 × 0.7 + 0.7 × 0.28 = 0.40 nm. The controller now believes the process is running ~0.4 nm wide. If the etch's sensitivity is 0.5 nm of CD per second of etch time, it changes the next lot's etch time by 0.40 / 0.5 ≈ 0.8 s in the direction that narrows the line. Why λ < 1? With λ = 1 the controller would have swung the recipe by the full 0.6 nm after the first lot and then back and forth on every measurement; one noisy lot must not swing the recipe, so the controller deliberately reacts to a third of what it sees and converges over several lots.

The combinations of tool, chamber, product, layer, and reticle multiply into thousands of control threads per fab, maintained by APC software (Applied SmartFactory, or in-house systems at TSMC, Samsung, and Intel).

### FDC and sampling

**Fault detection and classification (FDC)** watches the tool rather than the wafer. Every chamber streams hundreds of sensor traces per wafer, and each one has a meaning:

| Sensor | What a drift means |
|---|---|
| RF forward and reflected power | Reflected power rising means the plasma has changed or the match network is failing; the wafer is getting less power than the recipe says |
| **DC bias** on the wafer (the steady negative voltage the wafer settles to under an RF plasma, typically tens to hundreds of volts, because the light electrons reach it faster than the heavy ions in each RF cycle; it sets how hard the ions strike the surface) | The plasma's ion energy has shifted, so etch rate and profile will shift |
| Chamber pressure and **throttle-valve** position (the throttle valve is a butterfly valve in the exhaust line between chamber and pump; a controller opens or closes it to hold the recipe's pressure against the incoming gas flow) | The valve opening further for the same pressure means the pump is weakening or a leak has opened |
| Each mass-flow controller (MFC) flow | Flow not matching setpoint means a clogged or drifting controller; a few sccm is nanometres of CD |
| ESC temperature and helium **backside** leak (helium is flowed into the micrometre-scale gap between wafer and chuck to carry heat across it, because in vacuum there is no air to conduct it) | A rising leak rate means the wafer has lifted off the chuck and the gas is escaping at the edge, so it is no longer clamped flat; it will run hot and etch unevenly |
| Optical emission spectrum (OES) | The endpoint signature arriving early or late means the film was thinner or thicker than expected |
| Chamber wall temperature | A cold wall after idle time explains a first-wafer effect |

FDC compares each trace to a **golden trace** window (the recorded sensor curve of a known-good wafer, with tolerance bands around it) or a multivariate model (**PCA** and **PLS**, principal component analysis and partial least squares, statistical methods that compress hundreds of traces into a few health scores); a deviation triggers a warning, a wafer hold, or an automatic tool interdict before the next wafer starts. FDC catches the failures metrology would see only hours later: a clogged showerhead hole, a drifting MFC, an ESC losing clamping, a leaking O-ring.

Because metrology is expensive and slow, only a fraction of wafers are measured: a typical **sampling plan** measures 2–3 wafers per lot at 5–13 sites for CD or thickness, and inspects 1–2 wafers per lot for defects at critical layers, with sampling increased for new tools, post-PM qualification, or when SPC charts show drift. **Statistical process control (SPC)** charts every measured parameter against **control limits** (typically ±3 standard deviations of the stable process, derived from its measured capability); an out-of-control point places the tool or lot on hold until an engineer dispositions it. The risk in sampling is obvious once stated: a 2-of-25 sample cannot see an excursion that hit one wafer, which is exactly why FDC watches every wafer through the tool's sensors instead.

### Non-product wafers

A large share of tool time goes to wafers that will never become chips. **Monitor wafers** (**test wafers**) check tools: **particle monitors** (a bare **prime wafer**, a new polished wafer of full specification from Module 03, run through a chamber and scanned on a KLA Surfscan SP7 for added particles ≥ ~20–30 nm, against a spec of a few adders per pass), **film monitors** (blanket deposition then **ellipsometry**, which measures film thickness from how polarized light reflects, or a **four-point probe**, which measures a film's **sheet resistance**: the resistance across any square of the film, in ohms per square, which for a film of known material gives its thickness in one electrical number; four probes are used so that the contact resistance of the probes themselves drops out), and **etch-rate monitors**. **Dummy wafers** fill empty slots in batch furnaces, which need a full uniform load for gas-flow and thermal reasons, and season chambers after PM. **Pilot** (send-ahead) wafers run ahead of a lot at risky steps. Non-product wafers are on the order of 10–20% of wafer moves in a mature fab and more during ramps, which for a 100,000 wspm fab is the equivalent of processing ~10,000–20,000 extra full-flow wafers a month, each consuming time on $5–200 M tools. They are reclaimed (stripped and re-polished by specialists such as RS Technologies and Pure Wafer) several times before retirement, and their cost is a line item every fab tries to shrink.

> **What can go wrong:** An uncontrolled recipe edit, made by an engineer debugging at 3 a.m. and never reverted, is the reason recipe management systems log every change and diff the running recipe against the POR before each lot. An SPC hold on a tool stops every lot behind it, so a false alarm from a badly set control limit costs real capacity. A sampling miss (an excursion on wafer 14 when wafers 1 and 13 were measured) is discovered weeks later at electrical test, and by then dozens of lots have passed through. And an APC controller with λ set too high chases metrology noise: it adjusts the recipe on every lot, adds variability rather than removing it, and shows up as a CD chart that oscillates.

## People

A GigaFab employs on the order of 3,000–6,000 people, and TSMC's total headcount is ~80,000+ (2024). The roles, with rough shares (the author's estimates; TSMC does not publish them, so all are ~):

| Role | What they own | Rough share of headcount |
|---|---|---|
| Technicians | Run the floor 24/7: alarms, PMs, reticle and consumable loading, hold dispositions, qualification wafers | ~40–50% (the largest group) |
| Process and integration engineers | A **module** (a process area such as litho, etch, thin films, diffusion/implant, CMP, or wet, and the team that owns it) or a layer: recipes, SPC charts, APC controllers, yield; integration engineers own the flow across modules for a node | ~15–20% |
| Equipment engineers | The tools: PM schedules, repairs, spares, uptime, matching, working alongside hundreds of resident **field-service engineers** (supplier employees stationed in the fab) from ASML, Applied, Lam, TEL, and KLA | ~10–15% |
| Device and yield engineers | Electrical test data (the **wafer acceptance test (WAT)**, a set of transistor and wiring measurements made on test structures on every wafer, plus die-level test results), defect **Paretos** (a bar chart of defect causes ranked largest first; you fix the top bar, then the next), and the yield-learning loop | ~5% |
| Industrial and facilities engineers | Capacity, dispatching rules, WIP, and cycle time; the building and utilities | ~5% |
| Quality, IT, planning, supply chain, **environment, health, and safety (EHS)** | Everything else | ~10% |

What the day looks like follows from the software stack. A technician's shift begins with a **pass-down** (the outgoing crew's handover of open alarms, holds, and PMs in progress), then works the alarm queue, the PM schedule, and the list of lots on hold that need a disposition; almost nothing on the floor is done from memory, because every action is a transaction against the MES. Process engineers spend their mornings on SPC charts and the daily yield meeting (which layer moved, on which chamber, on which lots) and their afternoons on experiments: split lots, matching wafers, and recipe changes under change control. Equipment engineers live in the E10 states: a chamber in unscheduled downtime is a countdown that they and the supplier's field-service engineer are both measured on. Yield engineers own the loop that connects all of it, from a Pareto of inline defects to a failure-analysis result to a fix in someone else's module. The fab's headcount is set by these loops, not by the number of wafers: automation removed the people who carried boxes, not the people who decide.

Because the fab runs 24/7, floor staff work rotating 12-hour shifts: covering every hour of the week with 12-hour shifts and days off needs four crews (two on duty each day, two resting), rotating between days and nights. Engineers are on call. TSMC's culture is famously intense: engineers respond to tool or yield issues at any hour, and in 2014 the company formed a formal three-shift R&D program (the "Night Hawk" team, paid a premium to develop 10 nm and 7 nm around the clock) so that process development itself never stopped. That culture is part of why TSMC's ramps are fast, and it became a flashpoint in Arizona. TSMC broke ground on **Fab 21** in 2021 planning to hire thousands locally, found the US pipeline of fab technicians and engineers thin, sent ~600 American hires to Tainan for 12–18 months of training, brought over 1,000+ Taiwanese engineers on assignment, and was reported (Rest of World, the New York Times) to have struggled with culture clashes over hours, hierarchy, and safety practices. Phase 1's start was pushed from 2024 into 2025, then pulled back in to reach N4 volume production in 4Q 2024, and as of 2026 the site's hiring-and-training model (local hires trained in Taiwan, a large expatriate cadre tapering over years) had become the template for the company's overseas expansion.

## Utilities: A Fab Is a Chemical Plant

### Electricity

A leading-edge GigaFab draws several hundred megawatts continuously and the largest sites approach a gigawatt. For scale, a large GigaFab site draws as much power as a city of ~500,000 people, and a gigawatt is the output of one large nuclear reactor. Roughly: process tools ~40–50% (EUV scanners at 1–1.4 MW each are the largest single loads; a 30-scanner fleet is ~40 MW), cleanroom HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning) ~20–30%, sub-fab pumps, abatement, and gas/chemical systems ~15–20%, and the UPW plant, ASU, and everything else the rest. TSMC consumed ~24.8 **TWh** (terawatt-hours; one TWh is a billion kilowatt-hours) in 2023 and ~25.6 TWh in 2024, roughly 9% of Taiwan's total electricity (estimates range ~7–9% by year and accounting), projected to reach anywhere from ~12% to ~24% by 2030 depending on the forecast as N2 and A16 fabs come online, which makes Taiwan's energy policy, 2025 nuclear phase-out, and LNG (liquefied natural gas) terminals fab supply-chain issues. TSMC has committed to 100% renewable electricity by 2040 (its RE100 target, moved up from 2050 in 2023) and contracts long-term output from Taiwanese offshore wind farms (as of 2025).

> **Worked example: electricity per wafer.** A 500 MW fab making 100,000 wafers a month uses 500,000 kW × 24 h × 30.4 days ≈ 365 million kWh a month, or ~3,600 kWh per wafer, about what a household uses in a year. At ~$0.10 per kWh that is ~$360 per wafer, ~3–4% of a ~$10,000 wafer, consistent with the ~5% utilities line in the economics table once water and gases are added. The 30-scanner EUV fleet at ~35–40 MW is about 7% of the site's draw.

Fabs have generators and **UPS** (uninterruptible power supply: batteries that carry the load for the seconds until generators start) for safe shutdown, but a grid sag of a fraction of a second trips sensitive tools and can scrap thousands of wafers mid-process. The mechanism is that plasma tools and scanners have interlocks that abort on a voltage dip of a few cycles, because a half-etched wafer cannot be resumed, and restarting a furnace or a UPW loop takes hours; the tools protect themselves and the wafers in them are lost. TSMC's fabs are therefore fed from dedicated substations with redundant feeds.

### Water

A GigaFab consumes on the order of tens of thousands of tonnes (cubic metres) of water per day; TSMC company-wide draws roughly 150,000–200,000+ tonnes per day across all fabs. The water goes mainly to the **UPW** plant, which turns municipal water into 18.2 MΩ·cm resistivity water. Resistivity is a purity gauge: pure water barely conducts electricity, every dissolved ion adds conductivity, and 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C is the theoretical limit, the resistivity that remains when the only ions left are water's own H⁺ and OH⁻. The plant also holds total organic carbon (**TOC**, the dissolved carbon compounds) below 1 ppb, dissolved oxygen below 1 ppb, particles below 1 per mL at 20 nm, and boron and silica at ppt levels, through a chain of unit operations: multimedia filtration (sand and anthracite beds for grit), activated carbon (adsorbs chlorine and organics), softening (removes calcium and magnesium), **reverse osmosis** in two passes (a membrane that passes water but not dissolved ions, driven by pressure), degasification (strips dissolved gases), ultraviolet (UV) light at 185 nm (makes hydroxyl radicals that burn remaining organics to CO₂) and 254 nm (kills bacteria), **electrodeionization** (an electric field that pulls the last ions out through ion-exchange membranes) and mixed-bed **ion exchange** (resin beads that swap remaining ions for H⁺ and OH⁻), and final **ultrafiltration** (a ~20 nm sieve) at the point of use, in a continuously recirculating loop so that water never stands still. Rinsing after every wet clean and CMP step consumes on the order of several thousand litres of UPW per wafer over the full flow, and cooling towers evaporate a large share of the remainder. Fabs recycle 85–90% of process water internally.

Not all of the site's water becomes UPW. A fab draws water for four different jobs: the UPW plant (the largest single use, and the part that is recycled and reclaimed), the cooling towers that reject the heat of hundreds of megawatts of tools and air conditioning (they evaporate a large share of what they are given, which makes cooling the hardest use to recycle), the wet scrubbers that wash acid and alkaline exhaust, and the domestic and fire systems. The site figure in the worked example below covers all four; the "several thousand litres of UPW" is the first alone.

> **Worked example: water per wafer.** Take a site drawing 30,000 m³ a day and finishing 3,290 wafers a day (the throughput from the WIP example). That is 30,000 / 3,290 ≈ 9 m³, or ~9,000 litres, of water per wafer, matching the "several thousand litres of UPW" figure once cooling and utility water are included. Here “drawing” denotes fresh-water intake, so the result is already about 9 m³ of intake per wafer; applying a recycling percentage again would double-count reuse. In a different model where 30,000 m³/day denotes total process-water demand before reuse, 85–90% substitution by recycled water would leave 1–1.5 m³ per wafer of make-up water, before cooling and other losses. State the boundary before comparing sites.

Water is the utility most exposed to weather. In spring 2021 Taiwan suffered its worst drought in over half a century (no typhoon had made landfall in 2020); reservoirs feeding Hsinchu and Taichung fell to ~10% capacity, the government cut irrigation to tens of thousands of hectares of farmland to preserve industrial supply, and TSMC contracted fleets of water tankers to truck water to its fabs and drilled on-site wells as a contingency. The episode led directly to TSMC building industrial water-reclamation plants (the first in Tainan, treating municipal wastewater to fab-grade) and to its 2030 targets for reclaimed-water share. UPW plants are built and often operated by specialists: Ovivo (USA/Canada), Kurita and Organo (Japan), and Veolia (France).

### Gases and chemicals

The problem with a fab's gases is that the process needs them at purities of parts per billion, many of them are lethal at parts per million, some ignite on contact with air, a few are among the most potent greenhouse gases known, and all of them must reach ~1,000 tools through a building full of people. The mechanism that solves it is a chain of containment from the tank to the chamber, with a sensor on every link.

Bulk nitrogen (tens of thousands of **Nm³** per hour, "normal cubic metres", gas volume measured at 0 °C and 1 atm so that quantities are comparable; purified to ppb O₂ and H₂O) purges every tool, FOUP, and pipeline; on-site ASUs also supply oxygen and argon. Hydrogen, helium, and dozens of **specialty gases** arrive in cylinders or ISO containers (standard shipping-container-sized tanks) at sub-fab gas cabinets: NF₃ (nitrogen trifluoride, for chamber cleaning), WF₆ (tungsten hexafluoride, the tungsten source), SiH₄ (silane, the silicon source), GeH₄ (germane), HBr and Cl₂ (hydrogen bromide and chlorine, silicon etchants), C₄F₈ (a fluorocarbon etchant), PH₃ and B₂H₆ (phosphine and diborane, the dopant sources). Many are toxic and some are **pyrophoric**: they ignite on contact with air. Silane is the classic case, because its silicon–hydrogen bonds react with oxygen so fast that the heat released ignites the rest of the gas.

A gas reaches the wafer along one path. It leaves its cylinder inside a ventilated **gas cabinet**, whose exhaust runs continuously so that a leak is drawn into the abatement system rather than the room, and whose **purge panel** flushes the line with nitrogen before and after every cylinder change so that air never meets the process gas. It travels through a **double-walled electropolished stainless line** (the inner tube is polished to a mirror finish so it sheds no particles and adsorbs little; the outer tube contains and detects any leak) to a VMB, into the tool's own gas box, through the MFC that meters it in sccm, and out of the showerhead over the wafer. Three safety devices sit on that path because of three failure modes: an **excess-flow valve** slams shut if flow exceeds what a tool could consume, i.e. if a line has broken; a toxic-gas sensor at every cabinet and VMB closes the cylinder valve automatically on a reading in the ppm range; and pyrophoric species are kept in cabinets separate from the oxidizers (oxygen, nitrous oxide), so that a leak of one cannot feed a leak of the other. Liquid chemicals (H₂SO₄ sulfuric acid, H₂O₂ hydrogen peroxide, HF hydrofluoric acid, NH₄OH ammonium hydroxide, IPA isopropyl alcohol, TMAH tetramethylammonium hydroxide developer, CMP slurries) are piped from a central chemical distribution room in double-contained lines, for the same reasons.

> **Worked example: nitrogen per wafer, and where it goes.** An on-site ASU for a GigaFab delivers on the order of ~40,000 Nm³/h of nitrogen (TSMC does not publish per-site figures; plants in this class are what the gas companies build for it). That is ~1 million Nm³ a day, and against the 3,290 wafers a day of the WIP example it is ~300 Nm³, roughly 400 kg, of nitrogen per wafer. Almost none of it touches the wafer. The largest sink is the sub-fab: every dry vacuum pump takes a continuous nitrogen **purge** (tens of standard litres per minute blown through its bearings and exhaust so that corrosive process gases never condense inside it), and a fab has thousands of pumps. Every process chamber is vented to atmosphere with nitrogen, not air, each time a load lock cycles; the load ports and stockers purge FOUPs; wet benches dry wafers under nitrogen; and gas lines are purged after every cylinder change. The base load is why the ASU is a permanent fixture on the site, and why the nitrogen contract is signed before the building is.

Process exhaust is segregated by chemistry (acid, alkaline, solvent, toxic/pyrophoric, general) and treated by point-of-use abatement at the tool and central wet scrubbers before the stack. The abatement is not optional: **PFCs** (perfluorinated compounds such as CF₄ and NF₃, used in etch and chamber cleaning) have greenhouse potencies thousands of times that of CO₂, because their carbon–fluorine and nitrogen–fluorine bonds absorb infrared strongly and nothing in the atmosphere breaks them for centuries. In numbers: the **global warming potential (GWP)** over 100 years is ~7,000 for CF₄ and ~17,000 for NF₃ (IPCC values differ by a few percent between reports), so 1 kg of CF₄ escaping the stack counts as ~7 tonnes of CO₂, more than a typical passenger car emits in a year. Burn-wet, plasma, or catalytic units at the tool are therefore specified to destroy ~95–99% of what reaches them, and the fab's emissions inventory is reported per wafer. Edwards (UK, part of Atlas Copco) and Ebara (Japan) supply most of the vacuum pumps and integrated pump-and-abatement units; DAS and CS Clean (both Germany) supply abatement. Module 04 covered where these consumables come from; the point here is that the sub-fab is a chemical plant with thousands of controlled connections and an EHS system as elaborate as the cleanroom's.

> **What can go wrong:** On 15 August 2017 a gas-supply error at a Taiwan power plant blacked out much of the island for hours, and in May 2021 two further island-wide outages hit within a week. The science parks were largely spared by backup power and by their priority in load-shedding, and TSMC reported only brief voltage sags with limited impact (from press reports); the episodes matter here because a sag of a few cycles is exactly the failure mode the electricity subsection describes, and a fab without its own substations and UPS would have scrapped every wafer that was mid-etch. The 2021 drought above is the water case, and the April 2024 Hualien earthquake described in the AMHS section is the seismic one: on the utility side, the seismic sensors also close every gas cabinet to a safe state, and each line has to be leak-checked before it is reopened. A toxic-gas alarm evacuates a bay in minutes and the bay stays closed until the source is found, which is why the gas monitoring network samples every cabinet and VMB continuously. And a UPW loop that picks up bacteria or a burst ion-exchange bed has to be flushed for hours or days before any wet tool can run, because everything downstream of the contamination is suspect.

## Contamination Sources and Gowning

The old cleanroom adage is that people are the dirtiest thing in the room, and even in a FOUP fab it is true. A seated, motionless person sheds ~100,000 particles ≥ 0.3 µm per minute; walking at normal pace, ~5–10 million per minute; skin flakes (a human sheds ~10⁹ skin cells per day), hair, cosmetic particles, textile fibres, and, from sweat and breath, sodium, potassium, and chloride ions. Those **mobile ions** are the oldest contamination problem in the industry: a sodium ion trapped in a transistor's gate oxide is a fixed positive charge that pulls electrons into the channel and turns the transistor partly on, shifting its threshold voltage; the resulting instability caused the 1960s MOS (metal-oxide-semiconductor, the transistor type in every chip) reliability crisis and is the reason skin never touches a wafer. The gowning protocol exists to contain all of this:

1. No cosmetics, perfume, ordinary paper, or wood pencils past the gowning room.
2. Gown in strict top-down order so nothing dirty passes over something clean: hair net and beard cover, face mask, hood, the **bunny suit** coverall (tight-weave polyester with woven carbon-fibre **ESD** threads; **electrostatic discharge** is a static spark, and one from a charged person can punch through a gate oxide about 1 nm thick, so the conductive fibres bleed charge to ground through the boots), boots over the coverall legs, safety glasses, and nitrile gloves. Gowns are laundered by specialist services on a schedule.
3. A **sticky mat** cleans soles; an **air shower** (~20–30 s of ~20 m/s filtered jets from all sides, with interlocked doors) strips loose particles from the gown before the cleanroom door opens.
4. Inside: no fast movement, no leaning over open tools, no touching the face; tool interiors are opened only with the FFU running and by procedure.

> **Worked example: particles landing on an exposed wafer.** How bad is a few minutes of exposure? A 0.5 µm particle of roughly unit density (1,000 kg/m³, like water) settles under gravity at ~8 µm/s. That comes from **Stokes' law**, v = ρ g d² / (18 µ), the speed at which air drag balances the particle's weight, where ρ is the particle density, g is gravity (9.8 m/s²), d is the diameter, and µ is the viscosity of air (≈ 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ Pa·s): 1,000 × 9.8 × (0.5 × 10⁻⁶)² / (18 × 1.8 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻⁶ m/s. It scales with diameter squared, so a 0.1 µm particle settles 25× slower, and denser mineral particles fall proportionally faster. The flux onto a horizontal surface is concentration × settling velocity × area. At ISO 5, 3,520 particles ≥ 0.5 µm per m³, onto a 300 mm wafer (area π × 0.15² ≈ 0.071 m²): 3,520 × 8 × 10⁻⁶ m/s × 0.071 m² ≈ 0.002 per second, ~7 particles per hour. At ISO 3 (35 per m³) it is ~0.07 per hour. So an open wafer in the ballroom collects a killer-sized particle every few minutes, which is why wafers are never left out there, while inside an ISO 3 mini-environment it can wait an hour and probably collect none. Treat this as a lower bound: for particles below ~0.1 µm, Brownian diffusion and electrostatic attraction deliver more than gravity does, which is why humidity and ESD control matter for the smallest particles.

Both environments are measured continuously. The ballroom and the mini-environments carry fixed **laser particle counters** (a pumped sample of air passes through a laser beam, each flash of scattered light is one particle, and its brightness gives the size; Particle Measuring Systems, Lighthouse, and TSI make them), which is how the ISO class is certified and trended. The number the process engineers actually watch, though, is on the wafer rather than in the air: **particles per wafer pass (PWP)**, the adders a bare monitor wafer picks up in one trip through a chamber, measured on the Surfscan and charted per chamber in SPC against a limit of a few adders per pass. The airborne count is the environment's report card and the PWP chart is the tool's, and a fab that confuses the two spends its money on a cleaner ballroom while its real particle sources sit inside the tools.

The larger contamination sources today are the tools and the process itself: flaking films from chamber walls and shields, worn robot end effectors and chuck pins, slurry agglomerates in CMP, resist bubbles, and backside particles transferred from chuck to chuck. That is why particle monitors and PMs matter more than gowning statistics. Backside particles are a special case in lithography: a 50 nm particle under a wafer on a scanner chuck deflects the surface by tens of nanometres over a millimetre-scale area, larger than the EUV **depth of focus** (the vertical range over which the projected image stays sharp, ~100 nm for EUV), producing a **hot spot** (a small area printed out of focus) of defocused features, so backside and chuck cleaning are routine. The **killer defect size** scales with the node: any particle larger than roughly half the minimum feature (~10–15 nm at N3/N2) can bridge or open a line. Note that the particle monitors described earlier detect adders down to only ~20–30 nm, larger than the killer size; the monitors catch the tail of the distribution, and the fab relies on the fact that a process that adds few 30 nm particles also adds few 15 nm ones, which is why KLA's inspection tools have chased ever-smaller detection thresholds (Module 13).

> **What can go wrong:** A typical particle excursion is traced backwards: the particle monitor for one etch chamber shows adders rising from 2 to 20 per pass; the PM log shows a robot end-effector pad was replaced two days earlier; inspection of the pad shows it is shedding, and it is replaced again with a pad from a different batch. A backside hot spot is found differently: ADI overlay and focus maps show a defocused patch on the same wafer position lot after lot, the scanner's chuck is cleaned, and the source is traced to a chuck pin on a track a few steps upstream.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Module 13's inspection sensitivity race exists because the killer size shrinks with every node while the monitor sensitivity has to be dragged down after it. Everything that section says about optical versus electron-beam inspection is an answer to the question this section poses: how do you find a 15 nm particle among 10¹² transistors?

## TSMC's Fab Organization

TSMC names fabs by number and builds them in **phases**: each phase is a separately constructed cleanroom block (typically ~25,000–30,000 wspm of capacity) that shares the site's utilities and is linked to neighbouring phases by AMHS bridges, so that "Fab 18" is really a campus of six to eight phases built over years. A **GigaFab** is TSMC's term for a 300 mm site with ≥ 100,000 wspm. Some terms in the table: **STSP** and **CTSP** are the Southern and Central Taiwan Science Parks (Tainan and Taichung); **risk production** means customers may **tape out** (freeze a design and send it to the mask shop to have its photomasks made) but yields are immature; **high-volume manufacturing (HVM)** is full-rate production; **specialty** processes are analog, RF, and sensor processes on older nodes; **planar** means the flat transistor of nodes older than ~16 nm, before the fin-shaped FinFET of Module 11; a **JV** is a joint venture co-owned with customers.

### Intel and Samsung, briefly

This is a side note, in one paragraph, because both companies' fabs follow the anatomy already described. **Intel** develops each process at **D1X** in Hillsboro, Oregon (the most advanced R&D site outside Taiwan, and the first fab with a **High-NA** EUV scanner, ASML's EXE:5000/5200; **numerical aperture (NA)** measures how wide a cone of light a lens can gather, and a larger NA resolves finer features, Module 08), and runs Intel 18A in volume at **Fab 52** and its neighbour Fab 62 in Chandler, Arizona, with Intel 4/3 at **Fab 34** in Leixlip, Ireland, and older nodes and packaging at sites in Israel and New Mexico. The differences from TSMC are in the process, the culture, and the customer base, not in the building, the AMHS, or the FOUP logistics.

## Bringing Up a Node

A leading-edge fab exists to run a process that did not exist when the building was designed. **Process bring-up** follows a multi-year cadence:

1. **Pathfinding (5–7 years before HVM).** Device architecture choices (FinFET → nanosheet → backside power → CFET, the sequence of transistor structures in Module 11; **CFET** is the complementary FET, an n-type transistor stacked on top of a p-type one to halve the footprint of a logic cell) are explored with imec (the Belgian research consortium), universities, and equipment suppliers on **short loops**, partial flows that run only the handful of steps under study (say, a fin etch and its metrology), so that an experiment takes days rather than the months of a full flow; materials and tool capabilities are screened.
2. **Process definition and PDK (3–4 years out).** The integration team fixes the flow. The problem the next deliverable solves is that a chip designer never sees the fab: they need to know what the node can print, how its transistors behave, and what its wires cost, without running a wafer. The **process design kit (PDK)** is that contract, released in versions (0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 1.0) as the process stabilizes, to design partners and **EDA** vendors (electronic design automation, the chip-design software companies). Each of its parts answers one of the designer's questions. **Design rules** say what geometry is legal (minimum line width and spacing, how far a via must sit from the end of a line), each rule being the fab's litho and etch capability written down as a constraint. **SPICE models** answer "how does the transistor behave": equations fitted to currents measured on test chips, which circuit simulators use to predict a transistor's current from its voltages. **Parasitic decks** answer "what do the wires cost": models of the unwanted resistance and capacitance of every metal level, so a simulator can predict delay. **Standard-cell libraries** are the pre-designed logic gates (a NAND, a flip-flop) that most of a chip is assembled from, drawn to the design rules by Arm, Synopsys, Cadence, and TSMC itself, and **IP** blocks are larger pre-designed pieces such as memory controllers. The negotiation that sets the cell dimensions is **design-technology co-optimization (DTCO)**. Cell height is counted in **wiring tracks**, the parallel lanes for metal lines at the minimum pitch that fit across the cell, and cell area is height × width, so the number of tracks, of nanosheets per transistor, and of fins is traded against what litho and etch can actually print. As an illustration, take a minimum metal pitch of 24 nm (leading nodes are in the low tens of nanometres): a 6-track cell is 144 nm tall and a 5-track cell 120 nm, a 17% area saving on every logic gate on the chip. It is not free: a 5-track cell leaves fewer lanes for the wires that connect its own transistors, so it needs a denser contact layer beneath it and more current per transistor from a taller nanosheet stack, and each of those is a new process step with its own defect contribution. DTCO is the meeting in which the design side asks for the track and the process side names its price in steps and yield.
3. **Test chips and the yield vehicle.** A large **SRAM** test chip (static random-access memory, the densest and most regular array on a chip, ideal for finding defects; 256 Mb is TSMC's traditional vehicle) plus logic test chips run through the developing flow while defect density **D0** (fatal defects per cm²) is tracked from ~1/cm² downward. **Scribe-line** structures (test structures placed in the strips between dies that the saw destroys at the end, so they cost no die area) give the per-wafer WAT electrical parameters (threshold voltage, contact resistance, via chains; the wafer acceptance test of the People section) for every lot.
4. **Risk production (~1 year before HVM).** Customers can tape out designs (the term was defined in the previous section); the foundry commits to ship, but yields and cycle times are immature. Early customers (Apple for N5, N3, and N2) accept the risk for first access.
5. **Volume production and ramp.** The fab goes from ~5,000–10,000 wspm to full capacity over ~1.5–2 years, phase by phase, each phase copying the first. TSMC's N2 is representative: risk production 2024, volume production from 4Q 2025 at Fab 22 with Fab 20 following, ~50,000+ wspm combined at end of 2025, and ~120,000–130,000 wspm targeted for end of 2026 (as of 2026).
6. **Yield learning.** D0 falls roughly exponentially with cumulative volume along a **learning curve** as each defect mechanism is found (inline inspection → classification → failure analysis → root cause → recipe or hardware fix) and removed. The shape has a mechanism: each fix removes one defect source, and because the remaining sources are many and roughly independent, each fix removes a roughly constant fraction of what is left, which is exactly an exponential decline against cumulative effort. A mature TSMC node reaches D0 ~0.1 defects/cm² or below. Cycle time falls too, as the fab learns which sampling and rework it can drop.

The yield model behind the D0 target is one line. If fatal defects land at random with density D0 (per cm²) on a die of area A (in cm²), the chance a die has none is Y ≈ e^(−D0 × A), where Y is the fraction of dies that work. The equation says a big die is hit far harder by the same defect density than a small one, because the exponent grows with area; the worked example below runs the numbers, and Module 13 refines the model for clustered defects.

> **Worked example: why big dies wait for a mature node.** Run the model along the learning curve, where D0 is tracked from ~1/cm² early in development toward ~0.1/cm² at maturity, for two dies: a ~100 mm² (1 cm²) mobile die and a ~600 mm² (6 cm²) GPU (graphics processor) die. For the mobile die the yield along that path is e^(−1) ≈ 37%, e^(−0.5) ≈ 61%, e^(−0.2) ≈ 82%, and e^(−0.1) ≈ 90%. For the GPU die it is e^(−6) ≈ 0.2%, e^(−3) ≈ 5%, e^(−1.2) ≈ 30%, and e^(−0.6) ≈ 55%. A phone chip is a saleable product at D0 = 0.5/cm² (61%); a reticle-sized GPU at the same D0 is scrap (5%) and does not become viable until D0 is near 0.2/cm². (**Reticle-sized** means as large as the ~26 × 33 mm ≈ 850 mm² field that one scanner exposure can print, the biggest single die that is physically possible; the 600 mm² GPU of this example is close to that limit.) That is why the first customer on each recent TSMC node has been a mobile SoC and the large AI and GPU dies have typically followed a year or two later on the same node, and why (Module 19) a reticle-limited die is built only on a node that has already been in volume production for a while.

Transferring a node between fabs is a discipline in its own right. Intel named its version **"Copy Exactly!"**: the receiving fab replicates the source fab's tool configurations, recipes, and facilities down to details that no one has proven matter, on the theory that finding out which ones matter is more expensive than copying. TSMC's practice is the same in substance: identical tool models and configurations, the same POR, the same chemical suppliers and often the same chemical lots, and **matching wafers** processed in one fab and measured in the other to prove that both produce the same films and CDs before product is transferred. The overseas fabs and every new phase in Taiwan are brought up this way.

> **What can go wrong:** The best-documented failures at this step are nodes that did not yield on schedule. Intel's 10 nm process (announced for 2015–2016) combined an aggressive density target with new materials and reportedly did not reach healthy yields until 2019, a multi-year delay that cost the company its process lead. Samsung's first 3 nm gate-all-around node was reported to struggle with yields for an extended period after its 2022 launch. TSMC's own N3B is reported to have slipped by a few quarters in 2022–2023 before its successor variants ramped. All three are "reported" rather than disclosed, because no foundry publishes yields; the pattern, though, is consistent: a node's schedule is set by D0, not by the calendar.

> **Why it matters downstream:** D0 sets the die-size economics in Modules 13 and 19. Whether a reticle-sized GPU die is viable at all, and what it costs, follows directly from the exponential above, and the learning curve is why a node's cost per good die keeps falling for years after launch.

## Fab Economics

### Depreciation and the 24/7 imperative

TSMC depreciates fab equipment over **5 years** **straight-line** (an equal charge every year; buildings over ~20 years); its annual depreciation charge is on the order of $20 billion. Equipment depreciation is a **fixed cost**: it does not change with output, and it accrues whether the fab makes 100,000 wafers or none. Everything about fab operation follows from that fact.

> **Worked example: depreciation per wafer.** A fab costs $20 billion: $15 billion of tools (75%) and $5 billion of building and facilities. Annual depreciation = $15 B / 5 + $5 B / 20 = $3.0 B + $0.25 B = $3.25 billion per year. At 100,000 wspm the fab produces 1.2 million wafers per year, so depreciation alone is $3.25 B / 1.2 M ≈ $2,700 per wafer at full output. If the fab runs at 80% **loading** (the fraction of its capacity in use), output is 0.96 million wafers and depreciation per wafer rises to ~$3,400; at 50% loading it is ~$5,400. A more realistic $30 billion N2 GigaFab at 100,000 wspm gives ~$4,000–4,500 per wafer, which against a manufacturing cost of ~$10,000–13,500 per N2 wafer (not disclosed; inferred below from price and margin) is ~35–45%. Per tool: a $200 M EUV scanner costs $40 M per year, ~$110,000 per day, ~$4,500 per hour in depreciation alone, which is why a scanner waiting 30 minutes for a FOUP is a reportable event.

> **Worked example: from price to cost to margin.** **Gross margin** is price minus manufacturing cost, as a share of price. An N2 wafer reported at ~$30,000, sold at a gross margin near TSMC's corporate ~58%, implies a manufacturing cost of $30,000 × (1 − 0.58) ≈ $12,600; if N2 carries a higher margin than the corporate average, say ~65%, the cost is ~$10,500, which is where the ~$10,000–13,500 range comes from. Against $12,600, the $4,000–4,500 of depreciation is ~35% of the cost of the wafer. Now drop loading to 80%: the same fab's depreciation per wafer rises by a quarter, ~$1,000–1,100 per wafer, and if the price does not move that comes straight out of margin. This is the whole mechanism by which a downturn in demand becomes a collapse in foundry profits.

That is why fabs run 24/7 at the highest utilization the operating curve permits, why TSMC's gross margin swings with loading (in the 2023 downturn, utilization on older nodes fell well below full loading and gross margin dropped from ~60% to ~53–54% in mid-2023), why a **fully depreciated** node (one whose equipment has passed its five years, so the charge falls to zero: N7 and N5 after 2025–2026) becomes enormously profitable with ~half its former cost gone, and why a fab that is a year late has lost ~20% of its equipment's economic life before shipping a wafer (the five-year clock starts when a tool is installed and accepted, not when it makes its first saleable wafer, and the tool's competitive life runs on the calendar regardless of whether product is flowing: a scanner bought for a node's launch is a generation older by the time a late fab starts using it).

Two forces from earlier in the module now meet. The operating curve says a tool should run at ~80% to keep cycle time sane; depreciation says every idle hour is a fixed cost with nothing to show for it. The resolution is not a compromise but a hierarchy: the bottleneck scanners, which carry the largest share of the depreciation, are run at 85–95% with every variability-reduction trick the process-flow section listed, and every cheaper tool family is bought with deliberate excess capacity so that it never becomes the queue. A fab, in other words, spends money on extra etchers to keep its scanners busy, and the ratio between the two is a design decision made years before the first wafer. The same logic reaches the customer: because the fab's cost is fixed and its capacity is committed years ahead, leading-edge customers increasingly reserve capacity with prepayments and long-term agreements (TSMC has reported carrying customer deposits for capacity since the 2021 shortage), so that the loading risk is shared rather than carried by the foundry alone.

### Cost per wafer breakdown

For a leading-edge wafer in the early years of a node, the approximate manufacturing cost structure is:

| Cost component | Share of wafer cost | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation (tools + building) | ~35–55% (higher in years 1–2 and at low loading) | Falls toward zero after year 5 |
| Materials and consumables | ~15–20% | Starting wafer (~$100–200 for prime, more for epi), resists (EUV resist is ~$5,000+/L), specialty gases, wet chemicals, CMP slurry and pads, sputter targets, reticles amortized over volume |
| Maintenance, spares, service contracts | ~10% | Chamber kits, EUV collectors, pump rebuilds, supplier service |
| Labor | ~5–10% | Higher in the US and Europe than in Taiwan |
| Utilities | ~5% | Electricity dominant, water and gases |
| Other (non-product wafers, scrap, overhead) | ~5% | |

N3 wafer prices in 2024–2025 were roughly $18,000–20,000 and N2 is reported at ~$30,000, against TSMC's corporate gross margin in the high 50s%, consistent with manufacturing costs in the ~$8,000–14,000 range depending on node and maturity. A 300 mm wafer at N3 yields ~100 large GPU dies or ~600 mobile SoC dies (system-on-chip, the main processor of a phone), so the fab's share of a $30,000 GPU is small, but the fab's share of the decision of *whether the GPU can exist at all* is total.

> **What can go wrong:** Underloading is the chronic risk: every point of loading lost is a fixed cost spread over fewer wafers, which is what the 2023 margin dip measured. A fab that opens a year late (permits, hook-up, tool delivery, or a node that will not yield) has burned a fifth of its equipment's depreciable life before its first revenue wafer. And the foundry businesses that have not reached TSMC's scale have run at a loss: Intel Foundry reported operating losses through 2024–2025, and Samsung Foundry, which is not reported separately, is widely estimated to have done the same (as of 2026). It is the same arithmetic run at lower loading and lower prices.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Fully depreciated N7 and N5 capacity is the pricing story of Module 20. Once the five years have passed, a mature-node wafer's cost is mostly materials, labour, and maintenance, which is why those nodes can be priced aggressively, why they carry the company's margin during a ramp, and why a rival cannot undercut them with a new fab that still carries its depreciation.

## Summary

- A leading-edge fab is a vertically layered building: air handling on top, a ballroom cleanroom on a 1–1.5 m waffle slab, the "dirty half" of every tool in the sub-fab below, utilities beneath that.
- Cleanliness is specified by ISO 14644-1 (ISO N = 10^N particles ≥ 0.1 µm per m³); the ballroom is ISO 5–6 while the spaces that touch wafers are ISO 1–3.
- The wafer's real environment is the FOUP (25 wafers, sealed, nitrogen-purged) and the tool's EFEM mini-environment; the room protects the tools, the box protects the wafer.
- Fans push ULPA-filtered air straight down at ~0.45 m/s (~400 air changes an hour) so particles are swept below the wafer level; litho areas hold ±0.1 °C because 1 °C moves a wafer's edge 0.8 µm against a 2 nm overlay budget.
- Gas-phase contamination (AMC) at ppb levels matters as much as particles: ammonia ruins chemically amplified resist, boron dopes silicon, siloxanes haze optics.
- Every FOUP move is made by ~1,000+ overhead vehicles: 4,000 lots × ~1,200 steps ≈ 6,600 deliveries an hour, which no human workforce could do.
- The flow is re-entrant: ~20 tool families visited dozens of times over ~1,300 steps, so ~3 months of cycle time is mostly waiting; Little's law (WIP = throughput × cycle time) puts ~300,000 wafers in the building.
- Kingman's formula explains why waiting explodes near full utilization (u/(1−u) = 4, 9, 19 at 80/90/95%); only bottleneck scanners are run that hard, and pooled, matched chambers plus low variability are how real fabs keep the X-factor at 2–4.
- Queue-time limits between steps exist because surfaces change chemically while they wait (native oxide, copper seed oxidation); the dispatcher must enforce them.
- Tools are cluster platforms (one vacuum robot, 4–8 chambers), accounted in SEMI E10 states, kept identical by tool matching, and wrapped in APC feedback and FDC sensor monitoring; only 2–3 wafers per lot are ever measured.
- A fab is also a chemical plant: hundreds of MW (~3,600 kWh per wafer), tens of thousands of m³ of water a day made into 18.2 MΩ·cm UPW (~9 m³ per wafer, mostly recycled), dozens of toxic gases behind double-walled lines and abatement.
- Depreciation over 5 years is a fixed ~$3–4 billion a year; that, not demand, is why fabs run 24/7, why loading drives margins, and why a fully depreciated node becomes the profitable one.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The whole fab is a Class 1 cleanroom."** → Reality: only the mini-environments inside tools and FOUPs are ISO 1–3. The ballroom around them is ISO 5–6, which is why people can work there at all; the FOUP, not the room, protects the wafer.
- **"Wafers move through the fab in a straight line, like a car on an assembly line."** → Reality: the flow is re-entrant. A lot visits the same ~20 tool families dozens of times over ~1,300 steps, and most of its 3–4 month cycle time is spent waiting in queues, not being processed (raw process time is ~25–50% of cycle time, an X-factor of 2–4).
- **"Fab cost is dominated by the building."** → Reality: ~70–80% of the $20–30 billion is process tools, and tool depreciation over 5 years is ~35–55% of the cost of every wafer in a node's early years. The building is expensive but is depreciated over ~20 years.
- **"Higher tool utilization is always better."** → Reality: queueing (Kingman's formula) makes waiting time explode as utilization approaches 100%. Bottleneck scanners are run at ~85–95%; other tools are deliberately left with slack to keep cycle time and WIP under control.
- **"Fabs run 24/7 because demand is high."** → Reality: they run 24/7 because depreciation is a fixed cost. Each idle hour of a fully staffed, fully equipped fab burns hundreds of thousands of dollars of asset life with no wafers to show for it, regardless of demand; in downturns fabs cut wafer starts and margins collapse.
- **"A new node is 'done' when it enters production."** → Reality: risk production starts with D0 several times higher than maturity. Yield, cycle time, and cost keep improving for 2–3 years through the learning curve, which is why early customers pay more and why old nodes become the profitable ones.
- **"'Lights-out' means the fab has few employees."** → Reality: automation removed the operators who used to carry cassettes, not the 3,000–6,000 engineers and technicians who keep a thousand tools matched, maintained, and yielding.
- **"A tighter cleanroom class always helps."** → Reality: the wafer's environment is the FOUP and the EFEM. Once the ballroom is clean enough to keep the tools and the load ports clean, further ballroom cleanliness buys almost nothing, because the particles that kill dies come from inside the tools.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 04 delivered the fab's inputs: polished and epitaxial 300 mm wafers from Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, and GlobalWafers arrive in shipping cassettes and are transferred into the fab's own FOUPs; photoresists, specialty gases, wet chemicals, slurries, targets, and photomasks flow into the sub-fab chemical rooms, gas cabinets, and reticle stockers. This module described the container those inputs enter: the building, the air, the automation, the software, and the economics that govern every one of the ~1,300 steps. Modules 06 through 12 now walk through those steps by mechanism, in the order the wafer meets them, starting with thermal oxidation and thin-film deposition (Module 06), the first things done to a bare wafer after its incoming clean. The fab's outputs, ~3–4 months after wafer start, are completed wafers with ~18 metal levels and pads, which go to wafer sort and test (Modules 13–14) and then to the packaging houses and TSMC's own CoWoS lines (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate, its advanced packaging, Modules 16–17).
