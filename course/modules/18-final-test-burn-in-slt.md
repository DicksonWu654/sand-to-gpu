# Module 18: Final Test, Burn-in and System-Level Test

Testing the pieces separately cannot prove that the assembled GPU works. A packaged **Blackwell** GPU (NVIDIA's 2024–25 generation, the B200 and GB200) leaving CoWoS assembly carries roughly $5,000–7,000 (analyst estimates) of silicon, HBM, interposer and substrate, and it has never been switched on as a system. **CoWoS** (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate, TSMC's package for large accelerators, Module 17) puts the GPU dies and their **HBM** stacks (**high-bandwidth memory**: towers of DRAM dies stacked on one another) side by side on an **interposer** (an intermediate fine-wiring structure: full silicon in CoWoS-S, or redistribution layers with local silicon bridges in Blackwell's CoWoS-L), which sits on an organic **substrate** (a laminated circuit board that fans the connections out to the module), under a metal **lid** that spreads the heat.

**Wafer sort** (Module 14) proved that each GPU die had working transistors and **scan chains** (test wiring that connects selected **flip-flops**, the one-bit storage element that holds a signal's value from one clock tick to the next, of which a GPU has hundreds of millions, into shift registers the tester can load and read) while it was still on the wafer, driven through a **probe card** (a plate of thousands of microscopic needles) that could not deliver full power, could not see the HBM stacks that were not yet attached, and could not run the 200 Gb/s **NVLink** lanes (NVIDIA's GPU-to-GPU link) at speed.

Assembly then subjected the die to **reflow** (melting the solder joints in an oven) at ~250 °C, **underfill** cure (hardening the epoxy wicked under each die), lid attach and several further heating cycles, each of which produces **warpage**, the bowing of the die-interposer-substrate sandwich by tens to hundreds of microns (~) as its layers expand by different amounts; any of these can crack a **bump** (a ~100 µm solder joint between interposer and substrate), delaminate an interface or open a **microbump** (a ~40–55 µm joint between a die and the interposer) on an HBM stack.

The job of the back-end test chain is to find every one of those problems before the part is soldered to an **SXM module** (the GPU's own circuit board with its voltage regulators), whose selling price and parts bill depend on generation (Module 19 models H100 at ~$25–30k selling price and ~$3,000–3,500 manufacturing bill, and B200 at ~$30–40k and ~$6,000–8,000 respectively; these are estimates, not disclosed contract costs), then to an **HGX baseboard** (which carries eight modules and the switch chips between them) priced at $200,000+, then into an **NVL72 rack** (72 GPUs on one NVLink fabric) priced at $3–4 million, where a single bad GPU can idle 71 good ones. Two kinds of dollar figure run through this module and it helps to keep them apart: the *cost* of the parts in a package or module (the Blackwell range above; the $3,000 examples below are separate illustrative legacy-package models), which is what is lost when a unit is scrapped, and the *price* of a module, board or rack, which is what sits idle when a unit fails inside it. The unit of the trade is the **test insertion**: one pass of a device through a socket on a tester or fixture, the unit in which test cost is counted; a defective part that passes one is an **escape**. The difficulty is economic as much as technical: each insertion costs tester time on a machine worth several million dollars, burn-in ties up a 1 kW oven slot for a day or two, and the sequence must be tuned so that the escapes it lets through cost less than the screening that would catch them.

By the end of this module you will be able to explain why a GPU gets three different screens (final test, burn-in and system-level test) rather than one longer one; how a burn-in oven's acceleration is computed from its temperature and voltage, and what 48 hours in it is worth in field life; why system-level test exists even at 99% scan coverage; why an HBM stack that escapes its vendor's test costs a whole package rather than a stack; and how the failures a hyperscaler's fleet records close the loop back to the test program. Module 14 covered the tester and the design-for-test methods it uses; this module concentrates on what changes once the die is in a package.

## Before you start

- **Wafer sort** (Module 14). A probe card with thousands of needles touches each die while it is still on the wafer and a tester runs scan and self-test patterns through it; sort finds most defects but cannot supply full current, run the fastest links, or see chips that are not yet attached.
- **Scan, BIST and fault models** (Module 14). Design-for-test circuits let the tester load a pattern into every flip-flop and read the result back; **built-in self-test (BIST)** engines do the same for memories. "Coverage" is measured against a list of modelled faults (a wire stuck at 0 or 1, a transition that is too slow), not against real programs.
- **Binning, ECID and e-fuses** (Module 14). Each die's measured speed and minimum voltage, and its unique **electronic chip ID (ECID)**, are burned into one-time-programmable fuses so the module and the driver know what this part can do.
- **Yield and defect density** (Module 13). Random defects per cm² (D0) set the fraction of dies that work, Y ≈ e^(−D0·A) for a die of area A; a bigger die yields worse.
- **CoWoS anatomy** (Module 17). GPU dies and HBM stacks sit on a silicon interposer via ~40–55 µm microbumps; the interposer sits on an organic substrate via ~100 µm C4 bumps; the substrate meets the board through ~1 mm BGA balls; a metal lid is glued on top.
- **HBM stack anatomy** (Module 15). 8–16 thinned DRAM dies are stacked on a logic base die and joined by **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, copper columns running vertically through each die; the stack talks to the GPU over a 1,024-bit bus.
- **Thermal expansion mismatch** (Module 16). Silicon expands ~3 ppm/°C (parts per million of its length per degree) and organic laminate ~15 ppm/°C; solder melts at ~217–250 °C; every heating cycle shears the joints between them.
- **Leakage and speed versus temperature** (Module 11). A transistor's off-current rises roughly exponentially with temperature and its drive current falls, so a chip's power and its fastest safe clock both depend on how hot the transistors are.
- **Electromigration and oxide wear-out** (Module 12). High current density slowly moves metal atoms until a via opens; a high electric field slowly damages the gate insulator; both speed up with temperature, and the school-chemistry rate law exp(−Ea/kT) describes by how much.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## Why Package Test Exists: What Sort Could Not See

A useful way to design a final-test program is to list what wafer sort could not physically do, because that list is exactly the set of failure modes final test must own. Sort had three handicaps: the probe card is a poor conductor of large currents and fast signals, the die was alone (no HBM, no second die, no substrate), and it was cooled by a metal chuck rather than by its package.

**Interfaces that did not exist at sort.** On a CoWoS package the GPU talks to each **HBM3E** stack (the HBM generation Blackwell uses) over 1,024 data bits plus command and address, routed through the silicon interposer at ~40–55 µm microbump pitch (Module 17). A **lane** is one data wire with its timing reference; a **channel** is a group of lanes with its own command and address wires; the **physical layer (PHY)** is the circuitry at each end that drives and samples them. At sort those signals end at microbumps the probe card cannot usefully contact at speed, and the HBM was not there. Final test is the first time the GPU-to-HBM physical layer is trained: each channel runs **link training** (box below), the memory controller sweeps read and write timing per lane, and **HBM MBIST** (**memory built-in self-test**, an on-chip engine that writes and reads back every address) plus GPU memory patterns exercise all eight stacks at the ~8–9.2 Gb/s per pin of HBM3E. On Blackwell the two GPU dies also talk over the ~10 TB/s **NV-HBI** die-to-die link (NVIDIA's high-bandwidth interface between the two halves of the chip) across a **CoWoS-L** bridge (the CoWoS variant with small silicon bridges embedded in an organic interposer, Module 17), which likewise exists only after assembly.

> **Intuition: link training and the eye diagram.** Picture 1,024 wires of slightly different length and capacitance, each carrying bits at ~9 Gb/s, so each bit lasts ~110 picoseconds and arrives at a slightly different moment on every wire. **Link training** is the controller sending known patterns and nudging each lane's sampling clock in picosecond steps until every lane samples in the middle of its bit. The result is pictured as an **eye diagram**: overlay thousands of received bits and the clean region where a 1 and a 0 are clearly apart looks like an open eye; its height in millivolts is the voltage margin, its width in picoseconds the timing margin. A lane with a cracked or resistive microbump has a smaller eye than its neighbours, and the training log is the only place it shows. The picture has one blind spot: a glitch that closes the eye once in a billion bits is invisible in it and is found by counting errors over a long run.

**Full-power operation.** A probe card feeding an 800 mm² die through several thousand needles is limited by needle current rating (~0.5–1 A each for **MEMS** probes, needles made by micro-machining, the micro-electro-mechanical-systems technique, rather than by drawing wire), contact resistance and path inductance, so sort runs the die at reduced activity. A **rail** is one of the chip's supply voltages, delivered on its own set of pins and copper planes; a large chip has several (memory interface, SerDes, input/output), and the **core rail**, which feeds the logic at ~0.75–0.9 V, is the one that draws ~1,000 A on a Blackwell-class GPU. A core rail drawing 1,000 A at ~0.75 V through needles rated at 0.5–1 A needs 1,000–2,000 needles for power alone and as many again for the return current. Each needle with its contact is ~0.3 Ω (Module 14's figure); 2,000 in parallel are ~0.15 mΩ, which at 1,000 A still drops ~0.15 V, a fifth of that 0.75 V rail. That loss is **IR drop**, current × resistance lost across the supply path; a transistor's speed depends on the voltage it actually sees, so a sagging rail makes timing fail in ways that look like defects, and sort throttles activity rather than judge the die at full load. In the package the **C4 bumps** (the ~100 µm solder joints between interposer and substrate), the substrate's copper power planes and the **load board** (the custom circuit board that carries the test socket and plugs into the tester, described in the next section) with its **decoupling capacitors** (charge reservoirs beside the socket that supply the nanosecond current spikes the tester's cables cannot) let the tester push 1,000 A-class currents at ~0.75–0.9 V, and only now can the die run at full **thermal design power (TDP)**, the sustained power its cooling is designed for, where marginal power-delivery paths, IR-drop-sensitive timing and vias prone to **electromigration** (metal atoms pushed along a wire by high current density until a void opens, Module 12) show up.

**High-speed I/O at speed.** Blackwell's NVLink 5 runs at 200 Gb/s per lane (18 links, 1.8 TB/s per GPU); **PCIe** (PCI Express, the general-purpose serial link between the GPU and its host CPU) Gen 5 at 32 GT/s and Gen 6 at 64 GT/s. A **SerDes** (**serialiser/deserialiser**) turns a wide, slow parallel bus inside the chip into one very fast serial wire and back at the far end. NVLink 5 signals with **PAM4** (four-level pulse-amplitude modulation), which sends one of four voltage levels per symbol, two bits per symbol; **GT/s** (giga-transfers per second) counts symbols rather than bits. At those rates a symbol lasts ~5–10 ps and the signal is a wave on a wire. Any change in the wire's geometry changes its **characteristic impedance** (the ratio of voltage to current a travelling wave sees) and reflects part of the wave back, as a ripple in a stream partly reflects where the channel suddenly deepens; a probe needle hanging off a trace is such a change, and the reflection lands on the bits that follow. A probe card is therefore an **impedance discontinuity** that cannot pass those rates, so sort tests SerDes in low-speed or **internal-loopback** modes (transmitter wired to its own receiver inside the chip, proving the logic but not the channel). The final-test socket and load board are controlled-impedance channels, and the tester (**automatic test equipment, ATE**: the multi-million-dollar machine that drives patterns and measures voltages and currents, Module 14) either loops each lane back through the socket or uses dedicated high-speed instruments (Advantest's Pin Scale Serial Link and successors) to measure **eye height** (open voltage margin, mV), **jitter tolerance** (how much timing wobble on incoming edges the receiver survives) and **bit-error rate (BER)**, the fraction of bits received wrong, targets ~10⁻¹² or better, at rate.

**Thermal behaviour.** A die on a sort chuck is heat-sunk by a massive temperature-controlled plate; the package's real thermal path (die, **TIM**, the **thermal interface material** paste or foil between die and lid, then lid, then the **thermal head**, the temperature-controlled plate the test cell presses onto the lid) is different and worse. Final test at a controlled **junction temperature (Tj)**, the temperature of the transistors themselves rather than of the lid or the air, exposes leakage-driven thermal runaway, temperature-dependent timing paths and thermal-sensor calibration errors. The **thermal runaway** loop: leakage rises roughly exponentially with temperature (roughly doubling every ~10–20 °C on a 5 nm-class process), leakage is heat, and heat raises the temperature, so a die whose cooling is marginal at 85 °C can climb on its own until it fails.

**Package-induced defects.** The driver is **CTE** mismatch: the **coefficient of thermal expansion** is how much a material lengthens per degree, and silicon at ~3 ppm/°C and organic laminate at ~15 ppm/°C disagree by ~12 ppm/°C, so a 26 × 33 mm die and its substrate disagree by ~0.1 mm (33 mm × 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C × 250 °C) across a reflow swing, and the joints between them take the shear. Because the layers are bonded together, the mismatch also bows the whole sandwich: that bowing is the warpage of the opening, and it is why the corners and edges of a package, where the bow is steepest, are where the joints fail first. The results: bump cracks; **low-k dielectric** cracks (the porous, brittle insulator between the die's copper wires, Module 12) at die corners where shear concentrates; underfill voids that later trap moisture; delamination of lid adhesive or of an HBM stack's **NCF** (**non-conductive film**, the pre-applied underfill between stacked DRAM dies); microbump opens on HBM stacks stressed by interposer warpage. These appear as opens, shorts or **intermittents** (connections that make and break with temperature or vibration) that continuity and leakage catch in the first second of the first final-test insertion (**FT1**), or as marginal timing on a few HBM lanes that only the interface test catches.

> **What can go wrong: the lane that passes at 25 °C.** A microbump with a hairline crack from interposer warpage still touches at room temperature and its lane trains normally. At 85 °C the interposer and substrate expand by different amounts, the crack opens by a fraction of a micron, contact resistance rises and the lane's eye narrows until the sampling clock cannot find a clean centre. A program that trained only at room temperature would ship the part, and the field would see correctable, then uncorrectable, memory errors on one channel of one stack months later. FT1 catches it by training at hot, recording every lane's margin and failing any lane below a limit set from the population, even when the link as a whole trained. The failing lane's ID maps through the design database to a microbump position on the interposer, and when failing positions cluster at a stack corner or a package edge, the assembly line's warpage map (Module 17) tells the **OSAT** (the **outsourced assembly and test house** that packaged the part as a service) which reflow profile or underfill to change.

## The Post-Packaging Flow

Three screens, three questions. Final test asks: does this package work, at full power and speed, right now? Burn-in asks: will it still work in three months, or is it carrying a defect one stress cycle from failing? System-level test asks: does it work as a product, booting real firmware and running real workloads, rather than as a circuit answering a tester's patterns? Each needs a different machine, and the order is set by cost: the cheapest, most informative screen goes first, so the expensive screens only see parts that passed the cheap ones.

Three terms before the table. An OSAT does packaging and test as a service, while a **test house** does only test. A **system-on-chip (SoC)** is a phone's main processor, with CPU, GPU, modem and memory controllers on one die. **JEDEC** (the Joint Electron Device Engineering Council) is the industry body whose standards define trays, package outlines, moisture ratings, HBM and the reliability tests later in this module.

For a high-end logic part, as of 2025–26, the sequence is standard enough that the Taiwanese test houses describe it in the same words for every customer. For NVIDIA's Blackwell family the reported flow is a first final test, a burn-in, a second final test, and then a system-level test, which is one more insertion than **Hopper** (NVIDIA's 2022 H100 generation) received. The fallout column is illustrative (industry ranges, not published NVIDIA data).

| Step | What happens | Typical time per unit | Typical fallout (illustrative, ~) | Where |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| Final test 1 (FT1) | Full tester (ATE) program at hot and/or cold: continuity, leakage, quiescent supply current (IDDQ), scan, memory self-test (MBIST), HBM interface, SerDes loopback, lowest-voltage and highest-frequency (Vmin/Fmax) search, fuse programming | 5–20 min for a large GPU across 1–3 insertions | 2–5% | KYEC, ASE, Amkor; TSMC runs a package-level screen ahead of it (see Where Back-End Test Happens) |

In order, with what physically happens:

1. **Incoming inspection.** A camera reads each package's 2D code, a gauge checks **ball coplanarity** (how far the bottoms of the solder balls deviate from one plane; a high ball will not wet at reflow), a sample is X-rayed for voids and bridged balls; gross assembly damage is removed before a socket is spent on it. This step is a side note in a module about electrical screens: it catches only mechanical damage and mislabelling, and one sentence is what it warrants.
2. **FT1.** A **test handler** (the robot that moves packages between trays and the socket, described in the next section) places each package in a socket on a load board docked to the ATE, a thermal head holds Tj, and the tester runs the full program and burns the fuses that record the result. The most informative insertion per minute goes first.
3. **Burn-in.** The packages move to sockets on burn-in boards in an oven and for one or two days sit at 125 °C Tj and elevated voltage with slow patterns toggling their logic. Nothing precise is measured; the oven only stresses and reads coarse self-test pass/fail.
4. **FT2.** Back on the ATE, a shortened program re-measures leakage, minimum voltage and the interfaces and compares each unit with its own FT1 record by ECID. Burn-in is bracketed by tester insertions because only the tester's precision instruments can measure whether leakage or minimum voltage drifted, and drift is the signature of a part that burn-in damaged but did not kill.
5. **SLT.** Each package goes into a socket on a stripped-down product board, boots real firmware and runs workloads for tens of minutes. The longest insertion comes last and sees only parts that passed everything cheaper.
6. **Outgoing QA and shipment.** A sample of each lot is re-tested and inspected, the lot record is tied to every ECID, and the parts are sealed in dry bags in trays for the module assembler.

Consumer and automotive microcontrollers run a shorter flow (often a single FT at two temperatures, no BI or SLT); memory runs a longer one with multiple burn-in passes; GPUs and phone SoCs run the full sequence.

## The Test Cell: Handlers, Sockets and Thermal Control

At sort the wafer prober moves the wafer; in final test a **test handler** moves the packages. The tester is docked to the handler, and the handler presents devices to a **load board**, a thick custom printed circuit board (**PCB**) that plugs into the tester head and routes every tester channel to one or more **test sockets**, spring-loaded receptacles that press the package's balls or pads against contacts. Tester, handler, load board, socket and thermal head together are a **test cell**, the unit in which a floor counts capacity.

### Handler architectures

Package vocabulary: **BGA** and **LGA** (**ball grid array** and **land grid array**) packages have solder balls or flat pads in a grid on the bottom, as every large processor does; **QFN**, **SOIC** and **TSSOP** (quad flat no-lead, small-outline integrated circuit, thin-shrink small-outline package) are small **leadframe** packages (the die is glued to a stamped metal frame whose legs become the package's pins) with metal pads or leads around the edge, used for microcontrollers and analog parts. Throughput is quoted in **UPH** (units per hour), and **xN parallelism** means N devices tested at once in N sockets on one load board.

Three handler families cover the industry. **Pick-and-place** handlers use a gantry with vacuum nozzles to lift devices from JEDEC trays, plunge them into sockets, then sort them into output trays by bin. They handle any flat-topped package (BGA, LGA, QFN) and are the only option for large, high-power parts; throughput is 1,000–10,000 units per hour depending on parallelism and test time. The Cohu MATRiX, Advantest M48xx series (M4841, M4872) and Hon Precision's platforms sit here, and a Blackwell GPU runs at low parallelism (x1 to x4) because each site needs a kilowatt-class thermal head. **Gravity-feed** handlers tilt tracks so that leaded or leadless packages (SOIC, TSSOP, QFN) slide from an input tube into the socket and out again; cheap, fast (10–20k UPH), small packages only. **Turret** handlers hold devices on a rotating table with 16–32 pick heads indexing past test, mark and inspection stations at 30–40k UPH, serving discretes and small QFNs (Cohu via Rasco and Ismeca, Hon Precision, Epson). Memory uses massively parallel pick-and-place with 256–1,024 sockets per insertion because DRAM pattern loops are long and only parallelism makes them economic.

Two handler numbers matter: the **index time** (dead time while the handler swaps one device for the next, typically 0.3–1 s for pick-and-place) and the **contact yield** (fraction of first insertions that make good electrical contact). Results go into numbered **bins**: bin 1 is pass, bin 2 by convention a continuity or contact failure, higher bins specific failures or lower speed grades, and the handler drops each device into the matching output tray. A failing continuity test triggers an automatic re-insert, and parts that pass on retest are logged as contact issues; test houses watch "bin 2 recovery" closely because a dirty socket can look like a 5% yield drop.

> **Worked example: cell throughput.** UPH = 3,600 × sites ÷ (test time + index time), in seconds. A GPU at x2 with a 300 s program and 1 s index gives 3,600 × 2 ÷ 301 ≈ 24 UPH, ~200,000 units per cell-year at 95% uptime (24 × 8,760 × 0.95). A microcontroller at x64 with a 1 s program and 0.5 s index gives 3,600 × 64 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 150,000 UPH. So 1 million GPUs a year needs ~5 cells for one 5-minute insertion, ~10 for a 10-minute program, plus cells for FT2 and any cold insertion: every extra minute of program costs about a cell per million units, while one microcontroller cell serves hundreds of millions of units a year.

### Sockets

The socket is the consumable at the centre of the cell. For BGA and LGA packages at 0.3–1.0 mm pitch two contact technologies dominate. **Pogo-pin** (spring-probe) sockets use a barrel, plunger and spring per ball, with 15–40 g contact force and tens of milliohms contact resistance; they last ~500,000–1,000,000 insertions and can be built down to ~0.35–0.4 mm pitch. **Elastomer** sockets use a silicone sheet loaded with vertical columns of conductive particles: gold-plated nickel particles aligned into columns by a magnetic field while the silicone cures, so each column conducts only when compressed under a ball and its neighbours stay isolated. They have very short **electrical length** (the path a signal travels through the contact, which sets how much it distorts a fast edge) and low inductance, good for high-speed lanes, but shorter life (~50,000–200,000 insertions) and more sensitivity to contamination. Smiths Interconnect, Yamaichi, Johnstech, Leeno, ISC and Enplas supply most of the market. A GPU socket with several thousand contacts, half of them power and ground, and NVLink contacts specified to 20+ GHz is a custom item costing thousands of dollars and lasting weeks in production.

Tens of milliohms per contact matters on a kilowatt part. Share 1,000 A across ~2,000 power contacts at 30 mΩ each: in parallel they are 30 mΩ ÷ 2,000 = 15 µΩ, so the droop across the socket is 1,000 A × 15 µΩ = 15 mV and the heat in the contacts is 1,000² × 15 µΩ = 15 W. Fifteen millivolts is a couple of percent of a 0.75 V rail, comparable to the Vmin **guard band** (the ~30–50 mV safety margin added to a part's measured minimum voltage before it is fused, worked through in the Vmin example below), and 15 W inside a matchbox-sized socket warms the contacts and the part; both rise as contacts wear.

> **What can go wrong: socket wear.** *Contact resistance drift*: each insertion scrubs the plunger tips; after tens of thousands the plating wears through, resistance climbs and marginal Vmin results start to fail. *Tin transfer*: solder from the balls smears onto the tips and oxidises into an insulating film; the symptom is a rising bin-2 rate with high retest recovery, which a bad lot would not show. *Debris*: flux residue, tray fibres and broken plungers make a few pins read open on every part until cleaned. *The cure*: cleaning every few thousand insertions (the cadence set per product from the bin-2 trend), plunger replacement at the rated count, and spare sockets on the shelf at thousands of dollars each so a cell never waits. The tell-tale is site correlation: a fall-off on one site of an x2 board that recovers on re-insert is the socket; on both sites, not recovering, it is the lot.

### Thermal control

Final test runs at temperature, and for high-power parts the temperature that matters is Tj, not the ambient. The chip measures its own Tj through **thermal diodes**. A **bias** is a steady DC voltage applied to a device to set its operating state, and a diode is **forward-biased** when that voltage is applied in the direction it conducts; a forward-biased diode's voltage falls ~2 mV per degree of temperature rise, so a few diodes across the die report where the silicon actually is. As Tj rises, leakage rises roughly exponentially and drive current falls (carriers scatter more in a hotter lattice, Module 11), so the chip burns more power and runs slower: a chip that meets 2 GHz at 60 °C may need ~30 mV more at 85 °C (~). Two binning terms depend on this: **Vmin** is the lowest supply voltage at which the part passes its patterns at a given frequency and Tj, and **Fmax** the highest frequency at which it passes at a given voltage and Tj.

The classic method is **tri-temp**: the handler soaks devices at cold (−40 or −55 °C), room and hot (85–125 °C, up to 155 °C for **automotive grade 0**, the harshest temperature grade of **AEC-Q100**, the Automotive Electronics Council's qualification standard, rated to 150 °C ambient; the grades are tabulated under Reliability Qualification below) before insertion, with dry air or nitrogen purge against condensation on the cold side. That works for a few watts. A GPU dissipating 500–1,000 W would heat itself by tens of degrees in a second, so modern cells use **active thermal control (ATC)** heads: a liquid- or refrigerant-cooled plate pressed onto the lid through a compliant interface, a heater embedded in the plate, and a controller that reads the on-chip thermal diodes and modulates heating and cooling to hold Tj at set point through the program. A lid-to-plate path of ~0.05 °C/W with 1 kW through it puts the die 1 kW × 0.05 °C/W = 50 °C above the plate, so a Tj target of 85 °C needs a plate near 35 °C during the heaviest block and near 85 °C during an idle one; the die heats in milliseconds, too fast to follow the diode, so the program tells the controller which block comes next and it pre-cools or pre-heats. Cohu's T-Core Rev20200530 datasheet specifies up to 800 W dissipation, ±1 °C control accuracy at the heater and a ramp up to 100 °C/s; junction accuracy also depends on the thermal interface and feedback mode; Advantest's ATC 2.0 on the M4841 runs up to 16 sites in parallel; Hon Precision's liquid-cooled ATC heads for GPU test are rated at 1,000 W (ATC3.3) and 2,000 W (ATC3.5) of device dissipation. The head also makes binning meaningful: Vmin and Fmax must be measured at a defined Tj because leakage and transistor speed both shift with it, and a **speed grade** (the clock the part is sold as guaranteeing) assigned at an uncontrolled 60 °C will not hold at 85 °C in a server.

**Cold test.** The flow table says "hot and/or cold" because the cold insertion is the least standard part of a GPU program, and NVIDIA does not publish whether Blackwell gets one (~). What cold finds is worth stating, because it is more than the hot-carrier paths named in the burn-in section below. First, **hold-time races**: a flip-flop needs its input to stay steady for a moment after the clock edge, and at cold the logic between two flip-flops runs faster (carriers scatter less in a cold lattice, Module 11), so a signal can reach the next flip-flop before it has finished capturing the previous value. A slow path can be rescued by more voltage; a race gets worse with more voltage, so it must be found and the part failed. Second, **temperature inversion**: on 5 nm-class processes at a supply only a few hundred millivolts above the **threshold voltage** (the gate voltage at which a transistor starts to conduct), cooling the die raises that threshold, and the loss of drive outweighs the faster carriers, so the same path is slower at cold than at hot. The limiting corner of the Vmin search is therefore sometimes the cold one, and a voltage fused from a hot-only search can be wrong at a cold boot. Third, the **opposite mechanical shear**: cooling contracts the organic substrate more than the silicon, pulling on the joints that heating compressed, so a bump or microbump that touches at hot can open at cold, and the analog circuits (the bandgap reference that sets every on-chip voltage, PLL lock ranges, SerDes receivers) sit at their other design corner.

What cold costs on a kilowatt part is what makes it optional. A data-centre GPU never sees −40 °C: it boots in a 15–35 °C room and runs at Tj 40–85 °C, so its cold corner is a cold boot at room temperature or a chilled plate at ~0 °C-class temperatures (~), not the −40 or −55 °C an automotive or military part must survive. And a chilled plate can hold a die cold only while the die dissipates little: with 1 kW through ~0.05 °C/W the die sits 50 °C above the plate, so a −40 °C Tj at full load would need a −90 °C plate, beyond a refrigerated head (Cohu's T-Core ranges to −55 °C at the plate). The cold blocks are therefore the low-activity ones, scan, leakage, hold-time patterns and the Vmin point at a slow pattern rate, run either in the same insertion by stepping the head down or in a separate insertion on a handler whose test chamber is sealed and purged with dry nitrogen so that the dew point stays below the plate temperature and no frost forms on the socket to short the balls. Soaking a package of ~100 g of copper lid, laminate and silicon (~) to a cold set point takes tens of seconds to minutes, which is why the throughput example above counts "any cold insertion" as extra cells, and the part must warm back above the dew point before it meets room air. Automotive parts, whose contracts require it, get a full −40 °C insertion as a matter of course; a data-centre GPU gets whatever its field data says it needs.

## Final Test: What the Program Does and What It Costs

A final-test program is a negotiated list, not a fixed checklist: every block costs seconds on a machine that costs hundreds of dollars an hour, and every block must earn those seconds by owning a class of defect that nothing cheaper catches. The design-for-test (DFT) team contributes the structural blocks, the product engineers the binning and interface blocks, the firmware team the boot and telemetry checks, and a program manager arbitrates test time against escape rate for the life of the product. The program is written in the tester vendor's environment (Advantest's SmarTest for the V93000, Teradyne's IG-XL for the UltraFLEX) and released once it gives the same answers on several testers. For a large GPU it runs to a few hundred blocks, in roughly this order.

1. **Continuity and leakage.** *Asks*: is every pin connected and none shorted? *How*: every pin carries an **electrostatic discharge (ESD) protection diode** to each supply rail (a diode conducts one way and blocks the other; these dump a static shock into the rail instead of the transistors). With the rails at 0 V, force ~100 µA out of a pin: the diode forward-biases and the pin reads ~0.7 V, proving the socket contact and the package wiring; an open pin drives the tester to its voltage limit; a pin shorted to the rail reads ~0 V. Then each pin's leakage is read in nanoamps at nominal rails. *Number*: about one second for several thousand pins. *Owns*: contact problems, cracked bumps, gross shorts; a fail triggers the handler's re-insert.
2. **Power-up and IDDQ.** *Asks*: does the chip come up in order and draw the right current when idle? *How*: rails are brought up in sequence with current ramps compared against expected profiles, then **IDDQ**, the quiescent supply current with clocks stopped and every node settled, is read at several logic states. On micrometre-era processes IDDQ was microamps, so a bridging defect drawing milliamps stood out against a single limit; on a 5 nm-class die at hot, leakage is tens of amps and a defect's milliamps vanish into it, so IDDQ is an **outlier screen**: each unit is compared with its wafer or lot population and with its own readings at other states, and a unit far from its neighbours fails though no absolute limit is violated. *Owns*: bridging defects and sequencing faults; the value is stored for FT2.
3. **Structural test: scan and logic BIST.** *Asks*: is the logic still correct at full power and temperature? *How*: **compressed scan patterns** (Module 14: an on-chip decompressor expands a short stream from the tester into the full pattern for millions of flip-flops, and a compactor squeezes the responses back to a few bits, so the tester moves tens to hundreds of times (~) fewer bits than the raw patterns contain) for **stuck-at faults** (a node held at 0 or 1), **transition faults** (a node that switches too slowly for the clock) and **small-delay faults** (a path slower than designed by a fraction of a cycle, found by launching and capturing at speed); **logic BIST (LBIST)** generates pseudo-random patterns on chip and compresses the responses into a signature. FT re-runs a subset of sort's patterns because IR drop and heat change which marginal paths fail. *Number*: tens of seconds (~). *Owns*: assembly damage to the die (a cracked low-k corner) and paths that fail only under real power delivery.
4. **Memory BIST with repair re-verification.** *Asks*: does every **SRAM** (static random-access memory, the fast on-chip memory used for caches and register files) still work, including those repaired at sort? *How*: each of thousands of SRAM instances is written and read by its own BIST engine with **March patterns** (Module 14: sequences that march through every address writing and reading 0s and 1s in both directions, so a cell that is stuck, coupled to its neighbour or slow to hold a value is exposed), and the sort-time repairs are re-verified because a cell marginal at sort may have degraded through reflow. *Number*: seconds. *Owns*: SRAM cells and the repair map.
5. **HBM and die-to-die interface.** *Asks*: do all eight stacks and the second die talk cleanly at speed and hot? *How*: link training, then per-lane **margining** (shifting each lane's sampling time and voltage threshold until it fails, to measure how much margin it has rather than only whether it works), HBM MBIST through the base die, full-array patterns at hot, NV-HBI training and loopback. *Number*: minutes, the largest block on a GPU program. *Owns*: microbump opens, interposer wiring defects, HBM stack escapes.
6. **SerDes.** *Asks*: do NVLink and PCIe meet their eye and error-rate targets? *How*: **PLL** lock first (**phase-locked loop**, the on-chip circuit that multiplies a reference clock to the gigahertz clocks the SerDes needs and holds its phase), then per-lane loopback at rate with BER and eye margin. *Number*: 18 links at 200 Gb/s per lane; BER ~10⁻¹² or better. *Owns*: transmitter and receiver defects, damaged package traces.
7. **Functional and performance binning: Vmin and Fmax search.** *Asks*: at what voltage and frequency does this unit work? *How*: a compact pattern set runs at several voltage-frequency points; the **Vmin search** finds the lowest passing voltage at the target frequency and the **Fmax search** the highest passing frequency at a target voltage, each at controlled Tj. *Number*: the worked example below. *Owns*: nothing defective, but it is where most die-to-die variation in shipped product is decided.
8. **Fuse programming, analog and thermal.** *Asks*: is the result written into the part, and are its sensors honest? *How*: the ECID, fused voltage, speed grade and disabled-**SM** configuration (**streaming multiprocessor**, the GPU's repeated compute block; dies with a few dead SMs are sold as a lower tier, Module 14) are burned and read back; the thermal sensors are calibrated against the ATC head's known Tj; the on-chip regulator telemetry (circuits reporting voltage and current to the driver) and clock generators are checked. *Number*: seconds. *Owns*: a part that lies to its driver.

> **Worked example: a Vmin search.** The program runs a 2 s pattern set at the target frequency and a fixed Tj of 85 °C, starting at 0.90 V. A linear sweep down in 10 mV steps would take 18 runs (36 s) to reach 0.72 V; a binary search over the 0.60–0.90 V window needs about five runs (~10 s), converging on a last pass at 0.73 V and a first fail at 0.72 V. The program adds a **guard band** of ~40 mV (~30–50 mV is typical) for ageing, tester-to-tester error and socket droop, and fuses 0.76 V as the operating voltage. Dynamic power scales with the square of voltage (a 1% change in V is a 2% change in V²), so each 10 mV at 0.76 V is 2 × 10/760 ≈ 2.6% of dynamic power, ~25 W on a 1 kW GPU: a guard band 20 mV wider than needed costs ~50 W per GPU for life, and one 20 mV too narrow ships parts that fail after a year of ageing. That is why Vmin is measured at controlled Tj with a pattern set chosen for its worst-case paths, and why the guard band is revisited as field data accumulates.

> **Worked example: tester cost per unit.** A loaded V93000 with high-speed instruments costs on the order of $100–300 per hour to own and operate (~). A GPU spending 10 minutes across its two FT insertions at x2 parallelism occupies the tester for 10 ÷ 2 = 5 minutes per unit, 1/12 of an hour: $100–300 ÷ 12 ≈ $8–25. At x1 it is 10 minutes, 1/6 of an hour, $17–50. For a package that costs ~$3,000 to build and sells as a ~$30,000 module, either is trivial. For a $3 microcontroller the same arithmetic forces test time under one second and parallelism above x64 (Module 14): 1 s ÷ 64 sites ≈ 16 ms per unit, and $200 per hour × 16 ms ÷ 3,600 s ≈ $0.001, a tenth of a cent.

> **Why it matters downstream: the fused Vmin and Fmax.** The 0.76 V fused above is an instruction. When the GPU is soldered onto its SXM module (Module 19), the module's **voltage regulator module (VRM)**, the converters that turn the board's 12 or 48 V into the sub-volt core rail, reads that fuse through the driver and sets its output to it, so every unit runs at its own voltage. Summed over 72 GPUs, those fuses set the rack's real draw against its budgeted 120 kW-class supply and cooling; a population fused 20 mV high is a rack that throttles. The fused Fmax decides the speed grade the part is sold as, which is what a hyperscaler's contract specifies and its schedulers assume.

## Burn-in: Physics and Practice

### The bathtub curve and infant mortality

> **Intuition: light bulbs and new cars.** Of a batch of new light bulbs, the ones that fail mostly do so in the first days, from a manufacturing flaw such as a thin spot in the filament; then almost none fail for years; then they all start to go as the filaments wear out. New cars behave the same way, which is why the first thousand miles find most assembly faults. A chip that has run for a few days has proved it is not one of the early failures; burn-in compresses those days into hours.

Semiconductor failure rate versus time follows the **bathtub curve**: a declining **infant mortality** region, a long flat region of low constant failure rate, and a rising **wear-out** region. The infant-mortality population is parts that passed test but carry a latent defect one stress cycle from failure: a thin spot in a **gate oxide** (the ~1–2 nm insulator under every transistor gate) not yet broken down; a **via** (the vertical metal plug joining one wiring layer to the next) with a void across 80% of its section, which still conducts today because 20% of the cross-section is left; a contact that conducts at room temperature but opens after a few thousand thermal cycles; a particle bridging two lines with a resistive short that has not yet electromigrated into a hard one; a **TSV** (the copper column through an HBM die) with a seam in its copper fill. Structural test cannot see these because the circuit still works. **Burn-in** deliberately ages the part through the infant-mortality region before shipment, so that parts which would have failed in the customer's first months fail in the oven instead; the price is a small fraction of everyone else's useful life, a tiny number of good parts damaged by the stress, and the oven, board and time.

### Acceleration

Burn-in tries to reveal weak parts before shipment by making selected failure mechanisms progress faster. Heat and electrical stress help atoms or trapped charges make movements that would happen more slowly in normal use. The calculation below estimates that speed-up; it does not turn the oven into a universal clock for every possible failure. A defect grows when an atom or a trapped charge hops over an energy barrier; the fraction of attempts with enough thermal energy to clear a barrier of height Ea is the **Boltzmann factor** exp(−Ea/kT), where k is Boltzmann's constant and T the absolute temperature, so kT is the typical thermal energy of an atom. Energies are in **electron-volts (eV)**, the energy an electron gains falling through one volt: kT is 0.026 eV at 25 °C and 0.034 eV at 125 °C, and because a typical defect barrier of ~0.7 eV is 20–27 times kT, a modest change in T moves the rate a great deal.

> **Intuition: why a small temperature change gives a large acceleration.** Think of the barrier as a wall and the thermal energy as how high the atoms jump on average. Going from 55 °C to 125 °C raises the average jump by only ~20%, but the atoms that clear a wall 20 times higher than the average jump are the far tail of the distribution, and a 20% higher average lifts that tail by a factor of tens. A temperature the part survives for a day therefore makes a defect age as it would in months.

The temperature dependence follows the **Arrhenius relation**: the rate of a process scales as exp(−Ea / kT), where Ea is the **activation energy** in electron-volts, k is Boltzmann's constant (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K) and T is the absolute junction temperature. The **acceleration factor** between a stress temperature and a use temperature is

AF_T = exp[ (Ea / k) × (1/T_use − 1/T_stress) ],

Here T_use is the temperature during ordinary operation and T_stress is the higher temperature during burn-in; both must be in kelvin. AF_T is the ratio of the modeled defect-growth rates. If that ratio is large, the same mechanism progresses much faster in the oven. For that mechanism and those assumptions, one hour at stress corresponds to AF_T hours at use. This is not a guaranteed service life for the whole chip.

JEDEC's **JEP122** (its catalogue of failure mechanisms and the models describing them) tabulates Ea by mechanism: ~0.7 eV is the conventional value for generic defect-driven infant mortality, and it is also the value the qualification standards of a later section (JEDEC's JESD47 and the automotive AEC-Q100) assume for **HTOL**, the 1,000-hour high-temperature operating life test that qualifies a new product, described under Burn-in versus HTOL below; electromigration is ~0.9 eV; time-dependent dielectric breakdown ~0.6–0.9 eV depending on model; some contamination-driven mechanisms exceed 1.0 eV; hot-carrier injection is close to zero or even negative in Ea, which is one reason cold test exists. A single number is a compromise, and a burn-in engineer who knows the dominant defect type on a process will adjust.

> **Worked example: acceleration of 125 °C burn-in versus 55 °C use.** Take Ea = 0.7 eV, T_use = 55 °C = 328.15 K (a typical data-centre junction temperature at moderate load), T_stress = 125 °C = 398.15 K. Then 1/T_use − 1/T_stress = 0.0030474 − 0.0025116 = 5.358 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. Multiply by Ea/k = 0.7 eV ÷ (8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K) = 8,123 K: the exponent is 8,123 × 5.358 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.352, so AF_T = e^4.352 ≈ 78. Under this model, every hour in the oven equals ~78 hours of use; a 24 h burn-in is equivalent to ~1,870 h (~2.6 months) and a 48 h burn-in to ~3,740 h (~5 months) of field operation, which is comfortably past where the infant-mortality curve flattens on a mature process. If the use temperature were 85 °C instead (a hot-running GPU at full load), AF_T falls to ~10, and the same 48 h covers only ~3 weeks of field time. This is why data-centre parts are burned in longer than consumer parts, and why the burn-in specification must be written against the customer's actual operating temperature, not a generic one.

Three of the named mechanisms, one sentence each. **Hot-carrier injection (HCI)** is damage to the gate oxide near a transistor's drain by electrons accelerated to high energy in the channel; at low temperature the lattice vibrates less, electrons scatter less and reach higher energies, so HCI-sensitive paths fail cold rather than hot, which is one of the reasons a cold insertion exists (Thermal control, above). **Time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB)** is the gate oxide wearing out under a sustained electric field until it punctures. **Bias temperature instability (BTI)** is a transistor's threshold voltage (the gate voltage at which it starts to conduct, defined under Thermal control) drifting while it sits under bias at temperature, slowing it without breaking anything.

Voltage adds a second multiplier. Oxide and interconnect defects accelerate roughly exponentially with over-voltage, AF_V = exp(γ × ΔV), where ΔV is the stress voltage minus the use voltage and γ is a mechanism- and process-dependent constant with units of per volt; published models put γ at ~10–15 per volt for oxide-type defects on advanced processes (~), so each 0.1 V of over-voltage multiplies the defect-growth rate by e^1.0 to e^1.5 ≈ 2.7–4.5×. Burn-in typically runs **VDD** (the core supply voltage) 10–20% above nominal (a 0.8 V core at 0.9–0.95 V), which on published models contributes a further ~2–10× acceleration. Combined temperature and voltage acceleration of a few hundred is common, which is how the industry got from the 168 h burn-in of the 1980s to 4–48 h today. The limit is that too much stress starts to consume useful life or damages good parts through mechanisms (bias temperature instability, TDDB on thin oxides) whose Ea and γ differ from the defects being screened; a burn-in that is too hard shows up as a Vmin shift between FT1 and FT2 across the whole population, not just the fallout.

> **Worked example: voltage acceleration and the combined factor.** Take γ = 10–15 per volt as above (an order of magnitude; the real value is process-specific and rarely disclosed). With ΔV = 0.12 V (a 0.80 V rail run at 0.92 V), AF_V = e^(10 × 0.12) to e^(15 × 0.12) = e^1.2 to e^1.8 ≈ 3–6×. Combined with the thermal factor, AF ≈ 78 × 4 ≈ 300, and a 48 h burn-in equals 48 × 300 = 14,400 h ≈ 1.6 years at 55 °C use, against ~5 months from temperature alone. That product is what let 168 h become 24–48 h without losing coverage of the infant-mortality region; it is also why the voltage cannot simply be raised further, since the same exponential accelerates TDDB and BTI in the good parts.

### Static, dynamic and monitored burn-in

**Static burn-in** applies bias and temperature with no switching; it stresses oxides under a steady (DC, direct-current) voltage but leaves most nodes fixed and misses defects that need current flow (electromigration, resistive vias). **Dynamic burn-in** clocks the part and drives patterns so that nodes toggle and current flows through the interconnect; on large SoCs the patterns come from on-chip logic and memory BIST at a slow clock, or scan patterns loaded from the burn-in system, and the goal is **toggle coverage** (the fraction of internal nodes that switched at least once), not fault detection. **Monitored burn-in** (test during burn-in, TDBI) reads BIST results periodically so that each failure is time-stamped; the fallout-versus-time curve is the data that later justifies shortening the burn-in. Burn-in is not at full power, but on a large GPU it is not at low power either, and the two facts need separating. The patterns run at a fraction of nominal frequency, so the switching power that a 1 kW workload would produce shrinks to tens of watts; a GPU toggling at full rate would push its own Tj far past 125 °C in seconds, and even 10% activity would scatter Tj by tens of degrees across a board. Leakage, however, does not scale down with the clock, and at burn-in conditions it dominates. Take the tens of amps of quiescent current the IDDQ block measures at 85 °C and nominal voltage (~22 A in the FT2 delta example below): leakage roughly doubles every 10–20 °C, so 125 °C multiplies it by ~4–16, and running VDD 10–20% high raises it again, so a Blackwell-class die in the oven draws of the order of 100–300 A (~) of pure leakage at ~0.9 V, a few hundred watts (~), with its clocks barely running. That is the heat a burn-in slot must remove to hold Tj at 125 °C rather than let the thermal-runaway loop of the first section take over, and it is why the thermal envelope of a GPU slot is measured in hundreds of watts to a kilowatt even though the workload is a small fraction of TDP. For a phone SoC or a microcontroller, whose leakage at 125 °C is a few watts, the older picture of a board of sockets in hot air shedding tens of watts per device is still right. In either case per-slot thermal control (a heater plus a cold plate per socket on a GPU board, the chamber air on a low-power board) sets Tj rather than the workload.

### Boards, ovens and systems

Devices go into sockets on a **burn-in board (BIB)**, a thick multilayer PCB with 8–256 sockets and edge connectors for power and pattern signals, built on a high-**Tg** laminate (**Tg**, the **glass-transition temperature**, is where the board's resin softens; a laminate with Tg above ~170 °C survives 125–150 °C for thousands of hours where ordinary **FR-4**, the standard glass-fibre-and-epoxy board material, would warp). BIBs slide into an **oven** (forced-air chamber at 125 ± 3 °C, sometimes up to 150 °C) whose **backplane**, the fixed board at the back of the rack into which every BIB's edge connector plugs, connects them to a **burn-in system** supplying power, patterns and monitoring. Suppliers include Micro Control Company (high-power, with thermal control per **DUT**, the **device under test**; used for CPUs and GPUs), Aehr Test Systems (packaged-part systems and, distinctively, full-wafer burn-in: the FOX-XP contacts an entire 300 mm wafer and offers configurations up to 18 wafers; its March 2024 high-power photonics configuration supports nine wafers at up to 3,500 W per wafer, used for silicon carbide and silicon photonics, with a first system shipped for AI-processor wafers in early 2025), Incal, Dong-Il, Chroma and Advantest. Wafer-level burn-in suits silicon carbide and photonics when their reliability requirements justify screening every die and full-wafer contact provides better throughput than individual sockets; for a GPU the package changes the thermal and electrical environment and the defects burn-in hunts are largely assembly-induced, so packaged burn-in remains the norm. KYEC's reported upgrade of its Blackwell slots from ~600 W to ~1 kW of thermal capacity is the visible symptom of GPU power growth reaching the burn-in floor.

Picture one Blackwell-class slot, because the 8–256-socket board above is a phone-SoC picture and the 1 kW figure only makes sense against the leakage arithmetic of the previous subsection. The socket is the same kind as the final-test socket, several thousand pogo contacts of which half carry power and ground, but built from high-temperature plastics and spring alloys that do not relax over weeks at 125 °C; it sees one insertion every day or two rather than one every few minutes, so creep at temperature rather than scrub wear is what retires it. On the lid sits a thermal unit that is a slow cousin of the ATC head: a heater to bring the die to 125 °C before its own leakage does, a liquid-cooled cold plate to carry away the few hundred watts (~) the die produces once it is there, and a controller reading the on-chip thermal diodes to hold the set point, because a die whose leakage doubles every 10–20 °C cannot be left to find its own temperature. Under the board each socket has its own current-limited supply of several hundred amps (~) at ~0.9 V, so that a part failing short takes out only itself. Power delivery and cooling of that order per socket mean a GPU burn-in board carries a few to perhaps a dozen sockets (~) rather than hundreds, and the chamber's forced air sets only the ambient for the board, connectors and the lightly loaded parts; the die's Tj is set by its own leakage, the heater and the plate, which is how a liquid cold plate and a 125 °C chamber coexist. The ~600 W to ~1 kW upgrade was heavier copper through the backplane, larger cold plates and more chiller capacity per slot, not a hotter oven.

A high-power burn-in slot-hour costs on the order of $0.20–1 (oven, board amortisation, power, floor space, handling), so 24–48 h costs ~$5–50 per GPU plus the FT2 insertion: small next to the part's value and the cost of a field failure in a rack, which is why GPUs get burn-in and $3 microcontrollers mostly do not.

> **What can go wrong: in the oven.** *A device short*: a part that fails hard can pull a board's shared rail down and take every other device on it out of stress; per-socket current limiting isolates it. *A socket that opens at temperature*: a contact that relaxes at 125 °C leaves the part unpowered for 48 hours; it then "passes" FT2 because it was never stressed, the worst outcome, and the reason monitored burn-in checks every socket's current and BIST response through the run. *Condensation*: a board cooled too fast in humid air sweats, so racks cool under dry air. *Over-stress*: a population-wide Vmin shift or IDDQ rise at FT2, rather than a few outliers, means the good parts have been aged; the recipe, not the lot, is at fault. *Oven non-uniformity*: the Arrhenius exponent changes by (Ea/k) × ΔT ÷ T² for a small error ΔT in the stress temperature, and at 125 °C that is 8,123 K × 3 K ÷ (398 K)² ≈ 0.15, so a 3 °C error moves AF_T by ~15% and a corner at 122 °C delivers 15% less stress than the recipe assumes, and fallout that differs by board position points to poor airflow.

### Why some products skip burn-in

On a mature process with good data, 100% burn-in may become hard to justify: whether 0.05% fallout pays for the screen depends on screen cost, escape cost and how well the test targets field failures. The alternatives are statistical. **Part average testing (PAT)**, standardised for automotive in AEC-Q001, fails any device whose parametrics (IDDQ, Vmin, **ring-oscillator** frequency: a loop of an odd number of inverters whose oscillation frequency measures how fast that chip's transistors are) are outliers relative to its lot or wafer even though inside datasheet limits, because outliers carry a disproportionate share of latent defects. **Voltage stress at final test** applies the burn-in over-voltage for seconds rather than hours. **Wafer-level burn-in** moves the screen to where a failing die costs $350 rather than $3,000. **Early-life failure rate (ELFR)** sampling per JESD74A monitors whether the population is drifting toward needing the full screen again. The typical trajectory is 100% burn-in during ramp, a data-driven reduction from 48 to 24 to 8 h as monitored-burn-in curves flatten, and eventually a sample or PAT-only regime once field returns and ELFR agree; high-value data-centre parts rarely reach the last step because the cost of an escape is so asymmetric.

### Burn-in versus HTOL

The two are often confused. Burn-in is a production screen on 100% of units, short, and non-destructive by design. **High-temperature operating life (HTOL)**, JESD22-A108, is a qualification test on a sample (three lots of 77 units in JESD47), run for 1,000 h at 125 °C Tj, destructive in intent (parts are stressed to demonstrate the constant failure rate, then scrapped) and performed once per new product or process. Its output is a **FIT** rate (**failures in time**, failures per billion device-hours), and the constant rate it measures is the flat floor of the bathtub, after infant mortality and before wear-out. HTOL tells you the population's failure rate on that floor; burn-in removes the infant-mortality tail from each unit shipped.

## FT2: Measuring What Burn-in Changed

**FT2** re-runs a subset of the FT1 program, typically continuity, leakage, IDDQ, a short scan and MBIST block, the HBM interface and the Vmin point. The interesting output is not only pass/fail but the **delta**: a part whose IDDQ rose 30% or whose Vmin moved 20 mV during burn-in is degrading and is failed even if still within limits. Per-die deltas are possible because each package carries its ECID and the FT1 data is in the test-data database in **STDF** (**Standard Test Data Format**, the file format in which every tester logs every measurement per device, Module 14), so FT2 looks up the same unit's FT1 record before judging it.

> **Worked example: delta screening.** A unit read IDDQ = 22.0 A at FT1 and 28.6 A at FT2, +30%. The absolute limit is 35 A, so on an absolute screen it passes; against a delta limit of +20% it fails. Its Vmin moved from 0.72 V to 0.74 V, +20 mV, against a 15 mV delta limit: fail again. Why the delta is the better screen: the population's IDDQ at FT1 spans perhaps 15–33 A across a wafer's **process corners** (the extremes of transistor speed and leakage that normal manufacturing variation produces, from a fast, leaky corner to a slow, tight one), ±40% around the centre, whereas the drift of a degrading part is 20–30%. An absolute limit tight enough to catch a 30% rise on a 22 A part would fail every healthy 33 A part; one loose enough to pass them lets the 22 A part drift unnoticed. When the population spread is wider than the drift you are hunting, only a per-unit comparison sees it; and a lot in which every unit's IDDQ rose 25% is not a lot of degrading parts but an over-stressed burn-in.

> **What can go wrong: a delta that is not a delta.** FT1 and FT2 usually run on different cells. A thermal head on the FT2 cell holding Tj 3 °C high raises every unit's IDDQ by ~10–20% (leakage roughly doubling every 10–20 °C) and looks exactly like population-wide burn-in damage; a worn FT2 socket shifts every Vmin up by its extra droop. The defence is **correlation**: **golden units** (parts of known, stable behaviour whose readings on every cell are on record) run through every cell on a schedule, and a cell whose readings drift is pulled before it fails a lot. When a lot fails FT2 in bulk, the order is: run the golden units on that cell, re-test a sample on a correlated cell, and only then blame the oven.

## System-Level Test: Testing the Product, Not the Circuit

**System-level test (SLT)** asks whether the assembled product can carry out realistic sequences of work. Earlier structural tests look for specific modeled circuit faults, such as a signal stuck at one value or a transition that arrives too late. A scan program with 99% stuck-at and 90% transition-fault coverage has covered those fractions of its modeled fault lists; it has not checked those fractions of all possible programs. SLT adds evidence about interactions that are difficult to represent in those lists: **asynchronous clock-domain crossings**, where a signal passes between two clocks not locked to each other, so its timing depends on their momentary phase and differs on every run; the **power-management state machines** that switch **voltage islands** (blocks that can be powered down and back up under firmware control), where a block waking a few nanoseconds late corrupts its first transaction; the **PLLs** during frequency hops, when the clock is briefly unstable by design; the **boot ROM** (the fixed program the chip executes first, which loads its firmware from external flash) and its interaction with that flash and the driver; and every path whose timing is only exercised by a real instruction stream. Phone SoCs hit this wall first: escapes of a few hundred **DPPM** (defective parts per million shipped) that were invisible to ATE but crashed a phone on boot. Apple's response, widely reported to date from the early-to-mid 2010s (~), during the ramp of its A-series phone SoCs, was to run every SoC through an SLT insertion in which the chip boots real firmware on a fixture mimicking the phone's board and runs workloads (camera pipeline, GPU shaders, modem loopbacks, memory stress) at temperature for minutes. Apple's insistence pulled the equipment industry along: Advantest acquired Astronics Test Systems' semiconductor test business in 2019 largely for its SLT platform, Teradyne fields the Titan HP SLT handler, and Cohu, KYEC and ASE built their own high-density SLT racks.

The mechanics are unlike ATE. There is no million-dollar tester; each of hundreds of slots is a stripped-down application board, and a slot works like this:

1. **The slot board.** A small motherboard-like PCB with the product's own VRMs (so the part sees real regulator behaviour rather than a tester supply), a socket, storage where the product needs it (the GPU's HBM is already on the package), and PCIe and NVLink connectors wired to loopback or to a neighbour.
2. **Insertion.** A handler or technician places the package in the socket and a thermal head lands on the lid; the thermal problem is the same as at FT, at real workload power.
3. **Boot.** A **host controller** (a small computer per slot or group of slots) powers the rails in sequence, loads firmware over PCIe or **JTAG** (the standard four-wire debug port every chip carries, Module 14), and waits for the part to **enumerate**, that is, to announce itself on the PCIe bus with its identity and capabilities, as any plug-in card does when a computer boots.
4. **The workload sequence.** The host runs a scripted list of programs, each for a set time at a set Tj, and the part must produce known answers.
5. **Judgement.** The host compares results with expected values, reads the part's telemetry (temperature, current, error counters) and flags a fail on any mismatch, hang or excursion; the result goes into the STDF database against the ECID.

For a GPU the list is memory stress across all eight HBM stacks at several temperatures; NVLink loopback at full rate with error counting; **GEMM** (general matrix-multiply) **kernels** (a kernel is a program that runs on the GPU), the operation that dominates AI training, whose results are checked against a second computation so that a wrong answer with no error flag is caught; power-management cycling through idle, boost and throttled states; and thermal cycling between Tj set points to shake out intermittents. The whole runs 10–60 minutes per GPU; a phone SoC's list runs in minutes. Slot cost is ~$5,000–50,000, test time is long, and throughput comes from parallelism across a rack rather than speed per slot. The content is written by the product's firmware and validation teams rather than the DFT group, using the real driver stack (**CUDA**, NVIDIA's programming platform), and it is the only screen for the "boots, passes scan, hangs after 40 seconds of CUDA" class. On a mature GPU the SLT fallout is ~0.5–2%, nearly all of it parts that passed every ATE block; that number justifies the added insertion.

> **Worked example: SLT economics.** A rack of 100 slots on a 30-minute recipe turns each slot over twice an hour; at 90% uptime it delivers 100 × 2 × 0.9 = 180 UPH, ~1.5 million units per rack-year. One million GPUs a year needs two-thirds of a rack of pure slot-hours, but a real floor runs 3–5 racks: lots arrive in parallel, fails are re-tested, slots are always out for correlation or repair, and recipes stretch toward 60 minutes on new products. At ~$1–3 million per rack over three years, 3–5 racks over 3 million units come to ~$1–5 of capital per unit, about double for a 60-minute recipe, so ~$1–10 across the 10–60 minute range. Add power at real workload (~0.5 kWh per unit, a few cents), cooling, labour, slot boards consumed at $5,000–50,000 each, floor space and the test house's margin, and the service price lands at the ~$10–30 per unit in the cost stack below.

> **What can go wrong: SLT's own false fails and escapes.** *Marginal slot boards*: a slot with a noisy VRM or a worn socket fails good parts, and cheap boards drift individually, so every slot is run against golden units on a schedule and a slot whose fail rate departs from the rack average is pulled. *Non-deterministic workloads*: a kernel whose timing depends on the operating system or thread scheduling gives different results run to run, so recipes fix their seeds and precompute their answers. *Thermal drift*: a head holding Tj 5 °C off set point changes which marginal parts fail. *SLT's own escapes*: a part that passes a 30-minute recipe and hangs at hour six of a training job needs longer stress or a workload the recipe lacks; that class is why rack-level burn-in of days still exists downstream and why SLT recipes grow from field data rather than from the fault list.

## Outgoing QA and Shipment

**Outgoing quality assurance (OQA)** closes the flow. This section, like the incoming inspection at the head of the flow, is a side note: a list of mechanical, statistical and paperwork checks that catch gross damage, mislabelling and moisture rather than the electrical failures the rest of the module is about, so a couple of paragraphs is what it warrants. A sample per lot is re-tested on ATE under an **acceptable quality level (AQL)** plan: ANSI/ASQ Z1.4 tables give, for a lot size and target quality level, how many units to pull and how many failures reject the lot (a lot of 1,000 packages might have 80 pulled and be rejected on one failure, ~; the plan is the customer's). The sample is inspected visually (ball coplanarity to ~0.1 mm, marking legibility) and sometimes X-rayed, and the lot documentation links every unit's ECID to its wafer, sort, FT1, BI, FT2 and SLT records.

Parts ship in JEDEC trays with humidity indicator cards in **moisture-barrier bags** (foil laminate sealed with desiccant), because a large BGA at **moisture sensitivity level (MSL)** 3 or 4 that absorbs atmospheric moisture will "popcorn" at the module assembler's reflow: the substrate and underfill soak up water over days, at reflow that water flashes to steam at ~250 °C, and the pressure delaminates the die from the substrate or cracks the mould, sometimes audibly. The MSL scale (JEDEC's J-STD-020) rates how long a package may sit open at 30 °C and 60% relative humidity before it must be baked dry: level 1 unlimited, level 2 one year, level 2a four weeks, level 3 168 hours, level 4 72 hours, level 5 48 hours, level 5a 24 hours, level 6 bake before use. A package whose **floor life** has expired is baked at ~125 °C for up to 24 hours to drive the water out.

> **Why it matters downstream: MSL floor life versus the module assembler's reflow.** A tray of GPUs at MSL 3 opened on Monday at Foxconn has 168 hours before it must be baked or scrapped; if the SXM line stalls a week on a substrate shortage, every open tray needs a bake cycle and the schedule slips a day. The assembler opens bags per shift, tracks each tray's clock and keeps a bake-oven queue; the test house's part is to ship the bag correctly dried with the humidity card that proves it, because a popcorned package on the SXM line is a $3,000 loss no later test recovers.

## Reliability Qualification: JESD47 and AEC-Q100

Qualification happens once per product and process, on samples, before volume shipment, and is the evidence that the flow above can be trusted. The two governing documents are JEDEC **JESD47** (stress-test-driven qualification of integrated circuits, for commercial and data-centre parts) and **AEC-Q100** (the Automotive Electronics Council's stress qualification, with temperature grades 0 through 3: grade 1 is −40 to +125 °C ambient, grade 0 is −40 to +150 °C). The stress tests, with their JEDEC method numbers, are:

| Test | Method | Conditions | What it screens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preconditioning | J-STD-020 / JESD22-A113 | Moisture soak to MSL rating, then 3× reflow at 260 °C peak | Simulates board assembly before every other package stress |
| High-temperature operating life (HTOL) | JESD22-A108 | 1,000 h, 125 °C Tj, VDD max, dynamic; 3 lots × 77 units, 0 fails | Constant failure rate (FIT) |
| Temperature cycling (TC) | JESD22-A104 | −55/+125 °C or −40/+125 °C, 500–1,000 cycles | Bump, underfill, lid and substrate fatigue |
| Highly accelerated stress test, biased (HAST) | JESD22-A110 | 130 °C, 85% relative humidity (RH), 96 h, biased | Moisture-driven corrosion, ionic contamination |
| Unbiased HAST (uHAST) | JESD22-A118 | 110 °C, 85% RH, 264 h | Delamination, moisture ingress |
| Temperature-humidity bias (THB) | JESD22-A101 | 85 °C, 85% RH, 1,000 h, biased | Same, slower |
| High-temperature storage life (HTSL) | JESD22-A103 | 150 °C, 1,000 h, unbiased | Intermetallic growth, die-attach |
| ESD, human-body model (also abbreviated HBM, an unfortunate collision with high-bandwidth memory) | ANSI/ESDA/JEDEC JS-001 | Class 2 = 2 kV, though 1 kV is common on large SoCs | Handling damage |
| ESD, charged-device model (CDM) | JS-002 | 250–500 V | Automated-handling damage |
| Latch-up | JESD78 | ±100 mA injection, 1.5× VDD overvoltage | Parasitic thyristor triggering |
| Early life failure rate (ELFR) | JESD74A | Short burn-in on a large sample (e.g. 800+ units), post-test | Infant-mortality rate; burn-in decision |

One sentence of physics per stress:

- **Temperature cycling** repeats the CTE-mismatch shear of reflow hundreds of times, fatiguing solder joints and cracking underfill and lid adhesive the way a paperclip fails when bent back and forth.
- **HAST and THB** drive water into the package: at 130 °C and 85% humidity the vapour pressure forces moisture through mould and substrate in days rather than months, and under bias dissolved ions carry current and corrode the metal.
- **HTSL** grows **intermetallics**: metal pairs in contact, copper and tin at the bumps or gold and aluminium at **wire bonds** (the hair-thin gold wires that connect a die to its leadframe in older packages, Module 16), interdiffuse into brittle compounds that crack under the next thermal shock.
- **ESD** tests reproduce two ways a chip gets a static shock: the human-body model discharges a capacitor through a resistor to imitate a person touching a pin; the charged-device model imitates a package that has itself picked up charge on a handler and discharges through one pin, a faster, sharper pulse.
- **Latch-up** exploits a flaw built into every **CMOS** chip (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, the pairing of n-type and p-type transistors every digital chip is built from, Module 11): the n-well and p-substrate (the n-doped pockets and the p-doped wafer the two transistor types sit in, Module 10) form a parasitic **PNPN** sandwich, and a large enough current spike into a pin turns it on like a **thyristor** (a switch that stays on once triggered), shorting supply to ground until power is removed.

For a CoWoS package the package-level stresses (temperature cycling, HAST, HTSL) are where qualification fails most often: the 2.5D structure with an 800 mm²-class die, silicon interposer and 70+ mm organic substrate has a CTE stack that no earlier package had, and early CoWoS qualifications were dominated by interposer cracking and underfill delamination that were fixed by substrate core changes and underfill reformulation.

The FIT arithmetic below rests on one piece of statistics. If failures arrive at random at a true rate λ, the count in T device-hours follows the **Poisson distribution**, and the probability of seeing none is e^(−λT). A clean HTOL does not prove λ = 0; it says λ is probably not so large that zero failures would have been a fluke. The 60%-confidence upper bound is the λ for which zero failures had only a 40% chance: e^(−λT) = 0.4, so λT = −ln 0.4 = 0.916. JESD85 expresses this as a chi-squared value; the two are the same number.

> **Worked example: converting an HTOL result into a FIT rate.** Use the concrete qualification matrix reported for TI's AFE10004-EP: 3 lots × 77 units = 231 units, 1,000 h at 125 °C and zero failures. Other qualifications can use different matrices. With the AF_T of ~78 from the previous example (0.7 eV, 55 °C use), that is 231 × 1,000 × 78 ≈ 18.0 million equivalent device-hours at use temperature. JESD85 computes the upper-bound failure rate at a 60% confidence level from the chi-squared distribution, with 2 × (failures + 1) **degrees of freedom** (the parameter that selects which member of the chi-squared family applies; 2 for zero failures): χ²(0.60, 2 degrees of freedom) / 2 = 0.916, the same −ln 0.4 as above, so λ ≤ 0.916 / 18.0 × 10⁶ h ≈ 5.1 × 10⁻⁸ per hour, or ~51 **FIT** (failures in 10⁹ device-hours). At 90% confidence (χ²/2 = 2.30) it is ~128 FIT. If the same constant-rate model applied to 16,384 devices, its upper bound would correspond to 16,384 × 51 × 10⁻⁹ × 8,760 ≈ 7 modeled failures per year. This is neither a predicted measured rate nor a lower bound, and extrapolation to a complete GPU requires evidence that the tested mechanisms, operating stresses and population match.

## HBM: Known-Good Die, Known-Good Stack and the 2.5D Problem

HBM is the part of the package where test is hardest and the cost of an escape is highest, and it is why "known-good" appears so often in this course: a stack is a dozen dies that can never be separated, on an interposer from which it can never be removed, so every test skipped early becomes a scrapped assembly later.

**Known-good die (KGD) before stacking.** Each DRAM die in a 12- or 16-high stack must be nearly perfect before it is committed, because stack yield compounds: if each already-selected die has 99% probability of being defect-free at assembly, independent die quality gives 0.99¹² ≈ 89% for 12 dies and about 85% for 16, before base-die and bond losses. This probability is not the fraction of raw dies that passed wafer sort; rejected dies are excluded before stacking. DRAM sort therefore includes full-array patterns at hot and cold, retention testing (every cell must hold its charge for the refresh interval at 85–95 °C) and **row and column redundancy repair**, in which failing addresses are remapped to spare rows and columns by blowing **anti-fuses** (tiny insulators deliberately broken down by a voltage pulse to become permanent connections); post-repair testing selects KGD; test coverage determines the residual escape risk among selected dies. Because the TSVs are revealed and the wafer thinned to ~30 µm (Module 15) after the front end (**TSV reveal**: grinding the wafer's back away until the copper ends of the TSVs poke out), the wafer is probed twice: on the front side before thinning, and on the microbump side after TSV reveal, on a **temporary carrier** (a glass wafer glued to the thinned wafer so that something 30 µm thick can be handled), with probe cards that must contact ~25–40 µm microbumps without damaging them.

> **Worked example: redundancy repair and known-good die (illustrative).** Suppose the process leaves an average of ~20 killer defects in a DRAM die's cell array, each taking out one row or column. Without repair the defect-free fraction would be the Poisson zero term e^(−20) ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁹: no die would work, which is why DRAM has shipped with redundancy for forty years. Give the die 256 independent repair regions, each with 8 spare rows and 8 spare columns: twenty defects over 256 regions is ~0.08 per region, and the chance that any region collects more than 8 is negligible (the Poisson tail beyond 8 at a mean of 0.08 is below 10⁻¹²), so repair recovers effectively every die whose defects fall in the array. What sets KGD is the unrepairable remainder: a defect in the shared periphery (the row decoders and sense amplifiers that select and read out the cells, Module 15, the TSV field, the interface logic), which has no spares and occupies a few percent of the area, or a cluster that exhausts one region. If unrepairable defects affect 0.5% of raw dies and testing detects them, those dies are discarded; the remaining selected dies need not have a0.5% escape rate. Separately, assume the selected population has a measured residual defect probability of0.5% rather than1%. Its independent die-quality contribution to a12-high stack changes from0.99¹²≈89% to0.995¹²≈94%. This comparison illustrates the value of reducing escapes, not a claim that raw wafer yield can be multiplied again after perfect sorting.

**Stack test through the base die.** Once stacked, the individual DRAM dies are no longer accessible to a probe. The JEDEC HBM standard (JESD235 for HBM2/2E, JESD238 for HBM3) solves this by putting test infrastructure into the **base die** (the logic die at the bottom of the stack, built on a DRAM-vendor logic process for HBM3 and increasingly on a foundry logic process, TSMC N12 or N5-class, for HBM4). Two mechanisms are standardised. An **IEEE 1500** wrapper is a standard serial test port: a shift-register chain around each die through which a tester can, with a handful of pins, put any die in the stack into test mode, start its built-in self-test, read the result and burn its repair fuses; think of it as the JTAG debug port of the SLT section replicated for each die in the stack. A **direct access (DA) port** exposes a reduced-width test interface on the base die's microbumps so that the stack's full memory array can be tested with a conventional memory tester through a few hundred pads rather than the 1,024-bit-wide interface, which only exists once the stack is mounted on an interposer. The base die also contains MBIST engines, lane-repair logic for the wide interface and **TSV repair**: each group of signal TSVs carries spares, and a TSV found open or resistive at stack test is replaced by remapping to a spare through a **multiplexer** network (a multiplexer is a digital switch that routes one of several inputs to an output under a select signal). HBM4's move to a 2,048-bit interface and a foundry-built base die expands this test logic further and, on some implementations, lets the base die run memory-controller-like test traffic autonomously.

Stack test is done on the memory vendor's own ATE (Advantest T5503/T5800-class memory testers, high-parallelism handlers) at hot and cold, followed by a stack-level burn-in, and the result is a **known-good stack (KGS)** shipped to TSMC or the OSAT. Vendors do not publish their recipes; trade and conference descriptions put the stack test at tens of minutes of pattern time per stack across hot and cold insertions (~), the stack burn-in at the order of a day (~), and the whole at a few days of elapsed time per lot.

**The 2.5D known-good-stack problem.** A 36 GB, 12-high HBM3E stack is estimated at ~$500–700 in the 2025 pricing scenario of Module 15; lower-capacity stacks cost less. Once placed on the CoWoS interposer beside a GPU die and underfilled it cannot be removed (rework of a microbumped, underfilled die is impractical, and a future **hybrid-bonded** stack, whose dies are joined copper-to-copper with no solder (Module 17), would be still harder to rework; hybrid bonding is not required by HBM4), so a stack that fails after assembly scraps or downgrades the whole ~$5,000–7,000 Blackwell-class package. The memory vendor must therefore ship at 99.9%-class quality on an interface, the 1,024-bit bus at 8+ Gb/s, that it cannot fully test at speed until the stack is on an interposer, using the DA port and IEEE 1500 as proxies. TSMC mitigates with an intermediate probe after the **chip-on-wafer step** (the CoWoS stage at which dies and stacks are bonded to the interposer wafer but the interposer is not yet cut and mounted on a substrate, Module 17), before a substrate and lid are consumed. Even so, HBM-attributed fallout at package test is a standing negotiation between GPU vendor, foundry and memory vendor over who pays for the scrapped package; it is why HBM qualification at NVIDIA takes months and why Samsung's HBM3E struggled to qualify through 2024 (stack-level thermal and yield issues; its 12-high HBM3E finally passed NVIDIA's qualification in September 2025, roughly 18 months after development was completed).

> **What can go wrong: the escape-cost negotiation.** The argument over test content is an expected-value calculation. If a stack escapes its vendor's test with probability P and fails on the interposer, the expected loss per stack is P × package value: at P = 0.1% and a $3,000 package, $3 per stack; at 1%, $30, a tenth or more of the stack's price. Against that the vendor weighs a longer stack test and burn-in at the order of a dollar or two per stack (~). The GPU vendor's ledger is worse than it looks: eight stacks per package turn P = 0.1% per stack into 0.8% of packages, each of which has also consumed the interposer, substrate, lid, assembly slot and two good GPU dies. The result is contracts that specify test coverage and burn-in duration rather than only a DPPM number, a shared database tracing every scrapped package's stack IDs to the vendor's lot, and qualification programs that run for months before a new stack is allowed near a production interposer.

## Yield Fallout Along the Chain

Every stage after the fab removes some units, and because the unit's value rises at every step the late losses cost more than the early ones. The first row's value takes one line: with D0 ≈ 0.1 defects/cm² and a die of A = 8 cm², Poisson yield is Y = e^(−D0·A) = e^(−0.8) ≈ 0.45 (Module 13). A 300 mm wafer holds ~65 gross dies of that size, so a ~$17k wafer costs ~$17,000 ÷ 65 ≈ $260 per gross die and, after harvesting lifts the usable fraction to 60–80%, ~$350–400 per good die. **Harvesting** means selling a die with a few dead SMs as a lower-tier product rather than scrapping it. The figures below for a large AI accelerator on a mature process are illustrative ranges, not published data; the value column is build cost for units that are scrapped and selling price for the last row, whose assemblies are repaired rather than scrapped.

| Stage | Typical yield (illustrative) | Value at risk per unit | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wafer sort (after harvesting) | 60–80% | ~$350 (die share of a ~$17k wafer) | Poisson yield on 800 mm² is ~45%; harvesting (disabling bad SMs) recovers it |
| HBM incoming (KGS) | 99.5–99.9% | ~$200 per stack | Memory vendor's outgoing quality |
| CoWoS assembly | 97–99% | ~$2,500–3,500 | Chip-on-wafer, wafer-on-substrate, lid; interposer and bump losses |
| FT1 | 95–98% | ~$2,500–3,500 | Interface, bumps, full-power fails |
| Burn-in + FT2 | 99–99.8% | Same | Infant mortality |
| SLT | 98–99.5% | Same | Firmware/workload escapes |
| Module test (SXM) | 98–99.5% | ~$3,500–5,000 with PCB and VRMs | Handoff to Module 19 |
| Board and rack test | 99%+ per GPU | $200k–4M per assembly | Failures are repaired, not scrapped |

> **Worked example: compounded yield through five back-end stages.** This is an illustrative $3,000-package model, not a Blackwell BOM or disclosed NVIDIA yield.  Start with 1,000 die that passed sort. Assembly yield 98% leaves 980 packages. FT1 at 96% leaves 941. Burn-in plus FT2 at 99.2% leaves 933. SLT at 98.5% leaves 919. Module assembly and test at 99% leaves 910 SXM modules. The cumulative back-end yield is 0.98 × 0.96 × 0.992 × 0.985 × 0.99 = 0.910, so 9% of sorted die never become a shipped module. Now weight by value. The 20 assembly losses cost the die plus HBM plus interposer, ~$2,000 each if the substrate has not yet been consumed: $40k. The 39 FT1 losses at ~$3,000 each: $117k. The 8 BI/FT2 losses: $24k. The 14 SLT losses: $42k. The 9 module losses at ~$4,000: $36k. Total ~$260k on 910 shipped modules, or ~$285 per good module, which is roughly what the GPU die itself cost and several times the ~$50–150 per unit spent on final test, burn-in and SLT. A one-point improvement in FT1 yield (96 to 97%) is worth ~$30 per shipped unit; on a million units a year that is $30 million, which is why yield engineering continues long after the process is "mature". Contrast with sort, where the 250–300 die lost per 1,000 gross cost only ~$350 each: front-end yield loss is larger in count, back-end loss is larger in dollars per unit.

## DPPM and What the Field Actually Sees

**DPPM** (defective parts per million) is the outgoing quality metric the customer sees: of a million shipped parts, how many are defective on arrival or fail within warranty. Consumer logic ships at a few hundred to ~1,000 DPPM. That number follows from two things the earlier modules gave you: escapes scale with the fraction of real defects the fault models do not cover, weighted by how many defective die there were to begin with, so a 99%-coverage program on a 50%-yield product still ships defectives at the parts-per-thousand level (the Williams-Brown relation of Module 14, whose "~500 to a few thousand" DPPM for consumer parts is the figure at sort, before final test and SLT mop up); automotive customers demand single-digit DPPM and increasingly write "zero defects" into contracts, which in practice means <1–10 DPPM with a corrective-action process for every return; data-centre GPUs sit in between on paper, but the effective requirement is set by fleet economics rather than a contractual number.

A fleet report measures operational interruptions, which are different from unique failed devices. Meta's [Llama 3 paper, section 3.3.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3) reports 419 unexpected interruptions during a 54-day training snapshot on 16,384 H100s. Hardware-related categories include GPU and HBM faults, but some events are recoverable or repeated. Its v3 table also contains a count/percentage inconsistency in the faulty-GPU row, so that row should not be used to derive a precise annual failure probability.

> **Worked example: interruptions across a large fleet.** The reported total implies 54 × 24 / 419 ≈ 3.1 hours between unexpected interruptions across the training job. This is an operational event interval, not one GPU's lifetime. Annualizing it does not establish the fraction of GPUs needing replacement or a silicon FIT rate. Comparing it directly with the mechanism-specific HTOL bound above mixes different populations and failure definitions. Fleet telemetry instead helps identify which electrical, thermal, memory and software checks deserve attention.

Fleet interruptions motivate extended burn-in, SLT and telemetry-based screening at module and rack level, even though this report does not establish a per-GPU annual failure rate. Another concern is **silent data corruption (SDC)**, a GPU producing wrong arithmetic without raising any error flag, which has become its own test category. SDC cannot be caught by an error counter, because there is none; it is caught by **arithmetic self-checks**, in which a workload computes the same result two ways (a matrix product and its checksum, or the same kernel on two GPUs) and compares them, at SLT, at rack burn-in and periodically in the fleet. Keep the measurements apart: DPPM counts defective parts relative to parts shipped under a stated quality definition; the fleet report counts interruptions during an observed period. A repeated or recoverable interruption is not another uniquely failed GPU.

## Failure Analysis: From Field Return to Root Cause

**Failure analysis (FA)** is the laboratory work of turning a failed part into a named physical cause. A field-returned GPU follows a fixed path from the cheapest, least destructive step to the most expensive, because every destructive step destroys evidence the earlier ones could have read.

1. **RMA intake.** The **return merchandise authorisation (RMA)** ties the unit's ECID to its full test history and to the fleet's telemetry at the time of failure, so the analyst starts with the error codes the system logged.
2. **Re-test.** The part is run through the original ATE program and often SLT. Some 30–50% are "**no trouble found (NTF)**": the part passes everything, because the failure was a system-level interaction (a marginal module VRM, a cable, a driver bug), an intermittent that needs the rack's exact conditions, or a defect that has since healed. An NTF with a bad telemetry record goes to extended stress; a clean one goes back to the system team.
3. **Non-destructive imaging.** **2D and 3D X-ray** (Nordson, Zeiss Xradia, Nikon) resolves bump voids, cracks and bridging through lid and substrate. **Scanning acoustic microscopy (CSAM)** uses a 15–230 MHz focused ultrasonic transducer in water: a pulse reflects wherever the material changes, and at a solid-to-air boundary (a delamination, a crack) the echo comes back with its sign inverted, because air's acoustic impedance is far below any solid's, so a delamination shows as a distinctive inverted echo; it is the standard tool for lid, underfill and HBM-stack delamination. **Lock-in thermography** switches the part's supply on and off at a few hertz and keeps only the part of a thermal camera's signal that oscillates at exactly that frequency, rejecting everything except heat from the powered defect, and localises a short to tens of microns with microkelvin sensitivity. **Time-domain reflectometry (TDR)** sends a fast pulse down a package trace and times the echo from an open, giving the distance to the break.
4. **Localisation on the die.** After lid removal, **photon emission microscopy (PEM)**: transistors carrying heavy current and any **p-n junction** that is leaking (a junction is the boundary between n-doped and p-doped silicon, Module 10; every transistor has two, and a defect that lets current cross one is a leak) emit faint near-infrared light as fast-moving carriers give up their energy, so a leaking defect glows under a cooled camera looking through the back of the die, which is transparent at those wavelengths. **OBIRCH** (**optical beam induced resistance change**): a laser scans the die spot by spot, heating each slightly; a defective via or resistive short changes resistance with that heat more than good metal does, and the supply current, plotted against beam position, maps the defect as a bright spot.
5. **Destructive.** A **focused ion beam (FIB)** mills a cross-section through the localised spot: gallium ions for nanometre precision, a xenon plasma beam for large volumes such as a full TSV stack (Thermo Fisher Helios, Zeiss Crossbeam). Where the defect is a gate stack, a barrier layer or a nanometre-scale void, the FIB lifts out a **lamella**, a slice ~100 nm thick, for **transmission electron microscopy (TEM)**.

Turnaround is days for the non-destructive steps and weeks when a TEM lamella is needed. Hamamatsu supplies emission-microscopy cameras, Thermo Fisher and Zeiss the FIB and TEM, Nordson and Zeiss the X-ray and acoustic tools; MA-tek and iST are the large independent FA labs in Taiwan and Eurofins EAG serves the US. The output is a root cause, an **8D report** (an eight-discipline corrective-action format: define the team and problem, contain it, find the root cause, choose and verify a fix, prevent recurrence, close) and usually a new test block or tightened limit in the production program; this loop is how FT programs grow from a few hundred to a few thousand tests over a product's life.

## Where Back-End Test Happens and What It Costs

Final test is a service industry concentrated in Taiwan. **KYEC** (King Yuan Electronics) is generally described as the largest **pure-play** test house in the world (test only, no assembly), with [Q1 2026 consolidated revenue](https://www.kyec.com.tw/en/Ir/Quarterly) of **NT$**10.19 billion (New Taiwan dollars; roughly US$319 million at an illustrative NT$32 per US dollar) and a business now dominated by AI logic and HBM-related testing. **ASE** (with SPIL) and **Amkor** combine assembly and test, and Amkor's Peoria, Arizona campus (construction due mid-2027, production from early 2028, as announced as of 2025–26) will be among the first US advanced-packaging and test sites for CoWoS-class parts; Powertech and ChipMOS specialise in memory and display. NVIDIA owns no test floors: as of 2025–26 its Blackwell flow is reported to be split between TSMC (package-level test after CoWoS on its own testers), KYEC and ASE/SPIL for FT, BI and SLT, with Amkor as overflow, and dies fabricated at TSMC Arizona still return to Taiwan for CoWoS and test. Module-level test moves to the **ODMs** (original design manufacturers, who build boards and servers to NVIDIA's reference design: Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron, Inventec) in Taiwan and Mexico.

Who runs which insertion is reported rather than disclosed, and the reported division follows whose yield each screen gates (~). TSMC's chip-on-wafer probe and its package-level test after CoWoS are the foundry's outgoing gates on its own assembly: they catch the bump, interposer and HBM-attach losses that count against assembly yield while the package is still on TSMC's floor and TSMC's account, with a subset of the program on TSMC's own testers (its content is not disclosed; continuity, leakage, HBM training and a functional check is the natural minimum). The test houses then run the four insertions of the flow table, FT1, burn-in, FT2 and SLT, which gate NVIDIA's shipped quality. So TSMC's post-CoWoS test is not the flow table's FT1 but a screen ahead of it, though trade descriptions sometimes merge the two into one "final test" (~). The boundary is contractual as much as technical: an assembly defect found at TSMC's gate is TSMC's loss, while one that escapes to KYEC's FT1 becomes a negotiation over who pays for a scrapped $3,000 package, which gives the foundry every reason to run a real electrical test rather than a continuity check. Between floors the parts travel in JEDEC trays inside moisture-barrier bags by truck across western Taiwan (TSMC's advanced-packaging fabs at Longtan, Chunan and Chiayi; KYEC in Hsinchu and Miaoli; ASE in Kaohsiung), each lot with a travelling record. The data travel separately: every insertion on every floor writes its STDF against the ECID into the product owner's (NVIDIA's) test-data database, which is what lets FT2 at KYEC compare against an FT1 that ran on a different cell or a different company's floor, lets NVIDIA's product engineers watch every floor's yield in one place, and lets a rack failure in a hyperscaler's fleet be traced back through SLT, FT2, FT1, TSMC's test, sort and the wafer lot.

The cost stack per high-end GPU, approximate and as of ~2025–2026: FT1 plus FT2 tester time $8–50 depending on parallelism (the final-test section's arithmetic), burn-in $5–50, SLT $10–30, consumables and OQA $2–5, or ~$25–135, call it ~$30–150 per unit, 1–5% in the illustrative legacy ~$3,000-package **BOM** model (**bill of materials**, the summed cost of the parts in a product; a cost, not the ~$30,000 the module sells for). Module 19 quotes ~$100–200 because its figure also includes wafer sort and binning characterisation, and its ~1–2 weeks for this stage is elapsed time including queueing between test houses, against the ~3–7 days of processing in the table below. The capital behind it is substantial: a V93000 configured with high-speed SerDes and high-current instruments is $3–6 million, a 100-slot high-power SLT rack ~$1–3 million, and a 1 kW-per-slot burn-in system similar per thousand slots; KYEC’s 2025 consolidated cash-flow statement reports NT$32.36 billion of PP&E cash acquisitions, roughly US$1 billion at NT$32/US$, versus NT$14.86 billion in 2024. This is group facilities-and-equipment investment, not equipment-only spend recurring every year.

> **Worked example: how many testers one product needs.** Take 1 million GPUs a year with ~10 minutes of FT1 and ~3 minutes of FT2 at x2: 13 ÷ 2 = 6.5 tester-minutes per unit, 6.5 million tester-minutes ≈ 108,000 tester-hours a year. A tester gives ~8,000 productive hours a year (8,760 at ~90% utilisation), so FT alone needs 108,000 ÷ 8,000 ≈ 13.5, call it 12–15 V93000s, ~$40–80 million at $3–6 million each. Burn-in: 1 million × 24–48 h = 24–48 million slot-hours ÷ 8,000 h ≈ 3,000–6,000 slots, ~$3–18 million at the figure above. SLT: the 3–5 racks of the earlier example, $3–15 million. One product at one million units a year thus ties up ~$50–110 million of back-end test capital before handlers, sockets, thermal heads and floor space, the scale against which KYEC’s approximately US$1 billion-equivalent PP&E cash spend in 2025 across the group makes sense.

## The GPU Flow End to End, and Rack-Level Triage

Pulling the chain together for an NVIDIA data-centre GPU, with the cost of a failure and the time spent at each stage. The value column is build cost for the die, stack, package and module rows, which are scrapped, and selling price for the board, tray and rack rows, which are idled and repaired. Terms in the table: an **NVSwitch** is the switch chip that connects GPUs' NVLink ports so any GPU can reach any other; a **GB200 compute tray** is the Blackwell server unit holding two Grace CPUs and four GPUs; **ECC** (**error-correcting code**) adds bits to every memory word so single-bit errors are detected and corrected, and an **ECC scrub** reads all of memory to find and log them; **CRC** (**cyclic redundancy check**) is a checksum on every NVLink packet, and a **replay** a retransmission after a mismatch; **NCCL all-reduce** is the collective operation, through NVIDIA's communication library, in which every GPU sums a tensor with every other, the most fabric-intensive step in training; **HPL** is the LINPACK benchmark, a dense matrix solve that runs every GPU at full power; **AOI** is automated optical inspection of the assembled board.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wafer sort | TSMC Taiwan (or KYEC) | Scan, MBIST, IDDQ, SM harvesting, e-fuse | ~$350 per die | Hours per wafer (~1 week per lot, Module 19) |
| HBM stack test | SK hynix, Samsung, Micron | DA-port array test, IEEE 1500, stack BI | ~$200 per stack | Days |
| CoWoS assembly | TSMC Longtan/Chunan/Chiayi, ASE/SPIL, Amkor | Chip-on-wafer probe, X-ray, CSAM sample | $2,500–3,500 | 2–4 weeks |
| Package test: FT1, BI, FT2, SLT | TSMC, KYEC, ASE | As described above | $2,500–3,500 | ~3–7 days of processing; ~1–2 weeks elapsed (Module 19) |

Each level down the table repairs rather than scraps: a GPU failing SLT is scrapped or downgraded, a module failing board test is unplugged and returned to module test, a tray failing rack test is swapped in minutes. The governing principle is the **rule of ten** (Module 14): the cost of finding a defect rises roughly an order of magnitude at each level of integration (package, module, board, system, field), because each adds disassembly labour, retest time, the idled value of surrounding hardware and, in the field, customer downtime. Module 19 Part C describes the module, tray and rack stages and the ODMs that run them; here the point is only how the levels multiply.

> **Worked example: the rule of ten with dollars.** Rough figures for a defect that escapes: ~$50 to catch at package test, ~$500 at module test (rework and re-test), ~$5,000 at board test (a baseboard taken apart while its other GPUs wait), ~$50,000 at rack test (a tray swap, a re-run of a multi-day burn-in, a $3–4 million rack idle for days), ~$500,000 in the field (a training job of thousands of GPUs stalled, a technician visit, a return). A screen is worth running when its cost per unit is below its catch probability times the downstream cost. At $50 per unit, catching one defect in a thousand parts saves 0.001 × $50,000 = $50 if the defect would have surfaced at rack test (break-even) and 0.001 × $500,000 = $500 if in the field, ten to one; against field cost the break-even catch rate is one part in ten thousand (0.0001 × $500,000 = $50). SLT's ~0.5–2% fallout is 5–20 times the rack-test break-even of 0.1% and 50–200 times the field break-even of 0.01%, which is why a $50 package-level screen is justified even at a catch rate of one part in a thousand once field cost is counted.

**Rack-level triage** is a telemetry exercise. Every GPU reports through NVIDIA's **DCGM** (Data Center GPU Manager, the fleet monitoring software) and driver **XID** error codes (the numbered error classes the NVIDIA driver logs, from a hung kernel to an uncorrectable memory error): correctable and uncorrectable HBM ECC counts, NVLink CRC and replay counters, PCIe errors, thermal and power excursions, **retired HBM pages** (memory pages with too many correctable errors that the driver has stopped using). Rack burn-in runs synthetic workloads (all-reduce across 72 GPUs, matrix-multiply loops, memory bandwidth tests) for days while logging these counters, and the pass criterion is not merely "no crash" but thresholds on correctable-error rates and on arithmetic self-checks that catch silent data corruption. A GPU over threshold is identified by ECID and slot, its tray is swapped from spares and sent to a repair depot where the module is retested, the package removed if the fault is confirmed, and returned to NVIDIA for the FA path above; results feed back to the package test program and, if a pattern emerges by wafer lot, to the fab. The same loop runs more slowly on deployed clusters: hyperscaler fleet telemetry is the ultimate outgoing-quality test, and it shapes the next product's burn-in and SLT specifications.

## Summary

- Sort proves the die; final test proves the package: the HBM and die-to-die links, full current, at-speed SerDes and every assembly-induced defect exist only after packaging, and a probe card can deliver neither the amps nor the gigabits to test them.
- A GPU flow is FT1 → burn-in → FT2 → SLT, ordered cheapest-and-most-informative first; burn-in is bracketed by tester insertions because the oven can only stress and read pass/fail, and only the tester can measure the drift in IDDQ and Vmin that marks a damaged part.
- The test cell is tester + handler + load board + socket + thermal head; for a kilowatt GPU the thermal head (800–2,000 W class) is what makes speed and voltage bins meaningful, because 1 kW through ~0.05 °C/W puts the die 50 °C above the plate.
- An FT program is an ordered list of blocks (continuity via the ESD diodes, IDDQ as an outlier screen, scan, MBIST, interfaces, SerDes, Vmin/Fmax, fuses), each earning its seconds; the Vmin/Fmax search decides each unit's shipped voltage and speed grade, and every 10 mV of guard band is ~2–3% of a 1 kW part's power.
- Burn-in ages parts through the infant-mortality region; Arrhenius with Ea ≈ 0.7 eV gives AF ≈ 78 for 125 °C versus 55 °C, so 48 h ≈ 5 months of field life, and over-voltage multiplies that by a further ~3–6× to a combined ~300.
- Burn-in runs slow patterns, not a full-power workload, but on a Blackwell-class die the leakage at 125 °C and +10–20% VDD is alone a few hundred watts (~), which is what the ~1 kW slot with its heater and liquid cold plate exists to hold at set point; too much stress shows as a population-wide Vmin shift at FT2, and a socket that opens at temperature ships an unstressed part that looks perfect.
- FT2 screens on deltas (IDDQ, Vmin) against the same unit's FT1 record by ECID, not only on absolute limits, because the population spread is wider than the drift being hunted; cell-to-cell correlation keeps a thermal-head offset from masquerading as drift.
- SLT boots real firmware and runs workloads because scan coverage is measured against fault models, not products; its ~0.5–2% fallout is almost entirely parts that passed ATE, and a 100-slot rack at 30 minutes delivers ~180 UPH.
- Qualification (JESD47, AEC-Q100) is sampled, once per product, and a clean 231-unit HTOL bounds the constant failure rate at ~50 FIT; it says nothing about infant mortality, which is what the field mostly sees.
- HBM must ship as known-good stacks tested through the base die (IEEE 1500, direct-access port, TSV repair) because a stack that fails on the interposer scraps a $3,000 package; each half-point of known-good-die yield is worth ~5 points of 12-high stack yield.
- Back-end yield losses are fewer in count than sort losses but larger in dollars; a point of FT1 yield is worth ~$30 per shipped unit, and back-end test itself costs ~$30–150 per GPU.
- Fleet interruption telemetry helps improve screening, but repeated or recoverable events do not directly measure unique GPU failure probability or silicon FIT.

## Common Misconceptions

- "Wafer sort already tested the chip, so final test is a formality." → Sort could not test the HBM interface, die-to-die links, full-power operation, at-speed SerDes or any package-induced defect; FT1 fallout of 2–5% on a good process is real and expensive.
- "Burn-in runs the chip at full power in a hot oven." → Burn-in runs low-frequency patterns whose switching power is tens of watts; on a large GPU the few hundred watts (~) the slot must remove is leakage at 125 °C and over-voltage, not workload, and Tj is held by a heater and cold plate per socket rather than by the oven air. The acceleration comes from temperature and over-voltage, not from running the workload.
- "Burn-in and HTOL are the same thing." → Burn-in is a 100% production screen of hours; HTOL is a sampled 1,000 h qualification that measures the population's failure rate and scraps the samples.
- "System-level test is redundant if scan coverage is 99%." → Scan coverage is against a fault model; SLT catches clock-domain, power-management, firmware and analog-interaction failures that no fault model represents, and its fallout on GPUs is ~0.5–2% of parts that passed ATE.
- "A bad HBM stack costs the price of the stack." → Once on a CoWoS interposer it cannot be reworked; the whole package is lost, which is why HBM must ship at 99.9%-class quality and why HBM qualification takes months.
- "Yield loss is a front-end problem." → Sort loss is larger in count, but back-end losses on a $3,000 package cost several times more per shipped unit than the die losses at sort.
- "A DPPM of a few hundred means a few hundred per million of the fleet will fail." → DPPM counts parts defective at shipment; fleet failure rates are per year and dominated by infant mortality, HBM and packaging wear, which is how a fleet built from parts shipped at a few hundred DPPM sees ~5–8% of GPUs fail per year.
- "A clean HTOL means the product is reliable." → The example bounds a failure rate at a chosen confidence level under a constant-rate, mechanism-specific acceleration model. It does not certify every failure mechanism or the complete infant-mortality and wear-out behavior of the shipped system.
