# Module 04: Beyond Silicon: Compound Semiconductors and Fab Consumables

Materials play two roles in a chip factory: they become part of the finished device, or they help manufacture it. Some do both. **Consumables** are the materials used up or regularly replaced during manufacturing. Silicon is the main starting surface, or **substrate**, for most chips in this course. Other semiconductors can make better power switches, radio components or light sources. Consumables do different jobs: gases supply atoms for a film, acids clean a surface, and polishing mixtures remove material. Some supply atoms that remain in the chip; much of what enters the factory is removed or recycled after doing its job.

This module follows both sides of that story. **Half A** explains why a designer would choose something other than plain silicon: silicon-germanium (SiGe) to improve a transistor, silicon carbide (SiC) for an 800 V electric-vehicle inverter, gallium nitride (GaN) for a compact charger, gallium arsenide (GaAs) for a phone's radio, or indium phosphide (InP) for a laser in an optical link. Those links carry data between **GPUs**, graphics processing units used as accelerator chips for AI arithmetic. We also look at the **two-dimensional (2D) materials**, atomically thin sheets being tested as transistor channels for the 2030s. The aim is to understand each material's advantage and what it costs to manufacture, rather than to memorize the list.

**Half B** follows the fab's shopping list. A **fab** is a chip factory; a **process step** is one pass through a tool, such as depositing a film, exposing a pattern or cleaning a wafer. A leading-edge wafer goes through ~1,000+ such steps. A 300 mm wafer that leaves TSMC N2 at ~$25,000–30,000 (industry estimates; TSMC does not publish) has had thousands of dollars of consumables poured over it and pumped through it. We will connect each material to its job before asking who supplies it and why replacing that supplier is difficult.

The common challenge is control: the right composition, the right atomic structure, and impurities kept low enough for the particular job. Some impurity limits reach **part-per-trillion (ppt)** levels: one foreign atom in a million million, the same ratio as one second in 32,000 years. The requirement is not identical for every material. A fab must also prove that a new supply works reliably in its process. That **qualification** can mean a 6–18 month campaign through the full manufacturing sequence, checking that the fraction of working chips, or **yield**, does not fall. This is why switching vendors mid-node can take a year even when the replacement has the same chemical name.

By the end you should be able to explain why a SiC wafer costs tens of times more than a silicon one, how a GaN charger can be made from material grown on a silicon wafer, what 18.2 MΩ·cm water means and why a fab needs six cubic metres per wafer, why an **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** mask is a mirror, and how a disruption at one chemical supplier can reach a distant chip factory.

## Before you start

- **Crystals and lattice constants (Module 02).** In a single crystal the atoms repeat at a fixed spacing, quoted in **ångströms (Å)**, where 1 Å = 0.1 nm; silicon's cube repeats every 5.431 Å. That spacing is the material's fingerprint, and it decides what can be grown on what: two crystals with the same spacing stack cleanly, two with different spacings strain or crack.
- **Carriers, bandgap and dopants (Modules 02, 10).** A semiconductor conducts with two kinds of carrier: **electrons** and **holes** (a hole is a missing electron in the bonding network; it moves and carries current as if it were a positive charge). The **bandgap** is the energy toll an electron must pay to break free of a bond and conduct. **Dopants** are foreign atoms that add electrons (**n-type**, phosphorus or arsenic in silicon) or holes (**p-type**, boron in silicon) so that the toll no longer has to be paid by heat.
- **A transistor in one breath (Module 11).** Three regions in a row, **source**, **channel**, **drain**, with a **gate** electrode on top separated from the channel by a thin insulator; the gate's voltage opens or closes the channel. An **nMOS** transistor carries current with electrons, a **pMOS** transistor with holes, and **CMOS** logic pairs one of each. At the 2 nm generation the channel is a stack of thin silicon ribbons (**nanosheets**) with the gate wrapped round every side of each ribbon.
- **Epitaxy (Modules 03, 06).** Growing a crystal film that continues the substrate's lattice atom for atom, so the film and the wafer are one crystal.
- **Czochralski growth (Module 02).** A seed crystal is dipped into a melt of the same material in a quartz crucible and drawn out slowly, and a single crystal grows below it. It only works for materials that melt cleanly into a liquid of the same composition; several materials in this module do not.
- **The fab loop (Modules 05–12).** Deposit a film, coat it with a light-sensitive resist, expose the resist through a mask, develop it, etch the film through the openings, strip the resist, clean; repeated for each of ~80–100 pattern layers, ~1,000+ steps in all. Each pass consumes something from this module.
- **Purity notation.** ppm, ppb and ppt are parts per million, billion and trillion: one part in 10^6, 10^9 and 10^12. Purity is also counted in nines: **6N** means 99.9999% pure (six nines), **12N** means twelve nines.
- **Units you will see.** **Torr** is a pressure unit, 1/760 of an atmosphere; **bar** is roughly one atmosphere; **MPa** and **GPa** are megapascals and gigapascals (1 GPa ≈ 10,000 atmospheres). **Nm³** is a "normal cubic metre" of gas, a cubic metre measured at standard temperature and pressure, i.e. a fixed number of molecules. **MΩ·cm** is a unit of resistivity, the resistance of a 1 cm cube of the material. **cm⁻²** and **cm⁻³** are counts per square or cubic centimetre; for scale, silicon has ~5 × 10^22 atoms per cm³ and its surface holds ~6.8 × 10^14 atoms per cm².
- **Lithography wavelengths (Modules 07, 08).** The four generations of exposure light are **i-line** (365 nm, a mercury lamp line), **KrF** (248 nm) and **ArF** (193 nm) excimer lasers, and **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** at 13.5 nm. In every case the mask pattern is imaged onto the wafer reduced 4×.

## Why silicon wins, and where it loses

This begins Half A. Silicon's dominance rests on four things: an abundant raw material; a stable native oxide (SiO2) that **passivates** the surface almost perfectly, meaning it ties up the broken bonds at the surface so they neither leak current nor trap charge; a melting point (1,414 °C) low enough for Czochralski growth in a quartz crucible; and 60 years of accumulated process learning. But its intrinsic electronic properties are mediocre. **Mobility** is how fast a carrier drifts per unit of electric field, in cm²/V·s; higher mobility means a faster switch and a lower **on-resistance** (the resistance of a switch when it is turned on). Electron mobility in bulk Si is ~1,400 cm²/V·s and hole mobility only ~450 cm²/V·s. Its bandgap of 1.12 eV (an **electron-volt (eV)** is the energy an electron gains falling through one volt, 1.6 × 10^-19 J) is **indirect**: emitting a photon when an electron and hole recombine also requires a lattice vibration to balance momentum. That makes this light-emission route inefficient, so ordinary silicon is a poor material for an electrically driven laser diode. This does not forbid every kind of silicon laser: optically pumped Raman lasers use a different amplification mechanism. In a **direct** gap material (GaAs, InP, GaN) the electron and hole can recombine and emit a photon straight away. And silicon's **critical electric field**, the field at which a freed electron gains enough energy between collisions to knock loose more electrons in a chain reaction (**avalanche breakdown**), is only ~0.3 MV/cm (megavolts per centimetre; 0.3 MV/cm is 30 V across one micron), which forces high-voltage silicon devices to be thick, slow, and lossy.

> **Intuition: a lattice with a toll gate.** Think of a semiconductor as a crystal lattice with a small energy toll gate. Below the gate the electrons are all locked into bonds and nothing moves; each electron that pays the toll (the bandgap) becomes a free carrier and leaves a hole behind. Heat pays the toll for a few electrons, doping lets carriers in without paying, and an electric field drives the carriers that are through the gate. The height of the gate sets how hot and how high a field the material can tolerate before too many carriers get through on their own (wide gap = SiC, GaN); the ease with which carriers move once inside sets the speed (mobility). The analogy breaks in one respect: a direct-gap material lets a carrier fall back through the gate and pay out its energy as light, while an indirect-gap material like silicon makes it pay out as heat.

Every material in this half exists to fix one of those deficiencies. The table below is the reference you will want while reading; the smaller table after it says what each column means for a device.

## Silicon-germanium: the alloy inside every modern transistor

**Silicon-germanium (Si1-xGex)** is a fully **miscible** alloy: Si and Ge mix in any proportion because they share the same **diamond cubic** crystal structure (each atom bonded to four neighbours at the corners of a tetrahedron), and you can dial in any Ge fraction x from 0 to 1. The catch is that Ge's lattice constant (5.658 Å) is **4.2% larger** than Si's (5.431 Å), so the mismatch of Si1-xGex to Si scales as roughly 0.042·x. Grow SiGe **epitaxially** on a silicon wafer (as a crystal film that continues the wafer's lattice atom for atom) and the film wants to be bigger than the template it sits on. For a thin enough film, the crystal accommodates this elastically: the SiGe is squeezed in-plane to match the Si lattice, and it bulges out-of-plane (tetragonal distortion). This is a **pseudomorphic** or **coherently strained** film, and the compressive strain in it is exactly what device engineers want.

> **Intuition: a carpet on a smaller floor.** A strained film is a carpet stretched over a slightly smaller floor (or, for SiGe on Si, a carpet compressed onto it). Below a certain thickness the carpet just stays stretched, because the energy stored in the stretch is less than the energy it would cost to make a wrinkle. Above it, a wrinkle forms at the interface: an extra half-plane of atoms that ends in a line, a **misfit dislocation**, which lets the film relax back toward its own spacing. A wrinkle cannot simply stop in the middle of the carpet, so its ends run up to the surface as **threading dislocations**, and it is those threading segments that pass through the transistor.

Above a **critical thickness** h_c, the stored elastic energy exceeds the energy cost of creating misfit dislocations at the interface, and the film relaxes by generating them. The classic Matthews–Blakeslee **equilibrium** theory (which asks only which state has lower energy) gives h_c on the order of 10–20 nm for Si0.8Ge0.2 and only a few nm for Si0.5Ge0.5. In practice films grown at low temperature (~550–650 °C) can be grown **metastably** several times thicker (the People–Bean curve): above h_c the dislocation is energetically favourable, but it needs a nucleus to start and the atoms need enough thermal energy to move, so a film grown cold can stay defect-free for a while beyond the equilibrium limit. Misfit dislocations are catastrophic in a transistor: their threading segments punch up through the channel, act as leakage paths, and **getter** dopants (attract and trap them, so the doping is no longer where the design put it). So all production SiGe in logic is kept safely below h_c and is deposited by **selective epitaxy** in a reduced-pressure **chemical vapour deposition (CVD)** reactor (gases react on the hot wafer and leave a solid film behind; Applied Materials Centura Epi or ASM Intrepid class tools) using dichlorosilane (SiH2Cl2) or silane, germane (GeH4), HCl for selectivity, and diborane (B2H6) for in-situ p-type doping, at ~600–700 °C and ~10–80 **Torr** (1/760 of an atmosphere, so ~1–10% of atmospheric pressure). **Selectivity** means the film grows only where you want it: the HCl etches away the thin film that tries to nucleate on oxide or nitride faster than it can grow, so SiGe appears only on exposed silicon. **In-situ doping** means the dopant gas is mixed into the growth gas, so the film arrives already doped and no **ion implantation** (firing dopant ions into the wafer, the usual way to dope a finished layer; Module 10) is needed.

Recall from Before you start that the pMOS half of CMOS carries current with holes and, given silicon's poor hole mobility, has always been the weaker half (Module 11 has the full transistor); everything in job 1 is about making holes faster.

There are three distinct jobs SiGe does in a modern CMOS process:

**1. Embedded SiGe source/drain (eSiGe) for the pMOS transistor.** Intel introduced this at its 90 nm node in 2003 and every logic node since has used it. After the gate is defined, a recess is etched into the source and drain regions on either side of the pMOS channel, and SiGe is selectively regrown into the recess. Because the SiGe wants to be larger than the Si it fills, it pushes on the channel from both sides, creating uniaxial **compressive strain** along the direction the channel runs (crystallographically the <110> direction, a diagonal across a face of silicon's cubic cell; the angle-bracket notation is how crystallographers name a direction in a crystal, and round brackets, as in (100), name a plane).

> **Intuition: why squeezing makes holes lighter.** First, two words. The energies an electron may have in a crystal come in **bands**: the **valence band** is the range of energies occupied by the bonding electrons (the region below the toll gate), and the **conduction band** is the range above it where free electrons travel; a hole is an empty state near the top of the valence band. Holes in silicon are heavy because two energy bands sit on top of each other at the top of the valence band and mix, and mixing makes the carriers sluggish. Squeezing the crystal along the channel pushes one of those bands up and the other down. The holes then fill only the upper, lighter band, and a lighter carrier accelerates faster for the same voltage. The strain also reduces how often a hole is knocked from one band into the other (**intervalley scattering**), so it keeps its speed longer.

The result is a hole-mobility boost of ~50% or more and drive-current gains of 20–30%. The Ge fraction has climbed from ~17% at 90 nm to ~40–55% in **FinFET** nodes (a **field-effect transistor (FET)** is the general name for a gate-controlled switch like the one in Before you start; a FinFET is one whose channel is a thin vertical fin of silicon with the gate draped over three sides, the transistor generation from 2011 until nanosheets) and nanosheet nodes, with the recess brought closer to the channel each generation. Boron in-situ doping to >10^20 cm⁻³ (about one boron atom per 500 silicon atoms, close to the most silicon will dissolve) simultaneously lowers contact resistance, and Ge lowers the **Schottky barrier** to the contact metal, the energy step a carrier must climb where a metal touches a semiconductor; the taller the step, the higher the contact resistance. For nMOS, the equivalent trick would be a tensile stressor (Si:C with ~1–2% carbon, or SiP), which had a much rougher time in production; nMOS strain has come more from tensile nitride liners (a stressed film draped over the transistor), from **stress memorization** (a nitride cap that leaves strain behind in the silicon after an anneal, even once the cap is removed), and, in nanosheets, from process geometry.

**2. Sacrificial layers in gate-all-around nanosheets.** In a **nanosheet** transistor the channel is a stack of 3–4 silicon ribbons ~5–10 nm thick with the gate wrapped around every side of each ribbon; the SiGe layers are the spacers that hold the ribbons apart during fabrication and are dissolved away at the end. TSMC N2, Samsung SF3/SF2 and Intel 18A (the 2 nm-class generations of the three leading-edge manufacturers) build their channels from a **Si/SiGe superlattice**, a repeating sandwich of two materials a few nanometres each: alternating layers of ~5–10 nm Si and ~8–12 nm Si0.7Ge0.3 epitaxially grown blanket over the wafer. The whole stack must stay below the critical thickness of the ensemble (it is the total strain energy of all the SiGe layers together, not each layer alone, that can trigger dislocations), which constrains x to roughly 0.2–0.35. Later, during **channel release**, the SiGe is removed selectively with a vapor-phase HCl or a dry-etch chemistry that etches SiGe hundreds of times faster than Si, leaving suspended Si nanosheets around which the gate is wrapped. The Ge fraction is a tradeoff: higher x gives better etch selectivity but lower h_c and rougher interfaces.

**3. SiGe as a channel.** With Ge fraction pushed to 0.5–1.0, SiGe is a candidate pMOS channel in its own right. Intel, imec (the Belgian research centre where much of the industry's shared transistor R&D is done) and others have shown Si0.4Ge0.6 and pure Ge nanosheet pMOS transistors with superior hole mobility, but integration is brutal: Ge's oxide is unstable (see the germanium section), the SiGe/Si superlattice becomes inverted (now Si is the sacrificial layer), and the **thermal budget**, the total time-at-temperature the wafer sees after the layers are grown, must be cut to avoid Ge diffusing into the Si. As of ~2025 this remained an R&D option for nodes beyond TSMC's A14 (its 1.4 nm-class node, planned for ~2028), not a production choice.

> **Worked example: how thick can a Si0.75Ge0.25 layer be before it relaxes?**
> Misfit strain f ≈ 0.042 × 0.25 = 1.05%. In Matthews–Blakeslee form the critical thickness for a single strained layer is approximately h_c ≈ (b / (2πf)) · ((1 − ν cos²α) / ((1 + ν) cos λ)) · ln(h_c / b + 1). The symbols:
> b = the **Burgers vector**, the size of the lattice step a dislocation introduces, ≈ 0.38 nm (the atomic spacing along the slip direction) for the 60° dislocations that form in diamond-cubic crystals;
> ν = the **Poisson ratio**, how much a squeezed material bulges sideways, ≈ 0.28 for Si;
> α = the angle between the dislocation line and its Burgers vector, 60° for this dislocation type, so cos α = 0.5;
> λ = the angle between the slip direction and the in-plane direction that relieves the misfit, also 60°, so cos λ = 0.5.
> The geometric prefactor is (1 − 0.07) / (1.28 × 0.5) ≈ 1.45. Because h_c appears inside the logarithm as well as on the left, you guess, compute, and repeat until it stops changing: guess h_c = 15 nm gives ln(15/0.38 + 1) ≈ 3.7, so h_c ≈ (0.38 / (2π × 0.0105)) × 1.45 × 3.7 ≈ 5.76 × 1.45 × 3.7 ≈ 31 nm; plugging 31 nm back gives ln(82.6) ≈ 4.4 and h_c ≈ 37 nm. The equilibrium answer is ~35–40 nm. In plain language: a quarter-Ge film can be ~35–40 nm thick before it wrinkles; halve the Ge and it can be much thicker, double it and only a few nanometres survive. That is why nanosheet designers use ~10 nm SiGe sacrificial layers at x ≈ 0.25–0.3: each layer is comfortably below h_c, but the total stack of 3–4 SiGe layers plus interleaved Si still has to be engineered to stay coherent. Push x to 0.5 and h_c collapses to ~5–8 nm, which is why high-Ge channels are so hard.

> **What can go wrong:** The failure mode is always the same: misfit dislocations at the interface and the threading segments that rise from them, which leak current, trap dopants and scatter carriers. Engineers look for them three ways. **X-ray diffraction reciprocal-space maps** measure the film's in-plane and out-of-plane lattice spacings; a coherent film shows the substrate's in-plane spacing exactly, a relaxed one drifts toward its own. A **defect-decoration etch** (a chemical that etches faster where a dislocation meets the surface) turns each threading dislocation into a countable pit. And **cross-section transmission electron microscopy (TEM)** shows the interface directly, one wrinkle at a time. The fix is to lower the Ge fraction, the thickness or the growth temperature, or all three, until the film sits comfortably below the People–Bean curve for the whole thermal budget that follows.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The Ge fraction chosen here is inherited by two later modules at once. It sets the etch selectivity of the channel release in Modules 09 and 11 (more Ge, easier to remove the spacers without thinning the silicon ribbons) and it sets the critical-thickness ceiling on the whole superlattice; the nanosheet stack you will meet in Module 11 is the compromise between those two.

## Strained silicon: the idea that went the other way

This is a side note. **Strained silicon** in its original 1990s form (IBM, MIT, AmberWave) meant the opposite geometry: grow a thick, fully relaxed SiGe **virtual substrate** (graded from 0 to ~20–30% Ge over several microns), then grow a thin Si layer on top. Because relaxed SiGe has a larger lattice than Si, the thin Si is under **biaxial tensile strain**, stretched in both in-plane directions. Silicon's electrons have six equivalent energy valleys, six "lanes" they can travel in; stretching the crystal in-plane lowers two of them, electrons crowd into those two, and in those lanes they happen to be lighter along the direction of travel, so they move up to ~1.8× faster. It worked in the lab. It failed in production because the relaxed buffer inevitably contains ~10^5–10^7 cm⁻² **threading dislocations** (versus essentially zero in a CZ wafer, Module 02; at 10^6 cm⁻² a 1 mm² die contains ~10,000 of them) and a **crosshatch** surface roughness (a fabric-like ripple that the buried dislocation network imprints on the surface), and because **chemical-mechanical planarization (CMP)** of SiGe (the polishing step that grinds a wafer flat, covered in Half B) and Ge outdiffusion into the channel were headaches. The industry instead took the process-induced route: eSiGe, stress liners, stress memorization, and later the FinFET geometry, all of which put local strain into the channel without a defective global substrate. The lesson, which recurs throughout this module, is that the physics is rarely the bottleneck; the defect density and the integration cost are.

## Silicon carbide: the material you cannot melt

Start with the problem. An electric vehicle's traction inverter takes 400–800 V of direct current from the battery and chops it, tens of thousands of times a second, into the alternating waveforms that spin the motor, at currents of hundreds of amps. Every watt lost in the switches doing the chopping is range that is not driven and heat that must be carried away by a radiator. **Power electronics** is the branch of the industry that builds those switches, and the switch's job is contradictory: when off, it must hold back the whole battery voltage without leaking; when on, it must pass hundreds of amps with almost no voltage drop; and it must flip between the two states in nanoseconds.

When the switch is off, a **drift region** spreads the voltage drop through a thick, lightly doped layer. **Electric field** describes how quickly voltage changes with distance: forcing the same voltage drop into a shorter distance means a stronger field. The layer does not use up voltage; it provides enough distance to keep the field below the level that would cause breakdown. The higher the field a material can stand before avalanche (its critical field), the thinner that layer can be for a given **blocking voltage** (the voltage the device holds when off), and the thinner and more heavily doped the layer, the lower its on-resistance (the switch's resistance when on, from the first section). Silicon's ~0.3 MV/cm is the reason a silicon switch rated for 1,200 V is thick and lossy.

> **Intuition: a valve.** A power switch is a valve that must hold back a high pressure when closed (blocking voltage) and lose almost no pressure when open (on-resistance). The drift region is the length of pipe that holds the pressure. A material that tolerates 10× the field needs a pipe 10× shorter for the same pressure, and because a shorter pipe needs less margin it can be doped ~100× more heavily (the doping the layer can carry scales with the square of the field). Why a square: the field inside the layer falls from the critical field (call it E_c) at one end to zero at the other, and the rate at which it falls is set by the doping, because the field drops by a fixed step at every ionized dopant atom it passes (Gauss's law from school physics: field lines end on charges). The slope is E_c over the thickness W, so the SiC layer, which must fall from a field 10× higher across a length 10× shorter, needs 100× the slope and therefore 100× the dopant charge per unit volume. In symbols, W ∝ 1/E_c and doping N ∝ E_c/W ∝ E_c². Shorter and 100× more conductive together make the valve ~1,000× less resistive.

**Silicon carbide (SiC)** is the workhorse **wide-bandgap** material (a bandgap of roughly 3 eV or more, so the material tolerates high temperature and high field) for power electronics. Its critical field of ~2.5–3 MV/cm is roughly ten times silicon's. Two relations describe the consequence in an idealized drift-region model. The blocking voltage of a drift region of width W is roughly V_B ≈ E_c · W / 2, where E_c is the critical field (the field falls linearly across the layer, hence the half); so a given voltage needs a layer inversely proportional to E_c. The **specific on-resistance** is resistance multiplied by active area, expressed in Ω·cm² or mΩ·cm². Multiplication removes the simple advantage of making a device wider: more area gives more parallel current paths and lowers resistance. For the idealized drift layer it is R_on,sp = 4 V_B² / (ε µ E_c³), where ε is the **permittivity** (the material constant describing its electrical response to an applied field) and µ the electron mobility. The denominator ε µ E_c³ is the **Baliga figure of merit**: for a fixed rating, on-resistance falls as the cube of the critical field. A 1,200 V SiC **MOSFET** (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor: the gate-over-insulator switch sketched in Before you start, of which nMOS and pMOS are the two flavours) can therefore have a drift layer only ~10 µm thick and an ideal drift-region specific on-resistance hundreds of times lower than silicon at the same rating. A complete MOSFET also has channel, contact and other resistances, so this model is not a prediction of the full device-resistance ratio.

> **Worked example: how much lower is SiC's on-resistance at 1,200 V?**
> Take the ratio of R_on,sp for silicon over 4H-SiC at the same V_B. The E_c term alone gives (2.5 / 0.3)³ ≈ 580. Mobility works against SiC (µ_SiC / µ_Si ≈ 900 / 1,400 ≈ 0.64) and so does permittivity (relative ε ≈ 9.7 for SiC versus ≈ 11.9 for silicon, a factor ≈ 0.82). So the net advantage is ≈ 580 × 0.64 × 0.82 ≈ 300×, "hundreds of times" as stated. Now the thickness: W ≈ 2 V_B / E_c = 2 × 1,200 V / (2.5 × 10^6 V/cm) ≈ 9.6 × 10^-4 cm ≈ 10 µm, which is why the epi paragraph below says a 1,200 V MOSFET needs ~10–12 µm. The same 1,200 V in silicon would need ~80 µm at ~0.3 MV/cm.

SiC's thermal conductivity (~370–490 W/m·K) is three times silicon's, so the die can run hotter and be cooled more easily. And unlike silicon **IGBTs** (insulated-gate bipolar transistors; **bipolar** means the device conducts with both electrons and holes, and the IGBT is the silicon power switch used above ~600 V, which floods its drift layer with both carrier types to lower its resistance), SiC MOSFETs are **unipolar**, conducting with one carrier type only. An IGBT's flood of carriers must drain out before it can turn off, which produces a **tail current** that wastes energy at every switching event; a unipolar switch has no tail, so EV inverters built on SiC can run at higher frequency, and the **passives** (the inductors and capacitors that smooth the chopped current) shrink as the frequency rises.

**Why 4H?** SiC exists in more than 200 **polytypes**, stacking sequences of the same Si-C bilayers.

> **Intuition: stacked oranges.** Stack identical layers of oranges. Each new layer can sit in one of two sets of hollows in the layer below, so the stacking pattern (ABAB, ABCABC, ABCB...) can differ while every layer is identical. SiC has hundreds of such patterns, and because the electrons feel the pattern, the different polytypes have different bandgaps and mobilities even though the chemistry is the same.

How the pattern reaches the electrons: the stacking sequence sets how far the crystal goes along the stacking direction before it repeats, four bilayers (~1.0 nm) in 4H and six (~1.5 nm) in 6H, and that repeat distance is what the electron waves have to fit. A longer repeat folds the allowed energies along that direction back on themselves more times, which changes both the size of the gap and the curvature of the bands, and the curvature sets the **effective mass** (how heavy a carrier acts inside the crystal; a sharply curved band means a light, fast carrier). The bandgap tracks the fraction of the stacking that is hexagonal (ABAB-like) rather than cubic (ABC-like): 3C is 0% hexagonal, 6H 33%, 4H 50%, and the gap rises in that order. The mobility difference is in the masses: an electron moving along the stacking direction has an effective mass of ~0.3 electron masses in 4H but ~2 in 6H, so 6H's electrons are several times heavier in exactly the direction a vertical device needs them to move.

The three polytypes that matter are 3C (cubic, bandgap 2.36 eV), 6H (hexagonal, 3.02 eV), and 4H (hexagonal, 3.26 eV, stacking ABCB). A power MOSFET is **vertical**: current enters the top face of the die and exits the bottom, so it flows along the stacking direction, the **c-axis**, and the mobility along that axis is what counts. 4H wins because it has the highest bandgap of the easily grown polytypes, an electron mobility of ~900–1,000 cm²/V·s that is more than twice 6H's, and little difference between mobility along the c-axis and within the layers (the **basal plane**); 6H's mobility along c is only ~100 cm²/V·s, which is fatal for a vertical device. Essentially all commercial SiC power devices are 4H, on wafers cut 4° off the flat stacking face (the (0001) plane; as in the SiGe section, round brackets name a plane and square brackets a direction) toward an in-plane direction (crystallographically [11-20], a direction lying within the layers). Tilting the cut turns the surface into a staircase of atomic steps; arriving atoms walk to a step edge and attach there, copying the stacking order beneath instead of starting a new (3C) pattern on a flat terrace. This is **step-flow growth**, and it is how the epitaxial layer inherits the substrate's polytype.

**Growth: physical vapor transport.** SiC never becomes a liquid of the same composition at practical pressures (it has no **congruent melting point**, the term for a solid that melts into a liquid of its own composition): heat it enough (~2,830 °C at 35 atm) and it falls apart into liquid silicon and solid carbon. So there is no Czochralski for SiC. The industrial process is **physical vapor transport (PVT)**, also called **seeded sublimation** or the modified Lely method. A graphite crucible, typically **induction-heated** (a coil's alternating magnetic field drives currents in the graphite, which heats it from inside), holds SiC powder source at the bottom at ~2,300–2,500 °C and a 4H seed crystal glued to the lid at a slightly lower temperature, ~2,100–2,300 °C, in argon at a few millibar to a few tens of millibar. The source sublimes into Si, Si2C and SiC2 vapor species, which diffuse up the small thermal gradient (~10–30 °C/cm) and condense on the cooler seed. The crucible is a black box: at 2,400 °C you cannot see or probe the growth interface, and the graphite itself slowly reacts. Everything is controlled indirectly through the coil, the pressure, and the axial gradient. The furnaces themselves are largely in-house designs at the substrate makers; PVA TePla (Germany) and several Chinese furnace makers also sell PVT systems on the merchant market (their shares are not published; treat any figure as ~order of magnitude).

The growth rate is ~100–500 µm/h, so a usable **boule** (the grown crystal ingot, before slicing) 30–50 mm long accumulates over roughly a week, i.e. several millimeters per day at best. Compare a Czochralski silicon puller producing a 2 m, 300 mm-diameter ingot in a couple of days. That single fact explains most of SiC's cost. The other cost drivers:

- **Defects.** Early SiC was riddled with **micropipes** (hollow-core screw dislocations, now driven below 0.1 cm⁻²), but the remaining zoo of **threading screw and edge dislocations (TSD/TED)**, lines running up through the crystal, at ~10^3 cm⁻², and **basal plane dislocations (BPD)**, dislocations lying within the stacking plane, still limits yield. A BPD is the insidious one. Every vertical MOSFET contains a **body diode** between its source and drain as a by-product of its structure: the p-type well that houses the channel sits on the n-type drift layer, and that boundary is a **p-n junction** (the meeting of p-type and n-type material, which passes current in one direction only, i.e. a diode). In an inverter, at the instant both switches in a leg are off, the motor current has nowhere to go and flows backwards through that diode (**freewheeling**); a diode conducts with both electrons and holes, and when both flow through a BPD it spreads into a sheet-like **stacking fault** (a slab where the stacking order is wrong) that raises the diode's forward voltage over years of operation. Polytype inclusions (patches of 6H or 15R, a polytype with a 15-layer repeat, in a 4H boule) and carbon inclusions scrap whole slices.
- **Slicing.** 4H-SiC has a Mohs hardness of ~9.5 (silicon is ~7; diamond is 10). Diamond wire sawing a 150 mm boule takes days and loses ~30–40% of the crystal as **kerf** (the width of crystal the saw turns to dust) and subsurface damage; the wafer then needs diamond **lapping** (grinding both faces flat between rotating plates with a diamond slurry) and a long CMP. Laser slicing (DISCO KABRA, Infineon's Cold Split from its Siltectra acquisition) uses a sub-surface laser modification layer and cleaves wafers with almost no kerf, raising wafers-per-boule substantially.
- **Diameter.** The industry standardized on 150 mm (6-inch) through the late 2010s and is transitioning to 200 mm. Wolfspeed's Mohawk Valley fab in New York (opened 2022) was the first 200 mm SiC device fab in volume; SICC, TankeBlue, Coherent and others now ship 200 mm substrates. Going to 200 mm gives ~1.8× the die per wafer, but a 200 mm boule needs a bigger crucible, a longer run, and even tighter gradient control to avoid cracking.

> **What can go wrong:** Micropipes, threading dislocations, BPDs, polytype inclusions and, at 200 mm, boule cracking from thermal stress are the growth failures; the BPD-to-stacking-fault conversion is the field failure, because it shows up as a slow drift of the body diode's forward voltage after the device has shipped. Substrates and epi layers are mapped by **ultraviolet photoluminescence** (the crystal is lit with UV and the light it re-emits is imaged; dislocations and stacking faults glow differently from good crystal) and by surface-defect inspection on dedicated SiC tools from KLA and Lasertec (SICA series). The standard mitigation for BPDs is a buffer epi layer grown before the drift layer, in which most BPDs bend over and convert into harmless threading edge dislocations before they can reach the device; polytype inclusions and cracks are caught at slice inspection and scrapped.

The consequence is a 150 mm 4H-SiC substrate that sold for well over $1,000 in 2022 and, after aggressive Chinese capacity additions, had fallen to roughly $400–800 by 2025; a 150 mm silicon wafer, for comparison, costs tens of dollars (Module 03), so even after the collapse the SiC substrate is tens of times dearer. The substrate plus epi has historically been 40–50% of the finished SiC MOSFET cost, compared to a few percent for a silicon wafer in a silicon device. Substrate market share has swung sharply: TrendForce (a Taiwanese market-research firm) put Wolfspeed at ~34% of the ~$1.0 billion n-type substrate market in 2024, with TankeBlue (Beijing) ~17%, SICC (Shandong) ~17% and Coherent (formerly II-VI) ~14%, and by 2025 the two Chinese suppliers were reported to have caught up with or passed Wolfspeed as 150 mm prices collapsed. Wolfspeed itself, having spent billions on the 200 mm transition, filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 on 30 June 2025 and emerged on 29 September 2025 with ~70% of its debt eliminated (as of late 2025). ROHM/SiCrystal, Resonac (formerly Showa Denko), SK Siltron and STMicroelectronics (via its Norstel acquisition) are the other substrate makers of note; Soitec's SmartSiC bonds a thin monocrystalline 4H layer onto a low-cost polycrystalline SiC handle (a support wafer of many small crystals, cheap to make) to stretch each boule further.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Because the substrate and epi are 40–50% of a SiC device's cost, the price of the wafer is the price of the switch, and the price of the switch is a visible slice of an EV's inverter. That is why the 200 mm transition (1.8× the die per boule) and the Chinese 150 mm price war matter beyond the substrate industry: they set whether SiC inverters spread from premium 800 V platforms to mass-market cars, and they are what broke Wolfspeed's balance sheet.

**The epi layer** is where the device actually lives: 5–100 µm of lightly doped n-type 4H-SiC grown by **hot-wall CVD** (the whole chamber, not just the wafer holder, is heated, so the gases decompose uniformly) at ~1,550–1,650 °C from silane and propane in hydrogen (with HCl or chlorinated precursors for higher rates), doped with nitrogen (n) or trimethylaluminum (p). A 1,200 V MOSFET needs ~10–12 µm at ~8 × 10^15 cm⁻³, about one nitrogen atom per 6 million SiC formula units; the lighter the doping the more voltage the layer holds but the more resistance it adds. Epi doping uniformity of ±5% and thickness control of ±2% set the breakdown voltage: a 2% thickness error on a 10 µm layer is 200 nm, roughly 25 V of blocking voltage by the V_B ≈ E_c·W/2 relation. Aixtron, LPE (now ASM), Nuflare and Tokyo Electron supply SiC epi reactors.

**Why EVs.** Tesla's 2017 Model 3 was the first mass-market EV with a full-SiC main inverter (STMicroelectronics modules), and the industry followed as 800 V architectures (Porsche Taycan, Hyundai E-GMP, Lucid, most new Chinese platforms) made SiC's advantages decisive. An 800 V battery needs switches rated ~1,200 V for headroom against voltage spikes; at 1,200 V, a silicon IGBT reduces conduction loss by filling its drift region with carriers, but those stored carriers cause a turn-off tail. SiC can provide a low-resistance unipolar MOSFET without that IGBT tail. Silicon MOSFETs also exist at this voltage; the comparison is about losses and suitable applications, not a prohibition on building them. A SiC inverter typically adds ~5–10% to driving range versus silicon IGBTs at the same battery size (industry estimates; the gain depends on the drive cycle), mostly by cutting switching and conduction losses at partial load, and it shrinks the cooling system. STMicro, Infineon, onsemi, Wolfspeed, ROHM and Mitsubishi Electric lead SiC device supply; automotive is ~70% of the SiC device market (industry estimates), with solar inverters, industrial drives and, increasingly, AI data-center power supplies as the rest.

## Gallium nitride: the heterostructure material

**Gallium nitride (GaN)** has a slightly larger bandgap (3.4 eV) and critical field (~3.3 MV/cm) than SiC and a direct bandgap (the electron and hole recombine straight into a photon, as defined in the first section) that made it the blue-LED and Blu-ray-laser material (the 2014 Nobel Prize). For electronics, its killer feature is not a bulk property but an interface: an **AlGaN/GaN heterojunction** (a junction between two different semiconductors, here aluminium gallium nitride on gallium nitride) forms a **two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG)** without any doping.

> **Intuition: charge gathers at a boundary.** In GaN, the centres of positive and negative charge within the crystal do not quite coincide. This built-in separation is called **spontaneous polarization**. Aluminium gallium nitride (AlGaN) has a different polarization, and stretching it to fit GaN changes that separation further; this strain effect is **piezoelectric polarization**. At the boundary, the two arrangements do not cancel, leaving a net positive sheet of charge. It attracts electrons into a layer a few nanometres thick. They can move along the boundary but are confined across its thickness. That is what “two-dimensional electron gas” means: mobile electrons inside a solid, not a pocket of gaseous material. The polarization creates the attraction; it does not create electrons or supply energy like a battery.

In numbers: GaN's wurtzite (hexagonal) crystal is strongly polar; the spontaneous polarization of the GaN, plus the piezoelectric polarization of the tensile-strained AlGaN barrier grown on top of it, creates a fixed sheet charge at the interface that is neutralized by ~10^13 cm⁻² of free electrons (about one electron per 100 surface atoms) in a triangular **quantum well** (a thin layer whose energy floor is lower than its neighbours', so carriers pool in it) a few nm thick. Because these electrons live in undoped GaN, spatially separated from any ionized **donors** (dopant atoms that have given away an electron and are now fixed charged obstacles), they never hit one, and their mobility is ~1,500–2,000 cm²/V·s, giving a **sheet resistance** of ~300–400 **Ω/sq** (the resistance of any square patch of the sheet, whatever its size: a 1 mm square and a 1 µm square have the same resistance). A transistor whose channel is this 2DEG is a **high-electron-mobility transistor (HEMT)**.

> **Worked example: the sheet resistance of a 2DEG, and why a GaN switch is so low-loss.**
> Sheet resistance is R_sh = 1 / (q · n_s · µ), where q = 1.6 × 10^-19 C is the electron charge, n_s the electrons per unit area and µ their mobility. With n_s = 10^13 cm⁻² and µ = 1,500–2,000 cm²/V·s: q n_s µ = 1.6 × 10^-19 × 10^13 × 1,500 ≈ 2.4 × 10^-3 S (siemens, the unit of conductance, 1/Ω) per square, so R_sh ≈ 420 Ω/sq; at 2,000 cm²/V·s, ≈ 310 Ω/sq. That is the ~300–400 Ω/sq quoted above. The resistance of a channel is R = R_sh × L / W (length over width, because a longer channel has more squares in series and a wider one has more in parallel). A power HEMT channel 1 µm long and 1 mm wide is 1/1,000 of a square, so R ≈ 400 × 0.001 ≈ 0.4 Ω, before the contacts and the drift region are added. A single, undoped, two-dimensional sheet of electrons carrying amps with a fraction of an ohm is why GaN HEMTs are so low-loss.

**Bulk GaN is nearly impossible to grow.** The equilibrium nitrogen pressure over GaN at its ~2,500 °C melting point is ~6 GPa (~60,000 atmospheres), so melt growth is impractical. Bulk GaN substrates (50–100 mm) exist from Sumitomo Chemical/SCIOCS, Mitsubishi Chemical and Kyocera via **hydride vapor phase epitaxy (HVPE)** (GaCl + NH3 at ~1,000 °C, ~100 µm/h, grown thick on a foreign seed and then separated) or the slower **ammonothermal** method (**supercritical** ammonia, ammonia heated and pressurized past the point where liquid and gas stop being distinct, so that it dissolves like a liquid and flows like a gas, at ~500–600 °C and ~100–300 MPa, a nitride analog of hydrothermal quartz growth, in which the crystal grows from a solution under pressure). They cost thousands of dollars each and are used for laser diodes and emerging vertical GaN power devices, not for volume.

So almost all GaN is a thin film grown by **metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD)** (CVD in which the metal arrives as a volatile organometallic molecule) on a foreign substrate. Trimethylgallium (TMGa) and ammonia flow over a substrate at ~1,000–1,100 °C in a hydrogen/nitrogen carrier at 50–500 mbar, with a **V/III ratio** (moles of nitrogen-source gas per mole of gallium-source gas, group V over group III of the periodic table) of several thousand because NH3 **cracks** so inefficiently: only a small fraction of the ammonia breaks into reactive nitrogen at 1,000 °C, so a large excess is fed. TMAl and TMIn add Al and In. Aixtron (Germany) and Veeco (US) are the MOCVD tool duopoly, with Japanese Taiyo Nippon Sanso and Chinese AMEC also shipping. The three substrate choices define three industries:

- **GaN-on-Si.** Grown on 150–200 mm **Si(111)**, the silicon crystal face with hexagonal symmetry, chosen because it matches GaN's hexagonal lattice better than the (100) face used for CMOS; 300 mm is in pilot at Infineon, imec and others. The lattice mismatch to Si is ~17% and the thermal expansion mismatch ~54%, so the wafer would **crack** on cooldown (GaN's thermal expansion coefficient is about twice silicon's, so on cooling from ~1,000 °C the GaN wants to shrink more than the thick silicon beneath it; the silicon holds it stretched, the film goes into tension, and tension cracks it) without a several-micron buffer: an AlN nucleation layer, step-graded or superlattice AlGaN layers that deliberately build compressive strain during growth to cancel the tensile stress that appears on cooling, then a ~1–2 µm carbon-doped GaN insulating buffer (carbon traps stray carriers so the buffer does not leak), then the GaN channel and the ~20 nm AlGaN barrier. The wafer can be processed in a standard silicon fab (this is what makes GaN cheap). These are the 650 V devices in most compact 65–240 W USB-C chargers since ~2019 (Navitas, Power Integrations, Innoscience, EPC, Infineon's GaN Systems, Texas Instruments), in solar micro-inverters, and now in 48 V data-center power stages for AI racks. The rating is 650 V because rectified mains is ~400 V and a switch needs headroom for spikes; 1,200 V is the EV-inverter class. Innoscience (China) operates the largest GaN-on-Si capacity on 200 mm wafers.
- **GaN-on-SiC.** **Semi-insulating** 4H-SiC (doped or compensated so it has almost no free carriers; a substrate that neither leaks nor loads the radio-frequency signal) has only ~3.5% lattice mismatch to GaN and superb thermal conductivity, so it is the substrate for RF power: 5G **massive-MIMO** base station amplifiers (antennas with dozens of independently driven elements, each needing its own amplifier), radar, and satellite links. Sumitomo Electric Device Innovations, Qorvo, MACOM (which bought Wolfspeed's RF business in 2023), Ampleon and NXP lead. The substrate costs more than the whole GaN-on-Si wafer.
- **GaN-on-sapphire.** Sapphire is crystalline Al2O3. For LEDs; cheap, insulating, and thermally poor.

A native AlGaN/GaN HEMT is **depletion-mode** (normally on: the 2DEG exists at zero gate voltage), which is unacceptable for a power switch that must fail safe. Industry solves this with a **p-GaN gate** (a p-doped GaN layer under the gate depletes the 2DEG at zero bias; EPC, Infineon/GaN Systems, Innoscience) or a **cascode** (a low-voltage silicon MOSFET in series that does the turning-off; Transphorm, now part of Renesas). Reliability under high-field stress was the long pole for automotive qualification: **dynamic on-resistance**, in which electrons trapped in the buffer after the device has blocked high voltage temporarily raise its on-resistance, measured with pulsed tests. It is now largely solved for 650 V; 1,200 V GaN remains contested territory with SiC.

> **What can go wrong:** Four things, in order of appearance. Wafer bow and cracking on cooldown, from the 54% thermal-expansion mismatch, are managed by the strain-engineered buffer and checked by measuring the bow of every wafer. **Meltback etching**: at growth temperature gallium reacts with and dissolves silicon wherever it touches it, so the AlN nucleation layer must seal the silicon completely before any gallium arrives. Dynamic on-resistance from buffer traps is found by pulsed current-voltage tests after a high-voltage stress and fixed by the carbon doping profile and buffer design. And the normally-on problem is a design fact, not a defect, solved by the p-GaN gate or the cascode.

## Gallium arsenide and indium phosphide: the III-V workhorses

**Gallium arsenide (GaAs)** was "the material of the future" for logic in the 1980s and never got there, because it has no good native oxide and its hole mobility is poor, so **complementary logic** (the CMOS pairing of n- and p-type transistors, which needs good holes as well as good electrons) does not work. Where it does win is anywhere you need a direct bandgap or very fast electrons: the power amplifiers and switches in every smartphone's RF front end (the chips between the antenna and the digital radio), from Skyworks, Qorvo and Broadcom, largely fabbed at WIN Semiconductors in Taiwan, the world's largest GaAs foundry; **VCSELs** (vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers, which emit out of the wafer face and are made by the thousands per wafer) at 850 and 940 nm for face recognition and short-reach datacom; and red/infrared LEDs. The RF chips are built as **heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs)** and **pHEMTs**. A **bipolar transistor** is the older transistor family: current flows from an **emitter** through a thin **base** into a **collector**, and a small current fed into the base controls a large emitter-to-collector current, so the base does the job the gate does in a MOSFET but is driven by current rather than by voltage. In an HBT the emitter is a wider-bandgap material than the base, so it can be doped heavily for speed while the wider gap stops the base's holes from leaking back into it. A pHEMT is a HEMT (the 2DEG transistor of the GaN section) with a thin strained InGaAs channel.

GaAs melts at 1,238 °C, but arsenic's vapor pressure at that temperature is ~1 atm and rising steeply, so an open crucible would boil off its arsenic. Two crystal growth methods dominate:

> **Intuition: a lid of molten glass.** An open GaAs melt loses arsenic the way a pan of water loses steam, and as it does the melt drifts gallium-rich and the crystal grows off-composition, full of arsenic vacancies. LEC puts a lid on the pan: a layer of molten boric oxide, a glass, floats on the melt, and arsenic vapour cannot cross a liquid it does not dissolve in. The inert gas above the glass is pressurized above arsenic's vapour pressure so the arsenic cannot bubble the lid off, and the seed is pulled up through the glass. VGF seals the pan instead of putting a lid on it: the whole charge sits in a closed crucible. In both, the crucible is pyrolytic boron nitride rather than quartz because quartz dissolves slightly into the melt and puts silicon, an n-type dopant in GaAs, into the crystal.

- **Liquid-encapsulated Czochralski (LEC).** A layer of molten boric oxide (B2O3), about a centimeter thick, floats on the GaAs melt in a **pyrolytic boron nitride (pBN)** crucible (a very pure ceramic deposited from gas, which does not react with the melt) and physically seals the arsenic in; the chamber is pressurized with inert gas to 2–20 atm. Pulling proceeds through the B2O3 layer at a few to ten millimetres per hour (~order of magnitude), so a 150 mm boule is a day or two of pulling. The steep thermal gradients produce a **dislocation density**, measured as **etch pit density (EPD)**, the count of pits revealed by an etch, one per dislocation, of 10^4–10^5 cm⁻², which is fine for semi-insulating RF substrates. Undoped LEC GaAs is naturally semi-insulating at ~10^8 Ω·cm (a doped silicon wafer is ~1–10 Ω·cm, so this is ten to a hundred million times more resistive, close to an insulator) because of the **EL2** defect, an arsenic atom sitting on a gallium site whose energy level sits mid-gap and swallows stray carriers, so the crystal conducts almost nothing, ideal for microwave isolation.
- **Vertical gradient freeze (VGF)** and its close cousin **vertical Bridgman (VB)**. The charge sits in a sealed pBN crucible inside a multi-zone furnace; a gentle temperature gradient is walked upward so the melt freezes from a seed at the bottom, at a few millimetres per hour over a run of several days (~order of magnitude). The low gradient gives EPD below 10^3 cm⁻², which matters for optoelectronics: dislocations are **non-radiative recombination centres**, places where an electron and hole meet and release heat instead of light, and they kill laser lifetime. AXT (US-headquartered, China-based production), Freiberger Compound Materials (Germany) and Sumitomo Electric are the main merchant suppliers; 150 mm is standard, 200 mm has been demonstrated.

**Indium phosphide (InP)** is the substrate of long-haul and datacenter optics. The **lattice-matched ternaries and quaternaries** on InP (three- and four-element alloys whose composition is tuned so their lattice constant equals InP's: In0.53Ga0.47As, InGaAsP, InAlAs) cover the 1,310 nm and 1,550 nm telecom windows where silica fiber has minimum **dispersion** (different colours travelling at different speeds and smearing a pulse) and minimum loss. InP is worse to grow than GaAs: the phosphorus vapor pressure at its 1,062 °C melting point is ~27 atm, so LEC needs a high-pressure puller, and the crystal is brittle and **twins** easily (a twin is a region where the stacking mirror-flips, ruining the crystal). Standard diameters are only 75–100 mm, with 150 mm in development at Sumitomo Electric and AXT; the small wafer, the difficult growth, and a market that is tiny compared with silicon mean an InP wafer costs hundreds of dollars per square inch: a 100 mm InP wafer (~12 square inches) is a few thousand dollars (~order of magnitude), versus ~$100–150 for a 300 mm silicon wafer with nine times the area (Module 03). Sumitomo Electric, AXT, JX Advanced Metals and InPACT (France) are essentially the entire supply.

The substrate is only the platform. The layers that make the device, the HBT's emitter and base, the pHEMT's InGaAs channel, the dozens of alternating mirror layers of a VCSEL, the quantum wells of an InP laser, are grown on it by **MOCVD** (the same Aixtron and Veeco reactor class as GaN, fed with TMGa, TMIn, arsine and phosphine at ~600–750 °C) or, where the layers must be thinnest and most abrupt, by **molecular beam epitaxy (MBE)**, in which the elements are evaporated from heated cells in ultra-high vacuum and the beams of atoms land on the substrate one atomic layer at a time, at ~1 µm/h (Veeco and Riber build the tools). Epi is where most of the value is added: a finished laser epiwafer is worth many times the bare substrate beneath it.

> **What can go wrong:** Twinning during the pull (the crystal flips its stacking and the boule is scrapped from that point); loss of arsenic or phosphorus from the melt if the encapsulant or the pressure fails, which shifts the composition and leaves the crystal full of vacancies; and dislocation density above the customer's limit. Each is caught by the same routine: an etch-pit count on test slices for EPD, **X-ray topography** (imaging the whole wafer in diffracted X-rays, which shows strain and twins as contrast) for twins and slip, and resistivity mapping for semi-insulating material.

> **Worked example: what a square centimetre of InP costs, and why the wafer is not the bottleneck.**
> A 100 mm InP wafer has an area of π × (5 cm)² ≈ 78.5 cm² (~12 square inches). At ~$2,500 (mid-range of "a few thousand dollars"; ~order of magnitude) that is ~$32 per cm². A 300 mm silicon wafer is 707 cm² for ~$100–150, i.e. ~$0.15–0.20 per cm²: InP substrate area costs on the order of 150–200× more than silicon. Now put lasers on it. A DFB or EML laser die is roughly 0.3 mm × 1 mm, ~0.003 cm², so the substrate under one laser costs ~$0.10; even after edge exclusion and yield loss the substrate is cents per laser, a rounding error in a transceiver that sells for on the order of a thousand dollars. The same 78.5 cm² holds only ~20,000 such die sites, a ninth of what a 300 mm wafer would hold, and the world's InP device output comes off 75–100 mm wafers through a handful of MOCVD reactors and fabs. That is why the 2024–2025 shortage was of InP laser capacity rather than InP cost: the constraint was wafers per month through those reactors and fabs, not dollars per wafer.

Why this matters for AI: NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 (a rack of 72 GPUs wired together to act as one machine) and the **scale-out network** beyond it (the switches and fibre that link racks into a cluster) are stitched together with 800 Gb/s **pluggable optical transceivers** (the module that plugs into a switch port and converts electrical bits to light and back) moving to 1.6 Tb/s. An 800G module carries eight 100 Gb/s lanes (or four at 200 Gb/s); a 1.6T module carries eight 200 Gb/s lanes. For many single-mode fibre links at these rates, the source is an InP **electro-absorption modulated laser (EML)** (a laser with a built-in shutter that switches the light rather than the laser itself) or a directly modulated **DFB laser** (a laser with a built-in grating that selects one wavelength) from Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Mitsubishi Electric or Sumitomo Electric; silicon-photonics transceivers commonly pair their silicon optical circuits with an InP continuous-wave laser source. “Silicon photonics” describes the optical circuit platform, not a promise that every component is made of silicon. In 2024–2025 InP laser and EML capacity, not the switch silicon (the chip that routes the packets), was reported to be one of the tightest constraints on shipping AI-cluster networking, and both Lumentum and Coherent announced capacity expansions and a move to larger InP wafer diameters. InP also builds the fastest transistors anywhere (InP HBTs and HEMTs with f_max, the highest frequency at which the transistor still gives power gain, above 1 THz), used in test-and-measurement front ends and 6G research.

> **Why it matters downstream:** InP is an important light-source material for the single-mode data links discussed here, including many silicon-photonics designs. But counting every optical link as an InP laser would be wrong: short-reach links can instead use GaAs VCSELs. The connection to Module 19 is therefore an architecture question: which source does each kind of link use? Silicon’s inefficient direct light emission explains the demand for other source materials; it does not mean no silicon laser has ever existed.

## Germanium

This section is a short side note: germanium's production role in logic is as the alloy partner in SiGe, covered above, and its own wafers serve niche markets. **Germanium** was the semiconductor of the first transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) and lost to silicon in the early 1960s for exactly the reasons that still limit it: its oxide is a disaster (GeO2 is water-soluble and GeO sublimes at ~400 °C, so there is no stable passivation), and because its gap is half silicon's, heat alone frees ~1,000× more carriers in Ge than in Si, so a Ge transistor leaks badly at the 85 °C a chip runs at. It also takes less doping to make Ge behave like a metal, which limits how it can be doped. What it has is mobility: ~3,900 cm²/V·s for electrons and ~1,900 cm²/V·s for holes, the highest hole mobility of any common semiconductor, which is why high-Ge-fraction SiGe is the standard pMOS booster and pure-Ge channels keep reappearing on roadmaps. Ge's other roles today: the substrate for triple-junction GaAs/InGaP solar cells on satellites (lattice-matched to GaAs), infrared optics, GeO2 as the core dopant of essentially all optical fiber (the germanium raises the refractive index of the fiber core so the light is guided; one of the largest uses by tonnage), Ge-on-Si photodetectors in silicon photonics (Ge absorbs at 1,310 and 1,550 nm; Si does not), and SiGe heterojunction bipolar transistors.

Ge wafers themselves are easy to grow, which is the irony of the material: Ge melts cleanly at 938 °C, so Czochralski pulling works, in graphite or quartz crucibles under hydrogen or nitrogen, and dislocation-free 100–150 mm Ge crystals are routine. The refining chain starts from the germanium that concentrates in the dusts and residues of zinc smelters and some coal-fired power stations: the concentrate is chlorinated to volatile GeCl4, which is distilled to leave almost everything else behind, hydrolysed to GeO2, reduced in hydrogen to metal, and finally **zone-refined** (a short molten zone is swept along the bar many times and drags the impurities with it, because most impurities prefer the liquid) to 9N and beyond; the same route, pushed further, gives the ~10^10 impurity atoms per cm³ (about one atom in 4 × 10^12) of detector-grade Ge used in gamma-ray spectrometers, the purest bulk semiconductor made. Umicore (Belgium, with plants in the US) and AXT (US/China) are the merchant wafer suppliers, alongside Chinese refiners. What goes wrong is the oxide: a Ge surface left in air or rinsed in water loses its GeO2, so any Ge channel must be **passivated** another way, either by growing a few atomic layers of silicon on it by epitaxy and oxidising that (a **Si cap**), or by forming a thin GeO2 with ozone or plasma and immediately sealing it under ALD Al2O3 before it can dissolve or desorb; the density of charge traps at that interface is what decides whether a Ge pMOS transistor beats a strained-SiGe one.

Ge is a byproduct of zinc refining and coal fly ash; China produces roughly 60–70% of refined Ge (estimates vary), with suppliers including Yunnan Germanium (China), whose catalogue lists zone-refined germanium ingots, and Umicore (Belgium), active in refining and recycling. China put Ga and Ge under export licensing in August 2023 and banned exports to the US in December 2024; the Ge spot price roughly doubled through 2024. The same controls cover gallium, of which China refines ~90–98% by most estimates (from alumina production), which is the raw input of GaN and GaAs.

## The periodic table at 2 nm and below

This section is a forward-looking survey of research options, not a core process; it stays qualitative and does not attempt the depth of the SiC and GaN sections. TSMC N2, Samsung SF2 and Intel 18A all use silicon nanosheets with SiGe stressors, and the immediate successors (TSMC A16 with **backside power**, power wiring delivered from the back of the wafer rather than through the crowded front-side layers, then A14, the 1.4 nm-class node; Intel 14A, Intel's equivalent) do too. Where does the channel material go after that? imec's public roadmap (as of 2024–2025) runs nanosheet → **forksheet** (n and p nanosheets pushed together against a dielectric wall so they take less area) → **CFET** (complementary FET, nMOS stacked on pMOS, around 2030+) with silicon channels, and puts non-silicon channels around 2035 and beyond. The candidates:

> **Intuition: the gate's grip.** At a gate length of 10 nm the gate must control the whole channel from the outside. If the channel is thick, the far side escapes its grip and current leaks when the transistor should be off; this is what **electrostatic control** means. Thinning silicon to a few atoms restores the grip but its surface roughness then scatters carriers and kills mobility. A MoS2 sheet is naturally three atoms thick with a smooth surface and no broken bonds, so it keeps the grip without paying the roughness penalty.

- **High-Ge SiGe and Ge pMOS channels**, discussed above; the most conservative option.
- **2D transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)**: MoS2, WS2, WSe2. A single MoS2 monolayer is 0.65 nm thick, has a direct ~1.8 eV bandgap, and, because it has no **dangling bonds** (unsatisfied bonds at a surface) and is atomically thin, it keeps electrostatic control at gate lengths below 10 nm where a silicon sheet would have to be thinned to a few atoms and lose its mobility to surface scattering. Measured device mobilities are only ~10–100 cm²/V·s, but at such short channels transport is **quasi-ballistic** (a carrier crosses the channel with few or no collisions) and mobility matters less than **injection velocity** (how fast carriers enter the channel from the source). The showstopper has been **contact resistance**, quoted in **Ω·µm** (resistance times contact width, so devices of different widths can be compared): metals form Schottky contacts to MoS2 whose barrier is fixed by **Fermi-level pinning** (the metal's electrons lock to defect states at the interface, so whichever metal you choose, the same barrier appears). The 2021 TSMC/MIT result using semimetal bismuth contacts (a semimetal has too few states to pin) and later antimony cut contact resistance to ~100 Ω·µm and revived the field. TMDs are grown by MOCVD on sapphire at 700–1,000 °C and transferred, or grown directly at 300 mm on oxide at lower temperature with worse quality; Intel and TSMC showed gate-all-around 2D nanosheet transistors and the first 2D CMOS inverters (the simplest logic gate, one nMOS and one pMOS whose output is the opposite of its input; not the power inverter of the SiC section) and ring oscillators (a loop of an odd number of inverters that oscillates on its own; its frequency is the standard first benchmark of a new transistor's speed) at **IEDM** (the International Electron Devices Meeting, the annual conference where new transistors are first disclosed) around 2023–2024. Wafer-scale uniformity, transfer without wrinkles, and a compatible gate dielectric (the sheet has no native oxide and no dangling bonds on which an **atomic layer deposition (ALD)** film, the layer-by-layer gas-phase coating explained in the precursors section, can start to grow) remained unsolved for manufacturing as of 2025.
- **Oxide semiconductors** such as IGZO (indium gallium zinc oxide), already used in display backplanes; with ~10 cm²/V·s mobility they are useless for logic but are the leading candidate for the **DRAM access transistor** (**DRAM**, dynamic random-access memory, is a computer's main memory, each bit stored as charge on a tiny capacitor; the access transistor is the switch that connects each cell's capacitor to its **bit line**, the column wire that reads and writes it; ultra-low leakage lets a cell hold charge for seconds) and for low-temperature transistors in the **BEOL** (back end of line, the wiring levels built above the transistors).
- **Carbon nanotubes** and **III-V channels** (InGaAs nMOS, the 2010s' favorite, abandoned around 2016–2018 for lack of a stable oxide and for mismatch defects) round out the list.

The pattern is consistent: silicon's competitors always win on paper, and silicon's ecosystem always wins on defect density and integration cost, until the geometry itself makes silicon impossible.

## Photoresists

A **photoresist** is a polymer solution that is spin-coated to 30–300 nm, baked, exposed, and developed so that the pattern on the mask becomes a pattern in the resist, which then masks the etch or implant beneath. The **scanner** is the lithography tool that projects the mask image onto the wafer (Module 08); its **dose** is the light energy per unit area it delivers, in mJ/cm² (millijoules per square centimetre); **spin coating** means dropping the liquid onto a spinning wafer so it spreads into a uniform film; an **implant** fires dopant ions into the wafer through the resist openings; a **plasma etch** removes material with the reactive ions of an electrically excited gas (Module 09). The resist must do three contradictory things: respond to light strongly enough to be patterned at a dose the scanner can deliver in a fraction of a second, resolve features smaller than the wavelength that exposed it, and then stand up to a plasma etch as if it were rock. Module 07 covers the chemistry in depth; here the concern is supply, and the reason supply is the problem is that a resist is not a chemical but a recipe, tuned over years to one fab's tools and never quite reproducible by anyone else.

The four exposure generations use four chemistries. **i-line (365 nm)** resists are a phenolic resin (**novolac**) plus a **dissolution inhibitor**, a molecule (diazonaphthoquinone, DNQ) that keeps the resin from dissolving in the developer until light converts it to an acid that makes exposed regions soluble in the alkaline **developer** (the liquid that washes away the exposed or unexposed resist). **KrF (248 nm)** introduced **chemically amplified resists (CARs)**: a polymer backbone whose side groups are "protected" (capped so the polymer will not dissolve), plus a **photoacid generator (PAG)** that releases an acid molecule when it absorbs a photon.

> **Intuition: one photon, hundreds of unlocks.** In a CAR one photon unlocks one acid molecule. During the bake that follows exposure, that acid is a catalyst: it unlocks hundreds of protecting groups on the polymer, one after another, and is not consumed. So the resist sees a hundred photons' worth of chemical change for every photon absorbed, which is why it can be exposed a hundred times faster. The price is that the acid also wanders a few nanometres during the bake, blurring the edge of every feature.

That is why sensitivity is amplified ~100× at the cost of acid-diffusion blur. **ArF (193 nm)** resists could not use benzene-ring (**aromatic**) polymers, which absorb 193 nm light, so they moved to acrylic backbones with bulky cage-shaped hydrocarbon side groups for etch resistance and adhesion, plus fluorinated additives for immersion (exposure through a film of water on the wafer, Module 07). **EUV (13.5 nm)** resists come in two families: CARs re-engineered for a regime where each 92 eV photon generates a cascade of **secondary electrons** (electrons knocked loose by the photon, which do the actual chemistry) and only ~10–20 photons land per nm², and **metal-oxide resists (MORs)**, in which clusters of tin and oxygen atoms carrying organic side groups (the Inpria chemistry, acquired by JSR in 2021 for $514 million) absorb EUV ~4–5× more strongly than carbon-based polymers and **crosslink** directly (the clusters bond to one another where exposed, so no acid is needed), giving better resolution and **line-edge roughness** (the nanometre-scale wobble of a printed edge) at 15–25 nm film thickness.

> **Worked example: how many photons make an EUV feature?**
> An EUV photon carries 92 eV = 92 × 1.6 × 10^-19 J ≈ 1.47 × 10^-17 J. A dose of 15–30 mJ/cm² is 0.015–0.030 J/cm², so the photon count is 0.015 / 1.47 × 10^-17 ≈ 1.0 × 10^15 to 2.0 × 10^15 per cm². A square centimetre is 10^14 nm², so that is ~10–20 photons per nm². A 16 nm × 16 nm contact hole receives ~2,500–5,000 photons; the random (Poisson) fluctuation in that count is √N, so √2,500 / 2,500 ≈ 2% and √5,000 / 5,000 ≈ 1.4%, which is tolerable. But the edge of the feature is decided by the last nanometre, and each square nanometre along the edge receives only ~15 photons ± 4. The edge position therefore wobbles by a nanometre or more from place to place, and once in every few billion holes the fluctuation is large enough that the hole does not open at all, or two neighbours merge. This is what **stochastic** defects means: the pattern is a statistical object because the light arrives in too few packets, and it is why EUV resist chemistry is about absorbing every photon and why dose (and so throughput, Module 08) cannot simply be cut.

**Lam Research's dry resist** goes one step further: a tin-based organometallic precursor is deposited as a film by vapor-phase deposition instead of spin coating, exposed, then dry-developed in a plasma, eliminating the solvent, the coat non-uniformity, and much of the **pattern collapse** (tall thin resist lines pulled over by the surface tension of the rinse water as it dries); as of ~2025 it was in qualification and early use at memory makers, with details unpublished. A leading-edge fab uses dozens of distinct resist products: several EUV resists, ArF immersion resists for each critical layer, KrF for mid-layers, thick i-line resists for implant masking, plus the **ancillaries**: **bottom anti-reflective coatings (BARC)** (a film under the resist that absorbs the light that would otherwise reflect off the wafer and re-expose the resist from below), spin-on carbon and spin-on glass **hardmask** layers (films that take the pattern from a thin resist and are tough enough to mask a deep etch), **top coats** (a film over the resist that keeps immersion water out of it), and the developers and rinse solvents to go with them. Typical numbers, all approximate: film thicknesses of ~20–30 nm for EUV, ~100 nm for ArF immersion, up to several microns for i-line implant masks; doses of ~15–30 mJ/cm² for EUV and ~20–40 mJ/cm² for ArF; filtration to remove particles below ~10 nm; metals specified at the sub-ppb level.

Why so concentrated? Resist is a product of tacit knowledge: dozens of proprietary monomers, PAGs, quenchers (bases that mop up stray acid to sharpen the edge) and additives, synthesized in-house at multi-kilogram scale, filtered to remove sub-10 nm particles and metal ions to the sub-ppb (hundreds of ppt) level, blended to a formulation co-developed over years with one fab's litho team, and qualified layer by layer. An EUV resist costs on the order of several thousand dollars per liter versus roughly a thousand per liter for ArF immersion, and a 300 mm wafer takes ~1 mL per coat after modern low-dispense optimization (most of which spins off), so resist is not a huge line item per wafer; the issue is not price but irreplaceability.

The supply base is Japanese to a degree matched by no other category. **JSR** (taken private by the state-backed Japan Investment Corporation in 2024), **Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK)**, **Shin-Etsu Chemical**, **Fujifilm Electronic Materials** and **Sumitomo Chemical** hold, between them, roughly 90% of the world market for KrF/ArF resists and an even larger share of EUV resists, where TOK, JSR and Shin-Etsu are the qualified suppliers at TSMC, Samsung and Intel. **DuPont** (whose electronics business was spun off as the independent company Qnity Electronics in late 2025) and **Merck KGaA** (AZ Electronic Materials) are the non-Japanese players, strong in i-line, KrF, BARC and ancillaries.

> **What can go wrong:** Stochastic missing or merged contacts and bridged lines, from the photon statistics above; pattern collapse when tall narrow lines are pulled over by the drying rinse; particles and micro-bridges from an agglomerate or a gel in the resist; and lot-to-lot drift in sensitivity that shifts every feature width. Detection is by **CD-SEM** (a scanning electron microscope that measures feature widths) on sampled sites and, for the rare stochastic failure, by full-die e-beam inspection (Module 13). Mitigations are higher dose (at a throughput cost), metal-oxide resists that absorb more per nanometre, dry development that removes the rinse-water surface tension, and tighter incoming-lot qualification.

> **Why it matters downstream:** A resist's dose sets the scanner's throughput. Wafers per hour on an EUV tool are roughly source power divided by the dose the resist needs (Module 08); a resist that needs 30 mJ/cm² instead of 20 costs a third of the output of a $200 million machine. That is why resist sensitivity, not just resolution, is negotiated between the chemical company and the fab, and why a resist that is 1 mL per wafer can decide the economics of the whole line.

That irreplaceability became geopolitical on 1 July 2019, when Japan removed South Korea from its export "white list" and required individual licenses for three items: photoresist (specifically EUV-relevant resists), high-purity hydrogen fluoride, and fluorinated polyimide (for OLED displays). Samsung and SK hynix scrambled: Soulbrain and RAM Technology accelerated **12N** (twelve nines, 99.9999999999%) HF production in Korea, Samsung invested in Dongjin and imported resist via Belgium (JSR's JV with imec), and the Korean government launched a materials-localization program. Licenses were in practice issued after a few weeks and the restriction was formally lifted in 2023, but the episode showed that a single upstream country could halt the world's memory output with a customs form.

## Gases

A plasma etcher or a CVD chamber is a chemistry set whose only inputs are gases, so the gases are the recipe. A part-per-million of the wrong molecule in the wrong bottle becomes a defect: a ppm of water in tungsten hexafluoride reacts to make HF, which pits the stainless-steel line and forms tungsten-oxide particles that land on the wafer; a few ppm of oxygen in the nitrogen that purges every furnace grows an unwanted oxide on every wafer in it. The gas itself is usually cheap and often a byproduct of some other industry; what a fab pays for is the last four orders of magnitude of purification, the certification of every cylinder, and a delivery system that adds nothing on the way to the tool.

A fab uses on the order of 40–50 different gases, split between **bulk gases** (nitrogen, oxygen, argon, hydrogen, helium) delivered by pipeline or on-site plant at thousands of cubic meters per hour, and **specialty gases** (a few hundred cylinders' worth of etchants, deposition precursors, dopants and cleaning agents) delivered in cylinders, ton containers or ISO tanks (standard shipping containers built as tanks).

> **Intuition: the delivery train.** Follow one specialty gas from the dock to the wafer. The cylinder sits in a ventilated steel **gas cabinet** with its own exhaust and gas detectors. When a cylinder is swapped, the cabinet automatically purges the connection with dozens of cycles of vacuum and inert gas before the new cylinder is opened, so no air is carried in. The line runs to a **valve manifold box** that splits one supply to several tools, then to a **point-of-use purifier** and a **mass-flow controller** (a valve that meters the gas by measuring its heat capacity, to a fraction of a percent) at the tool. Every wetted surface is **electropolished 316L stainless steel**, a low-carbon steel polished electrochemically to a mirror so it neither outgasses nor sheds particles, in double-contained tubing. The gas at the chamber is the cylinder's gas plus nothing.

**Nitrogen** is the largest by volume: a large 300 mm fab consumes on the order of 30,000–60,000 Nm³/h (a Nm³ is a cubic metre of gas at standard temperature and pressure, i.e. a fixed number of molecules), used for purging every tool, **load lock** (the airlock between the cleanroom and a vacuum chamber) and **FOUP** (the sealed pod that carries 25 wafers between tools), as a carrier and dilution gas, and for blanketing chemical tanks. At that scale it is always made on-site by an **air separation unit (ASU)** owned and operated by Air Liquide, Linde, Air Products or Nippon Sanso next to the fab, cryogenically distilling air and then purifying the N2 to 99.9999%+ (sub-ppb O2, H2O and hydrocarbons) with catalytic and **getter** purifiers (beds of a reactive metal that chemically swallow traces of oxygen and water). Argon comes from the same ASU (it is ~0.93% of air); hydrogen and oxygen are either piped from a nearby industrial plant or generated on-site. **Helium** is used as a thermal-contact gas on the backside of wafers in etch and implant **chucks** (the plate that holds the wafer inside the tool; a thin layer of helium between the wafer and the cooled chuck conducts heat across the gap far better than vacuum does), as a leak-detection tracer, and as a carrier; it is extracted from a few natural-gas fields (Qatar, the US, Russia's Amur project, Algeria) and its price spikes every few years when one of them hiccups.

> **Worked example: how much nitrogen is that?**
> Take a fab drawing 40,000 Nm³/h. Nitrogen at standard conditions has a density of ~1.25 kg/Nm³, so the fab consumes 40,000 × 1.25 = 50,000 kg, or 50 tonnes, every hour. Over a year of 8,760 hours that is 50 × 8,760 ≈ 440,000 tonnes of nitrogen. A road tanker of liquid nitrogen carries ~20 tonnes, so trucking it would mean a tanker every 25 minutes, around the clock, forever. That is why the ASU is built next door, why it is sized in tonnes per day, and why a fab cannot run on delivered liquid nitrogen for more than the few hours its backup tanks last.

What a gas does, in four sentences, before the table. **Etchants** react with the surface to turn it into volatile molecules that the pump removes; an **anisotropic** etch cuts straight down and not sideways, and a **polymerizing** etchant deposits a Teflon-like film on the sidewalls as it goes, which protects them so the etch stays vertical. **Deposition gases** arrive at a hot or plasma-lit surface and leave atoms behind. **Dopant hydrides** carry one boron, phosphorus or arsenic atom per molecule into an epi layer or an implanter's ion source. **Cleaning gases** dissolve the film that coated the chamber walls during the last deposition; in **remote-plasma** cleaning the gas is dissociated in a side chamber so that reactive atoms, not energetic ions, do the cleaning and the chamber parts survive.

The structures named in the Use column: **contacts**, **plugs** and **vias** are the vertical metal connections, a contact from the transistor up to the first wiring level, a via from one wiring level to the next, each a hole filled with metal (the plug); **wordlines** are the row-select wires of a memory array; **3D NAND** is flash memory built as a vertical stack of 200+ layers; **STI** is shallow-trench isolation, the oxide-filled trenches between neighbouring transistors; **LPCVD** is low-pressure CVD, done in a furnace on a batch of 100+ wafers; **poly-Si** and **a-Si** are polycrystalline and amorphous silicon (many small crystals, or no crystalline order at all).

**GWP** is global-warming potential, the warming effect of a kilogram of the gas relative to a kilogram of CO2 over 100 years; NF3 and SF6 are among the strongest greenhouse gases known, which is why fabs abate their exhaust. **Sub-atmospheric cylinders** (Entegris SDS and Linde SAGE) store the toxic dopant hydrides adsorbed on a solid inside the cylinder at below atmospheric pressure, so that a leak sucks air in rather than pushing arsine out.

**Neon and Ukraine.** **Deep ultraviolet (DUV)** is the collective name for the KrF and ArF generations, and their **excimer lasers** (pulsed gas lasers whose light comes from a short-lived Kr-F or Ar-F molecule; Cymer, owned by ASML, and Gigaphoton, owned by Komatsu) fill their discharge tube with a premix that is ~96–98% neon with ~1–3% argon or krypton and ~0.1% fluorine, replaced continuously as the fluorine is consumed; the neon is the inert **buffer gas** that carries the electrical discharge. Neon is 18 ppm of air and is only economical to extract as a side stream of the largest cryogenic ASUs, which historically meant Soviet-era steel plants. Two Ukrainian purifiers, Ingas in Mariupol and Cryoin in Odesa, took crude neon from those plants and refined it to 99.999% laser grade, supplying an estimated 45–54% of the world's semiconductor-grade neon. Both shut down when Russia invaded in February–March 2022. Spot neon prices rose more than tenfold; Chinese ASU operators and Linde expanded purification; and Samsung, SK hynix and TSMC sharply reduced consumption through laser gas-recycling systems (Cymer and Gigaphoton both offer neon reclaim that cuts fresh-gas need by ~50%). Ukraine's share was already declining after the 2014 Crimea shock had prompted diversification; by 2024 China was reported to supply the majority of new neon. The lesson generalizes: many specialty gases are byproducts of some other industry's largest plants, and the purification step, not the raw gas, is the chokepoint.

The specialty-gas suppliers are a mixed group. **Air Liquide** (France) is also the largest supplier of on-site bulk plants; **SK Specialty** (Korea, formerly SK Materials) is the world's largest NF3 producer at ~40% share and a major WF6 producer; **Merck KGaA** bought Versum Materials in 2019 for $6.5 billion; **Resonac** (Japan, formerly Showa Denko) is strong in HBr, Cl2 and etch gases. Purity is specified at 99.999–99.9999% with individual contaminants at ppb levels, and cylinders are electropolished, baked, and certified individually.

> **What can go wrong:** The classic failures are all at the boundaries. And the supply-side failure is a shock upstream: the 2022 neon shutdown, or a helium spike when a single gas field goes down for maintenance, neither of which any in-fab monitoring can prevent, only inventory and dual sourcing can.

## Wet chemicals

The wafer is bathed on the order of 200 times in a leading-edge flow, and every bath must take particles and metals off the surface without leaving any of its own behind. That is the whole problem: a chemical clean enough to remove a foreign atom must itself be cleaner than the surface it is cleaning.

> **Intuition: why a trace of metal matters.** A metal atom left on the surface diffuses into the silicon during the next hot step. Iron and copper move fast in silicon and cluster into tiny precipitates that short the gate oxide or leak current across a p-n junction (the p-to-n boundary defined in the SiC section, which every transistor's source and drain form with the channel); sodium ions sit in the oxide and shift the voltage at which the gate switches. Fabs therefore specify surface metals below ~10^10 atoms per cm², about one foreign atom per 100,000 surface atoms, and the only way a bath can meet that is to carry far less metal than the surface is allowed to hold.

> **Worked example: 10 ppt of iron in one litre of rinse water.**
> One litre of water is 1,000 g, so 10 ppt of iron is 10 × 10^-12 × 1,000 g = 10^-8 g = 10 ng. Iron's atomic mass is 55.85 g/mol, so that is 10^-8 / 55.85 ≈ 1.8 × 10^-10 mol, or 1.8 × 10^-10 × 6.0 × 10^23 ≈ 1.1 × 10^14 iron atoms in the litre. Suppose only 0.1% of them stick to the 707 cm² face of a 300 mm wafer as it passes through: 1.1 × 10^11 atoms / 707 cm² ≈ 1.5 × 10^8 atoms per cm², already a few percent of the 10^10 cm⁻² budget from one litre of one rinse. That is why 10 ppt is the right order of magnitude for a solution the wafer sits in, and why the spec is written per metal, not in total.

The core chemistries are decades old but the purity has tightened by four orders of magnitude:

- **SPM** (sulfuric-peroxide mixture, nicknamed **piranha**): H2SO4:H2O2 in ~4:1 at 120–150 °C, strips photoresist and organics. Sulfuric acid is the highest-tonnage fab chemical; a large fab uses thousands of tonnes per year and increasingly recycles it on-site by thermal reconcentration.
- **SC-1 (APM)**, the ammonia-peroxide mixture: NH4OH:H2O2:H2O in ~1:1:5 at 60–75 °C, removes particles and organics. The alkaline solution slightly etches the oxide beneath a particle, and because both the particle and the wafer carry a thin charged skin of the same sign in an alkaline bath (the **electrostatic double layer**), they repel, and the loosened particle floats away. Followed by **SC-2 (HPM)**, the hydrochloric-peroxide mixture: HCl:H2O2:H2O ~1:1:6, removes metals as soluble chlorides. Together they are the **RCA clean** (1965, Werner Kern at RCA).
- **DHF**: dilute HF at 0.5–1% removes native oxide and leaves a **hydrogen-terminated**, **hydrophobic** surface: every surface silicon atom is capped with a hydrogen, water beads off, and the surface attracts particles from the air until it is re-oxidised, which is why an HF-last wafer is processed quickly. **BOE** (buffered oxide etch: HF buffered with NH4F, which holds the etch rate steady as the acid is consumed) etches oxide at a controlled ~100 nm/min in the standard 6:1 mix (six volumes of 40% NH4F solution to one of 49% HF).
- **TMAH** (tetramethylammonium hydroxide) at 2.38 wt% is the universal developer for **positive resists** (resists in which the exposed regions become soluble and wash away, the common case); it is also, at higher concentration, an anisotropic Si etchant and it is acutely toxic through the skin.
- **IPA** (isopropanol) for **Marangoni** or vapor drying: a wafer pulled slowly from water into an IPA-vapor atmosphere dries without watermarks because the IPA lowers the surface tension at the top of the water film and the resulting surface-tension gradient pulls the water off the wafer rather than letting it evaporate in place.
- Plus hot phosphoric acid (H3PO4 at ~160 °C for SiN removal, an enormous volume consumer in 3D NAND where hundreds of nitride layers are stripped), HNO3, acetic acid, ozonated water, and a growing number of proprietary formulated cleans (post-CMP cleans, post-etch residue removers, selective etches for nanosheet release).

The specification is metals. SEMI's C-series standards define grades; the tightest ("Grade 5"/"UHP") call for each of ~30 metals below 10 ppt, particles below a few per mL at 0.1 µm or smaller, and anions (negatively charged ions such as chloride, sulfate, nitrate) at ppb. A few thousand sodium ions per square micron in the gate oxide (~10^11 per cm²) is a measurable shift in a transistor's **threshold voltage** (the gate voltage at which it turns on), and an iron precipitate under the gate is a leakage site. Purification is by distillation (sulfuric, HF), **sub-boiling distillation** (evaporating the liquid below its boiling point so no droplets carry impurities over), **ion exchange** (resin beads that swap a captured metal ion for a harmless hydrogen ion), and, crucially, filtration and packaging: the bottle, drum, **PFA** piping (a Teflon-family plastic that leaches nothing) and every valve must not leach. Chemicals arrive in ISO tanks or 200 L drums, are transferred to a **bulk chemical distribution system (BCDS)**, the fab's chemical plumbing: bulk tank, day tanks, and filtered PFA loops to every tool, filtered again at point of use, and blended on-tool (SC-1 and DHF are mixed at the process chamber from concentrates and ultrapure water).

Who makes the tools: the traditional **batch wet bench** dips 25–50 wafers at a time through a row of tanks, while the **single-wafer spin cleaner** sprays each chemistry onto one spinning wafer in turn, so no wafer ever sees a bath another wafer has dirtied; single-wafer tools from SCREEN (Japan, the leader), Tokyo Electron, Lam Research and SEMES (Samsung's tool subsidiary), and ACM Research serve critical cleans, with batch benches from SCREEN and others kept for high-volume, less critical steps.

The most famous chokepoint is ultra-high-purity HF: **Stella Chemifa** and **Morita Chemical Industries**, both in Osaka, are the two suppliers of 12N ("99.9999999999%") HF that Japan restricted to Korea in 2019, and between them supplied the large majority of the world's leading-edge etching-grade HF. HF's raw material is fluorspar, ~60% of which is mined in China, so the chain is China → Japan → the world.

> **What can go wrong:** **Watermarks**: if a droplet is allowed to evaporate on the wafer it leaves a ring of silica and whatever else was dissolved, which is why Marangoni and IPA-vapor drying exist. **Particle re-deposition**: a batch bath collects the particles from every wafer that has passed through it and gives some of them back to the next one, which is why critical cleans moved to single-wafer spin tools. **Bath ageing**: peroxide decomposes and etch rates drift, so baths are dosed or replaced on a schedule and their concentration is monitored in-line. And HF is dangerous: it penetrates skin and binds calcium in the body, so every HF station carries calcium gluconate gel and every fab trains for HF exposure before anything else.

## Ultrapure water

Water is the fab's largest single consumable by mass. It rinses after every wet step, dilutes every chemical, feeds every scrubber and cools every chiller. Process rinse water must be **ultrapure water (UPW)**, and its specification is the most extreme of any liquid produced industrially.

> **Intuition: resistivity is a live ion meter.** Dissolved salts supply **ions**, atoms or groups of atoms with an electrical charge. Those ions carry current through water, so adding salt lowers its **resistivity**, the material's resistance to current flow after allowing for the measuring cell's size. Even pure water conducts a little: a tiny fraction of its molecules split into H+ and OH- ions. The target of 18.2 MΩ·cm means the water is close to that pure-water limit, not that every ion has vanished. The unit reads “megohm-centimetres.” This measurement mainly answers “how much ionic contamination is present?” It cannot certify the absence of neutral organic molecules, dissolved silica, bacteria or particles; those need separate measurements.

| Parameter | Typical leading-edge UPW spec | Ordinary drinking water |
|---|---|---|
| Resistivity | 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25 °C (rounded from 18.18 for pure water, which still contains equilibrium H+ and OH- ions) | ~0.002–0.02 MΩ·cm |
| Total organic carbon (TOC) | < 1 ppb (< 0.5 ppb for advanced fabs) | ~1,000–5,000 ppb |
| Dissolved oxygen | < 1–10 ppb | ~8,000 ppb |
| Dissolved silica | < 0.1–0.5 ppb | ~10,000 ppb |
| Boron | < 0.05 ppb | ~50–500 ppb |
| Individual metals | < 1 ppt each (sub-ppt targets for Fe, Cu, Ni, Zn) | ppb to ppm |
| Particles | on the order of 100–1,000 per liter above 20 nm; sub-10 nm counts are now specified | millions per liter |
| Bacteria | < 1 CFU per liter | tens to hundreds per mL |
| Temperature | 20–25 °C ± 0.5 °C (plus a hot-UPW loop at 60–80 °C) | |

**TOC** is total organic carbon, the carbon in dissolved organic molecules; a **CFU** (colony-forming unit) is one live bacterium, counted by letting it grow into a visible colony on a test plate. Resistivity is the easy one. TOC, boron, silica and particles are the hard ones. **Reverse osmosis (RO)**, the first serious purification step, pushes water through a membrane whose pores pass water molecules but not ions; boron passes it because boric acid is a weak acid that is mostly un-ionized at neutral pH, so the membrane does not see it as an ion; silica is similar; organics from resin leachates and the piping itself are continuous sources; and a 10 nm particle is not filterable by anything except an ultrafilter that itself sheds.

A UPW plant (built by Ovivo, Veolia, Kurita, Organo, Nomura Micro Science, or Xylem/Evoqua, with ion-exchange resins from DuPont, Lanxess and Mitsubishi Chemical) is a long train:

1. **Pretreatment**: coagulation and multimedia filtration, activated carbon (to remove chlorine, which destroys RO membranes), softening or antiscalant dosing (to stop calcium and silica scale plating out on the RO membranes downstream), and 5 µm cartridge filtration.
2. **Reverse osmosis**, usually two passes in series; each pass rejects ~98–99% of ions and most organics. RO recovery is ~75–85%, so 15–25% of feed goes to reject.
3. **Membrane degasification** to strip CO2 (which forms carbonic acid and loads the ion-exchange beds) and O2 (dissolved oxygen would grow an unwanted native oxide on any bare silicon surface it rinses).
4. **Electrodeionization (EDI)**: ion-exchange resin between membranes with an applied DC field, continuously regenerated by electrolysis instead of by acid and caustic.
5. **Primary mixed-bed ion exchange** to reach ~18 MΩ·cm. A **mixed bed** is a column of two kinds of resin bead mixed together, one swapping H+ for every positive ion that passes and the other swapping OH- for every negative ion; the H+ and OH- then combine into water.
6. **185 nm UV** oxidizes organics to CO2 and organic acids (TOC destruction), which the next bed removes.
7. **Polishing mixed-bed** (**non-regenerable**: thrown away when full rather than chemically refreshed, because regeneration chemicals would re-contaminate it) to reach 18.2 MΩ·cm and ppt metals.
8. **254 nm UV** for sterilization, then **ultrafiltration** (**hollow-fiber**, bundles of membrane tubes rated at ~10 nm) as the final particle barrier.
9. A **recirculating distribution loop** in **PVDF** (a fluoropolymer pipe that leaches almost nothing) or clean PVC piping at ~1–2 m/s, never stagnant, returning everything not used to the polishing stage. Points of use get a final 0.05 µm filter.

The energy and capital are large (a UPW plant for a large fab is a $100–300 million facility), and the reason it is so big is volume. A 300 mm wafer at an advanced node consumes on the order of 1,500–2,500 gallons (6–9 m³) of UPW across its ~1,000+ steps. Fabs report total daily draw of 2–10 million gallons: TSMC's first Arizona fab is cited at ~4.75 million gallons/day, and a large gigafab complex in Taiwan (TSMC's name for its biggest sites, each starting 100,000+ wafers a month) more. Because UPW is so pure, most of the rinse water leaving a tool is still cleaner than city water, so fabs **reclaim** it: segregated drains route lightly used rinse water back to the RO feed, more contaminated streams to treatment and reuse in scrubbers and cooling towers. TSMC reports a process-water recycling rate of ~90% in Taiwan (90.3% in 2023) and its Arizona site runs ~65% as of 2025 with an on-site industrial reclamation plant due in 2028 targeting 85–90%. The 2021 Taiwan drought, which forced TSMC to truck in water, made this a board-level topic.

> **Worked example: UPW for a 100,000-wafer-per-month fab.**
> Assume 1,800 gallons (6.8 m³) of UPW per 300 mm wafer, a mid-range figure for a ~1,000-step logic flow, and a 30-day month.
> UPW demand = 100,000 wafers × 1,800 gal / 30 days = **6.0 million gallons per day**, or ~22,700 m³/day (roughly nine Olympic swimming pools every day, or the water use of ~20,000 US households). At 2 m³/wafer-equivalent for chillers, scrubbers and cooling towers on top, total water throughput is ~8–9 million gallons/day.
> Now the intake. RO recovery of ~80% means the UPW plant must be fed 6.0 / 0.8 = 7.5 million gallons/day. If 85% of the process water discharged is reclaimed and returned to the RO feed, fresh make-up water is only what is lost: evaporation in cooling towers and scrubbers, RO reject, and the ~15% not reclaimed. A reasonable outcome is a fresh-water intake of roughly 2–3 million gallons/day (~8,000–11,000 m³/day) for a fab whose internal UPW flow is 6 million. Without reclaim, the same fab would draw ~8–9 million gallons/day of municipal water, which is why the reclaim rate, not the UPW spec, is what a city negotiates over before a fab is permitted. Note also that the UPW polisher beds, at ~1 ppb of allowed leakage, must be sized for 22,700 m³/day of flow: ion-exchange resin, UV lamps and ultrafilters are themselves significant recurring consumables.

> **What can go wrong:** A TOC excursion when a new run of pipe is commissioned and leaches plasticiser for weeks (new loops are flushed and hot-water sanitised before they carry process water); **resin fines**, fragments shed by ageing ion-exchange beads, which are particles; **biofilm** in a **dead leg**, any stub of pipe where the water stands still long enough for bacteria to colonise it, which is why loops never stop and dead legs are designed out; and **boron breakthrough**, the first sign that a polisher bed is exhausted, because boron is the ion the resin holds most weakly. All of it is watched by on-line resistivity, TOC analysers and laser particle counters at the points of use, and a fab will stop a tool on a UPW alarm before it stops it for almost anything else.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The reclaim rate is the number a new fab is permitted on. Module 05 lays out the plant that produces this water and Module 20 the politics of siting a fab in Arizona or Taiwan; both turn on the arithmetic in the worked example, in which a 6-million-gallon internal flow becomes a 2–3-million-gallon municipal draw only because 85% of the rinse water goes round again.

## CMP slurries and pads

A scanner can only hold focus over ~100 nm of height (Module 07), and a copper level deposited over a field of trenches is a landscape of hills and valleys far taller than that. Before every new level can be patterned, the wafer must be shaved flat to within a few nanometres, and the only process that does this is **chemical-mechanical planarization (CMP)**. It appears 20–30+ times in a leading-edge flow: **shallow-trench isolation (STI)** oxide (the oxide-filled trenches that isolate neighbouring transistors), poly (**polysilicon**, the dummy-gate material) and **replacement-metal-gate** planarization (the step in which a dummy gate is polished flush before it is swapped for the real metal one), tungsten and cobalt contacts, and one copper-plus-**barrier** CMP (the thin liner between copper and the dielectric, explained in the next section) per metal level for 15–18 levels.

> **Intuition: toothpaste on a soft brush.** A slurry works like toothpaste on a soft toothbrush. The chemistry converts the top few atomic layers of the surface into a soft oxide or a soluble complex, and the abrasive particles wipe that softened layer off; the pad is the brush. High spots feel more pressure than low spots and lose material faster, which is what makes the surface flat. The **Preston relation** says it in one line: removal rate ∝ pressure × velocity, with a constant that the chemistry sets. It breaks down at the edges of wide features, where the pad bends into the low region and polishes what it should leave alone (dishing, below).

The wafer is pressed face-down against a rotating polyurethane pad while slurry flows in at ~100–300 mL/min per **platen** (the rotating table that carries the pad) for a minute or two, plus pad wear and conditioner wear. A **slurry** is an engineered **colloid** (a suspension of particles too small to settle): 30–150 nm abrasive particles (colloidal silica for oxide, poly, Cu and barrier; **fumed silica**, made by burning SiCl4 in a flame, historically; ceria for STI because it removes SiO2 ~50× faster than SiN, making the nitride a **stop layer**, a film the slurry barely touches so polishing halts at it; alumina for older W processes) suspended at a few percent solids in water with an oxidizer (H2O2, KIO3 or ferric nitrate for tungsten; H2O2 for copper), a **complexing agent** (glycine for Cu, which binds the oxidized copper so it dissolves), a **corrosion inhibitor** (benzotriazole, BTA, for Cu, which forms a protective monolayer on copper wherever the pad is not rubbing, so only the high spots are attacked), pH buffers, surfactants and biocide. The chemistry defines the selectivity between the metal, the barrier and the dielectric; the particle-size distribution defines the scratch rate. Slurry is filtered at point of use with Entegris or Pall filters because a single 1 µm agglomerate scratches a line into the copper.

> **Worked example: how much slurry does a wafer use?**
> At 200 mL/min for 1.5 minutes, one CMP step uses ~300 mL of slurry, nearly all of which runs off the pad and down the drain. Over ~25 CMP steps that is 25 × 0.3 L ≈ 7.5 L of slurry per wafer. A fab starting 100,000 wafers a month therefore consumes ~750,000 L of slurry a month, ~750 m³, the volume of a large swimming pool, of a suspension whose particles must be counted and whose metals are specified at ppb. That volume is why slurry is shipped as concentrate, blended on-site with UPW in a dedicated distribution system, and filtered at the tool, and why waste slurry treatment is its own plant.

The **pad** is a cast, sliced, and grooved polyurethane sheet of controlled porosity and hardness, usually a hard top pad (DuPont's IC1000 family and its successors) laminated to a soft sub-pad (Suba IV): the hard pad flattens long-range hills (global planarization) while the soft one lets the stack follow the wafer's gentle waviness (local conformity). A pad is **conditioned** continuously with a diamond-grit disk (3M, Kinik, Entegris, Saesol) that re-roughens its surface so it keeps holding slurry, and lasts ~12–36 hours of polishing before it is replaced. Post-CMP cleaning with brush scrubbers (rotating polyvinyl-alcohol (PVA) sponge rollers that wipe both faces) and dedicated cleaning chemistries (from the same slurry vendors) removes the slurry residue that would otherwise be the biggest particle source in the fab. The CMP tools themselves come from Applied Materials (the Reflexion family, the majority of the market) and Ebara (Japan).

> **What can go wrong:** **Dishing**: a wide copper line bowls in the middle because the pad bends into it, thinning the line. **Erosion**: a dense array of narrow lines polishes faster than the surrounding field and ends up lower. **Scratches** from an agglomerate or a shed pad fragment, which cut across many lines at once. **Delamination** (peeling apart at an interface) of a mechanically weak **low-k dielectric** (the porous insulator between copper wires, chosen for low permittivity so that neighbouring wires couple less; the porosity is what makes it weak) under the shear of polishing. And **post-CMP residue**, slurry particles or BTA left on the surface. Each polish is stopped by **endpoint detection**, an in-situ eddy-current sensor for metal thickness or an optical sensor for dielectric, and the wafer is then measured on a post-CMP optical inspection tool for scratches and residue and on a film-thickness tool for dishing and erosion (Module 13).

## Sputter targets

Copper wiring must be lined with a fence that copper atoms cannot cross, because copper that wanders into silicon kills transistors, and the fence, and the thin copper film on it that the plating bath needs to start from, are put down atom by atom by sputtering. **Physical vapor deposition (PVD)**, covered in Module 06, ejects atoms from a **sputter target** with argon ions and deposits them on the wafer.

> **Intuition: billiard balls.** Argon ions accelerated onto the target knock atoms loose the way a cue ball scatters a rack: each ion's momentum cascades through the top few atomic layers and a few atoms fly out. The atoms fly across the chamber to the wafer and stick. How they scatter depends on the size and orientation of the metal grains they came from, because a grain is a tiny crystal with its own preferred directions, so a target's grain structure is written directly into the film's thickness uniformity across the wafer.

In a logic fab the targets are: **tantalum** (and TaN by **reactive sputtering**, in which nitrogen added to the argon turns the tantalum into tantalum nitride as it lands) for the copper **diffusion barrier**, the thin liner between the copper and the dielectric that copper atoms cannot cross; **copper** for the electroplating **seed layer**, the thin conducting film the plating bath needs to start growing copper (and for the seed in **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, vertical copper-filled holes through a die that connect stacked dies, Module 16); **titanium** and TiN for contacts and hardmasks; **cobalt** and **ruthenium** for liners and caps; **tungsten** for some metal gates and hardmasks; **aluminum** (with 0.5% Cu) for bond pads; and **nickel-platinum** for **silicides**, metal-silicon compounds that make a low-resistance contact to the source and drain. Memory fabs add many more. A representative recipe, all figures approximate: ~kW-class DC power on the target, argon at a few mTorr, the target a few tens of millimetres to ~100 mm above the wafer, depositing a few nanometres per second.

A target is a 300–450 mm disk of 5N–6N (99.999–99.9999%) metal, bonded to a copper backing plate, with a controlled grain size (typically < 50 µm, sometimes < 20 µm) and crystallographic **texture** (which crystal face most grains present to the plasma), because grain size and texture set the angular distribution of sputtered atoms and thus the film uniformity across the wafer; a target with coarse or mixed-texture grains gives a film with ±5% instead of ±1% thickness variation. Targets are consumed to an **erosion groove** (the ring where the magnetic field concentrates the plasma and the target wears fastest) and replaced every few hundred **kilowatt-hours** (the cumulative power delivered to the target, the standard way to count its life), and the spent target (still 60–70% of the metal) is returned for refining. Tantalum is the strategic one: it is refined from tantalite mined largely in the DRC and Rwanda (a "conflict mineral" under Dodd-Frank Section 1502, which requires US companies to trace its origin), reduced to powder by Global Advanced Metals or TANIOBIS (H.C. Starck), then **electron-beam melted** (melted in vacuum by an electron beam, which boils off volatile impurities), forged and rolled to a fine-grain plate.

> **Worked example: how many wafers does one tantalum target coat? (order of magnitude; every figure ~)**
> Take the recipe above: ~10 kW on the target and a deposition rate of ~2 nm/s, so a ~5 nm Ta/TaN barrier takes ~2.5 s of plasma, or 10 kW × 2.5 s = 25 kJ ≈ 0.007 kWh per wafer pass. A target retired after ~300 kWh has therefore coated ~300 / 0.007 ≈ 40,000 wafer passes. A leading-edge wafer needs a barrier on each of its 15–18 copper levels, so one target's life is ~40,000 / 17 ≈ 2,500 wafers' worth of barriers, and a fab starting 100,000 wafers a month goes through ~40 tantalum targets a month across its PVD chambers, each on the order of $10,000–30,000 (trade estimates). In mass: 5 nm of Ta over the 707 cm² of a wafer is 3.5 × 10^-4 cm³, or ~6 mg at 16.7 g/cm³; 40,000 passes put ~240 g on wafers, and since only a fraction of the sputtered atoms land on the wafer (the rest coat the chamber shields), the metal that actually leaves the target over its life is several times that, still a small part of a disk that weighs ~10–20 kg. A fab of this size therefore handles tonnes of tantalum a year in targets, of which only a fraction is sputtered away and the rest goes back as spent targets for refining, against a world tantalum mine output of only ~2,000 tonnes a year. That is why the return loop for spent targets matters as much as the mine, and why a target's life is set by the depth of its erosion groove, not by the mass of the disk.

The supply is a Japanese-American oligopoly: **JX Advanced Metals** (formerly JX Nippon Mining & Metals, spun out and listed in March 2025) claims the largest global share, on the order of half or more of semiconductor targets by most estimates, particularly in Ta, Cu and Ti; **Honeywell Electronic Materials** (Spokane, US) and **Tosoh** (Japan) are the other two majors; **Materion** and **Linde/Praxair Surface Technologies** (US), **Ulvac** and **Proterial/Hitachi Metals** (Japan), **Plansee** (Austria, W and Mo) and China's **KFMI (Konfoong)** fill out the market.

> **What can go wrong:** An inclusion or an oxide patch in the target charges up and **arcs**, blasting a droplet onto the wafer, and repeated arcing grows **nodules** on the target face that arc again. A target run past its life **burns through** at the erosion groove, exposing the backing plate and putting copper where it should not be. Sputtered metal that lands on the chamber shields instead of the wafer builds up and **flakes**, and every flake is a particle. All three show up on the wafer as thickness non-uniformity on the film-thickness map and as particle counts on the inspection scan, which is why targets are replaced on a kWh schedule and chamber shields on a wafer-count schedule rather than when they fail.

## Precursors for ALD and CVD

Every film in the **FEOL** (front end of line, the transistors), the **MOL** (middle of line, the contacts that reach them) and the BEOL that is not grown by oxidation or sputtered arrives as a molecule in the gas phase that decomposes or reacts on the wafer. A good **precursor** must do four things at once: be a gas, or become one, at a temperature its ampoule and delivery lines can hold; survive the trip to the chamber without falling apart; react completely on the wafer; and leave only gases behind. Most molecules fail at least one of the four, which is why the useful precursors for a given film number in the single digits. Module 06 covers the deposition physics; the consumable question is the molecule.

> **Intuition: ALD, one layer at a time.** In **atomic layer deposition (ALD)** the wafer is exposed to the metal precursor until every surface site has grabbed one molecule and no more (the reaction is **self-limiting**: once the surface is covered, nothing more sticks); the chamber is purged; then a second gas (water, ozone, ammonia) strips the molecule's organic arms and leaves one atomic layer of the film; purge again and repeat. Fifty cycles give ~5 nm, on every surface the gas can reach, including the walls of a deep trench. **CVD** is the same chemistry without the stop: both gases flow together and the film grows continuously, faster but less **conformal** (conformal means equal thickness on every surface, sidewalls included).

The precursor reaches the tool by one of two routes. A **bubbler** passes a carrier gas through the liquid precursor, which picks up the vapour; **direct liquid injection (DLI)** meters the liquid and flash-vaporises it at the tool. Solids are sublimed in a heated vessel. The important molecules:

- **TEOS** (tetraethyl orthosilicate, Si(OC2H5)4): the workhorse for PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD, in which a plasma supplies the energy so the wafer can stay cooler) and sub-atmospheric CVD SiO2, because it decomposes cleanly at ~650–750 °C (or at ~400 °C with O3 or plasma) and gives conformal oxide. Liquid, delivered by bubbler or DLI.
- **SiN precursors**: DCS + NH3 for furnace LPCVD; for the lower-temperature ALD nitrides needed after the metal gate is in place, molecules such as **trisilylamine (TSA)** and **hexachlorodisilane (HCDS)**, plus several proprietary amino-silanes.
- **TMA** (trimethylaluminum, Al(CH3)3): with H2O or O3 the textbook ALD reaction for Al2O3 (~1.1 Å per cycle at 300 °C); pyrophoric; also the Al source for AlN and Al-doped HfO2. Made by Nouryon, Albemarle and Lanxess and purified to semiconductor grade by Merck, Entegris and Adeka.
- **Hafnium precursors** for the **high-k** gate dielectric (an insulator that stores more charge per volt than SiO2, so it can be physically thicker and leak less while acting electrically thinner): **HfCl4** (a solid that must be sublimed at ~150–170 °C in a heated solid-source delivery vessel; reportedly the chemistry Intel used at 45 nm in 2007) and the liquid amides **TDMAH** and **TEMAH** (tetrakis(dimethylamido)- and tetrakis(ethylmethylamido)hafnium) that are easier to deliver but leave carbon and nitrogen. **ZrCl4** and **TEMAZ** likewise for the ZrO2/Al2O3/ZrO2 capacitor dielectric in every DRAM cell; Adeka (Japan) and SK trichem (Korea) have very large shares in these.
- **Titanium nitride precursors**: TiCl4 + NH3 for ALD TiN, the **work-function metal** (the gate metal whose electron affinity sets the transistor's threshold voltage) and barrier metal, and **TDMAT** (tetrakis(dimethylamido)titanium) for MOCVD TiN.
- **Tungsten**: WF6 nucleated with B2H6 or SiH4 then reduced with H2. **Molybdenum** (MoO2Cl2, MoCl5) is displacing W in 3D NAND wordlines (as of 2025) and is a candidate for logic contacts because Mo has lower resistivity at small dimensions and needs no fluorine-blocking liner.
- **Cobalt and ruthenium**: for MOL contacts, liners and caps; Co from carbonyl-based molecules (in which the metal is carried by carbon monoxide groups) and amidinates, Ru from cyclopentadienyl- and carbonyl-based compounds and from RuO4 solutions. Ru precursors are expensive (Ru itself is a platinum-group metal at ~$500–800 per ounce as of 2025, a volatile price, supplied mainly by Tanaka and Heraeus) and their **nucleation delay** on dielectrics, a metal that will not start growing on an oxide for the first dozens of cycles and then grows as islands instead of a film, is a chronic problem.

> **Worked example: how much TMA does a wafer use? (order of magnitude; every figure ~)**
> One nanometre of Al2O3 on a 300 mm wafer (707 cm²) is 707 × 10^-7 cm³ ≈ 7 × 10^-5 cm³; at a density of ~3–4 g/cm³ that is ~0.2–0.3 mg of oxide. Al2O3 is 102 g/mol and each formula unit needs two aluminium atoms, each delivered by one TMA molecule (72 g/mol), so ~0.3 mg of oxide needs ~0.4 mg of TMA at 100% utilization. Real ALD utilization is only ~5–10%, because each pulse must flood the chamber to saturate the surface and the rest is pumped away, so the true consumption is ~5 mg of TMA per nanometre per wafer. A 5 nm film across 100,000 wafers a month is then ~5 × 5 mg × 100,000 ≈ 2.5 kg of TMA a month, from ampoules that hold a few kilograms each. That is why a precursor is a small line item in mass and money and an enormous one in consequence: a few kilograms a month decides the gate dielectric of every transistor in the fab.

Precursors ship in electropolished stainless-steel **ampoules** of a few hundred grams to a few kilograms, with heated lines to the tool. A single 300 mm fab spends tens of millions of dollars per year on them (trade estimates).

> **What can go wrong:** A precursor that decomposes in a hot delivery line leaves solid in the line and particles on the wafer, so line temperatures are held within a few degrees of the window between condensation (too cold) and decomposition (too hot). An ampoule that is nearly empty delivers less vapour per pulse, and the film thins from wafer to wafer, which in-line **ellipsometry** (a film-thickness measurement from the polarization of reflected light) catches as a drift; ampoules are weighed and swapped before it happens. And nucleation delay on a dielectric surface gives islands instead of a continuous film for the first cycles, which a thickness-versus-cycles plot reveals as an intercept, and which is fixed with a surface pre-treatment or a different starting layer.

## Quartzware and SiC furnace parts

This is a short side note. The parts inside a hot tool are consumables too. Diffusion and LPCVD furnaces (Kokusai, TEL) use process tubes, liners and wafer boats of **fused silica (quartz)**; single-wafer etch chambers use quartz windows, rings and gas-distribution plates, along with **silicon** and **silicon carbide** edge rings that surround the wafer on the **electrostatic chuck** (the plate that holds the wafer flat by electrostatic attraction). An etch **focus ring** is a ring of silicon or SiC around the wafer's edge that makes the plasma see a continuous surface, so the outermost chips etch like the centre; it erodes under ion bombardment and is replaced every few hundred RF-hours (hours of plasma on-time), so a busy etcher consumes tens of thousands of dollars of rings per year, a business Lam Research and TEL themselves compete in. What goes wrong is simple: as the ring erodes, the feature widths at the wafer edge drift, which the edge-die metrology catches, and the ring is swapped on a schedule before that happens.

Semiconductor quartz is either **electrically fused** from the same Spruce Pine, North Carolina sand that supplies Czochralski crucibles (Module 02) or **synthetic fused silica** made by **flame hydrolysis** of SiCl4 (burning it in a hydrogen-oxygen flame to rain out pure silica soot, which is then fused), which is purer (ppb alkali metals) but costlier. **Heraeus Conamic** (Germany), **Tosoh Quartz**, **Shin-Etsu Quartz Products**, **Momentive** (US) and **Ferrotec** (Japan/China) supply ingots and shapes. Above ~1,200 °C, where quartz sags and **devitrifies** (slowly crystallises into opaque, brittle cristobalite), boats and paddles are **SiC**: sintered or **reaction-bonded** SiC (a porous carbon form infiltrated with molten silicon) sealed with a **CVD-SiC coating** deposited from methyltrichlorosilane at ~1,300 °C; CVD-SiC-coated graphite **susceptors** (the plate that absorbs the heater's energy and carries the wafers) carry the wafers in every SiC and GaN epi reactor. CoorsTek (US), Tokai Carbon, Kyocera and NGK (Japan) supply these.

## Photomasks: the supply chain in depth

A **photomask** (or **reticle**) is the master pattern that the scanner images onto the wafer, four times reduced, one field at a time. A leading-edge product needs 80–100+ of them, each different, each perfect, because a single printable defect on a mask is repeated on every die on every wafer. What makes a mask hard is that it must be defect-free over an area a hundred million times larger than the smallest feature it carries, and for EUV it must be a mirror made of eighty layers each a few atoms thick. The mask supply chain is short, opaque, and expensive, and it has its own equipment industry.

**Blanks.** The universal substrate is the **6025** blank: 6 inches × 6 inches × 0.25 inch (152 mm × 152 mm × 6.35 mm) of synthetic fused silica, polished to a flatness of a few hundred nanometers for DUV and a surface roughness below 0.2 nm **RMS** (root mean square, the standard average of a surface's height wobbles), on which the **absorber** stack (the opaque film that will be patterned into the mask's dark regions) is deposited by sputtering. For **DUV** the stack is either ~70–100 nm of chromium with a CrOxNy anti-reflective layer (a **binary mask**, or **chrome-on-glass, COG**: every point is either clear or dark), or, for critical ArF layers, a ~70 nm molybdenum silicide film tuned to transmit ~6% of the light with a 180° phase shift (**attenuated phase-shift mask, att-PSM**), which sharpens the **aerial image**, the light pattern that arrives at the wafer, at feature edges.

> **Intuition: why a leaky absorber prints sharper.** Light spilling under the edge of a dark feature blurs it. If the "dark" region lets 6% of the light through but half a wavelength out of step, that leakage arrives at the edge in opposite phase to the spill and cancels it instead of adding to it, so the edge prints sharper than it would with a perfectly opaque absorber. The 6% is a compromise: enough to cancel the spill, too little to expose the resist by itself.

The thinner "opaque MoSi on glass" stack used at the smallest pitches reduces **electromagnetic shadowing**, the distortion a tall absorber causes when light arrives at an angle and the absorber's own walls cast a shadow.

An **EUV blank** is a different animal, because 13.5 nm light is absorbed by everything, so the mask must be a mirror.

> **Intuition: a mirror made of eighty interfaces.** No material reflects 13.5 nm light usefully by itself, but each interface between molybdenum and silicon reflects a fraction of a percent. Space the interfaces so that the reflections from all eighty of them arrive crest on crest, a spacing of about half the wavelength, ~7 nm, and they add up to ~65%; this is a **Bragg reflector**. The price is fragility: a buried particle bends the layers above it, puts the reflections from that spot out of step, and prints as a dark blotch that no repair can reach, because the defect is inside the mirror.

The substrate is a **low-thermal-expansion material (LTEM)**, TiO2-doped silica (Corning ULE or Shin-Etsu's equivalent) with a thermal expansion coefficient within ±5 ppb/°C of zero (five parts per billion of length change per degree), because the mask absorbs ~30–40% of a 500 W EUV source's light and any expansion is an **overlay** error (a misplacement of this layer's pattern relative to the one beneath it); flatness below ~30 nm and roughness ~0.05 nm RMS are required. On it is deposited the Bragg reflector of 40 bilayers of molybdenum (~2.8 nm) and silicon (~4.1 nm), a 6.9–7 nm period tuned so that reflections from each Mo/Si interface add constructively at 13.5 nm and the 6° **chief-ray angle** (the scanner illuminates the mask 6° off the perpendicular, because a mirror cannot be lit head-on without the light returning into the source), giving ~64–67% peak reflectivity, capped with 2.5 nm of ruthenium to stop oxidation during cleaning. The absorber is ~55–70 nm of tantalum boron nitride (TaBN) with a TaBO anti-reflective top. For **High-NA** (ASML's next scanner generation, with a larger lens opening, numerical aperture 0.55 versus 0.33, which resolves smaller features but images the mask at 4× in one direction and 8× in the other, an **anamorphic** reduction), the thick absorber's shadowing becomes a major imaging error, and thinner (~30 nm) high-absorption alloys of Ru, Pt, Ni or Te are being introduced. The backside gets a conductive CrN layer for the scanner's electrostatic chuck.

The multilayer is deposited by **ion-beam sputtering** (Veeco's Nexus IBD tools are the industry standard) in a process that must add zero defects larger than ~20 nm across 40 layers and ~17,000 mm² (the 132 mm × 132 mm quality area), because a particle buried in the multilayer becomes a **phase defect** that prints and cannot be repaired. Only two companies have achieved this at production yield: **Hoya** (Japan, roughly two-thirds of the market) and **AGC** (Japan, the balance), a near-perfect duopoly that has held for a decade. An EUV blank is inspected on a **Lasertec ABICS** **actinic** blank-inspection tool (actinic means inspected with the same 13.5 nm light the scanner uses, so what it sees is what will print), and its defect map ships with it so the mask shop can rotate or shift the pattern to hide any remaining defects under absorber. EUV blank prices are not published; industry estimates run from tens of thousands of dollars toward $100,000 each, which makes the blank alone more expensive than most finished DUV masks.

**Writing.** Mask data preparation converts the design's corrected layer (Module 07) into a writer format. The correction is **optical proximity correction (OPC)**, pre-distorting the mask shapes so that, after diffraction in the scanner, the wafer pattern matches the design intent, or **inverse lithography technology (ILT)**, computing the ideal mask shape mathematically, which comes out curvy; **mask-process corrections** then compensate for the writer's and etcher's own distortions. For decades masks were written by **variable-shaped-beam (VSB)** electron-beam tools (NuFlare EBM-9500, JEOL JBX-3200), which flash rectangles one at a time; write time scales with **shot count**, the number of rectangles the tool must flash, and had grown to 20–40 hours for the most complex EUV layers, and it made the curvilinear patterns that ILT wants, which need millions of shots, essentially unwritable. **Multi-beam mask writers** solved this: an array of ~262,144 (512 × 512) **beamlets** (independent, individually switched electron beams) in IMS Nanofabrication's MBMW series or ~500,000 in NuFlare's MBM series are individually blanked as the stage scans, so write time is ~8–12 hours regardless of pattern complexity. IMS (Vienna, Austria; owned by Intel since 2017, with TSMC and Bain taking minority stakes in 2023) shipped the MBMW-101 in 2016, the **MBMW-201** in 2019, and the **MBMW-301** in 2023 for the 2 nm generation and High-NA; NuFlare (Japan, Toshiba group) followed with the **MBM-2000**, MBM-2000PLUS and, in 2023, the **MBM-3000** with 12 nm beamlets at 3.6 A/cm² (amps per square centimetre, the beam's current density) and a ~10 hour write time. Each writer costs on the order of $30–50 million, and a leading mask shop runs a fleet.

The written resist (a chemically amplified e-beam resist, ~50–100 nm) is developed, the absorber is dry-etched (Cl2/O2 for Cr; chlorine then fluorine chemistries for TaBN), the resist is stripped, and the mask is cleaned, an especially delicate step for EUV because the Ru cap and the Mo/Si stack cannot tolerate the SPM chemistry used on DUV masks.

**Inspection, repair and metrology.** A DUV mask is inspected **die-to-database** (the inspected image is compared against the design file, so any difference is a defect) on a KLA Teron 6xx-series 193 nm optical tool or an e-beam inspector. An EUV mask has a problem: a DUV inspector cannot see the phase defects or the small absorber defects that matter at 13.5 nm. The answer is **actinic patterned-mask inspection (APMI)** using an EUV source: **Lasertec's ACTIS A150** (introduced 2019, with a ~$100 million+ price) was for years the only such tool in the world and made Lasertec one of the most important small companies in the industry; the ACTIS A300 followed in November 2023 for High-NA (anamorphic) masks. Zeiss's **AIMS EUV** emulates the scanner's illumination to show what a defect will actually print as. Repair uses Zeiss **MeRiT** electron-beam **gas-assisted etch** and deposition (a focused electron beam triggers a local reaction with a gas to remove or add absorber with ~10 nm precision), or Bruker's nanomachining. **Registration** (where each feature sits relative to where it should, in nm) is measured on KLA IPRO or Zeiss PROVE tools to ~1 nm, and **CD** (critical dimension, the width of the smallest feature) on Holon or Hitachi mask CD-SEMs. Each EUV mask spends days in this loop.

> **What can go wrong:** Multilayer phase defects, which cannot be repaired and are hidden by shifting the pattern so that absorber covers them, using the ABICS map. Absorber defects (a missing or extra piece of TaBN), found by ACTIS, confirmed by AIMS (which shows whether they would actually print) and repaired by MeRiT. **Haze**, a crystalline growth of ammonium sulfate that forms on DUV masks under long exposure from residues of the cleaning chemistry, which is why mask cleaning chemistry and the atmosphere under the pellicle (the protective membrane over the mask, defined in the next paragraph) are controlled. Pellicle particles, which do not print but scatter light, and pellicle rupture, which drops the membrane onto the pattern. And mask heating drift during exposure, which the LTEM substrate minimises and the scanner's alignment corrects. The loop that catches all of it is ABICS at the blank, ACTIS after patterning, AIMS to adjudicate, MeRiT to repair, and periodic re-inspection through the mask's life.

**Pellicles.** A **pellicle** is a thin transparent membrane stretched on a frame ~6 mm above the mask surface, so that any particle landing on it sits far out of the focal plane and does not print. DUV pellicles are ~0.8 µm fluoropolymer films with > 99% transmission and cost a few hundred dollars; they are on every DUV mask and nobody thinks about them. EUV pellicles are hard because nothing is transparent at 13.5 nm: the membrane must be ~20–50 nm thick, survive 500+ W of EUV in vacuum at hundreds of degrees Celsius, and be mechanically stable across 110 mm × 140 mm. ASML's original polysilicon design reached ~83% transmission; the current metal-silicide-coated polysilicon type, manufactured under license by **Mitsui Chemicals** since 2021, achieves roughly 88–92% (published figures vary by generation). Because the light passes through twice (in and out), a 90% pellicle costs (0.9)² ≈ 81% of the dose, i.e. ~19% of scanner throughput, and it degrades with dose. **Carbon-nanotube (CNT)** pellicles developed at imec and being commercialized by Mitsui (targeting ≥ 94% transmission, with a 5,000-sheet-per-year line at Iwakuni-Ohtake that was scheduled for completion in December 2025; check current status) and by Canatu (Finland) are the next generation; membrane-only lab results exceed 97%. Because of the throughput penalty and the cost (EUV pellicles run on the order of tens of thousands of dollars each), TSMC ran its early EUV layers without pellicles and relied on frequent mask inspection instead; pellicle adoption has broadened as source power and yields have risen, and it is essentially mandatory for memory makers' high-volume layers.

**Cost and who makes them.** A binary DUV mask for a non-critical layer costs on the order of $5,000–20,000; a critical ArF-immersion att-PSM with aggressive OPC costs ~$50,000–150,000; an EUV mask costs on the order of $300,000–500,000 (and more for a High-NA mask), driven by the blank, the multi-beam write, and the actinic inspection loop. The three companies that consume the most masks, TSMC, Samsung and Intel, all run **captive mask shops** (TSMC's is believed to be the largest mask operation in the world by output; Intel's Mask Operations is in Santa Clara, California) that make essentially all of their leading-edge masks. The **merchant** mask makers, **Photronics** (US; with joint ventures PDMC in Taiwan with DNP and Xiamen in China), **Tekscend Photomask** (the former Toppan Photomask, sold by Toppan to the investment firm Integral in 2025), and **DNP** (Dai Nippon Printing), serve the fabless companies' DUV layers, foundries' mature nodes, and some EUV work (DNP and Tekscend both have EUV mask processes and the IBM joint development for 2 nm and High-NA masks); Hoya and Taiwan Mask Corp fill niches. Merchant EUV mask volume is small; the leading-edge mask business is, in practice, internal to the three companies who can afford it.

> **Worked example: the cost of one mask set at a 2 nm-class node.**
> A TSMC N2-class **SoC** (system on chip, a whole processor on one die) uses roughly 80–100 mask layers. Take a representative split: ~25 EUV layers (fin/sheet, gate cut, contacts, the first ~8–10 metal levels and their vias) at ~$400,000 each; ~50 critical and semi-critical ArF-immersion PSM layers at ~$100,000; ~20 KrF/i-line layers (implants, pads, thick oxides) at ~$15,000.
> EUV: 25 × $400k = $10.0M. ArFi: 50 × $100k = $5.0M. KrF/i-line: 20 × $15k = $0.3M. Base set ≈ **$15M**. Add EUV pellicles (~25 × ~$25k ≈ $0.6M), a set of engineering-change spares and re-writes (no mask set survives first silicon unchanged; a metal-layer respin re-makes ~5–10 masks, $1–3M), plus qualification and inspection time, and a realistic all-in for the program is **$20–30M**, consistent with industry estimates; High-NA half-field masks would roughly double the EUV line item for the affected layers. Now amortize it. At ~$25,000–30,000 per N2 wafer, a product that ships 100,000 wafers adds only ~$250 per wafer in mask cost, under 1%. A product that ships 2,000 wafers (a specialty **ASIC**, a chip designed for one customer's single purpose, or a research chip) adds $12,500 per wafer, half the wafer's price. This is the arithmetic that drives every fabless company toward high-volume products or **multi-project-wafer (MPW) shuttles** (a shared wafer run in which many small designs split one mask set and its cost) at leading nodes, and it is why a $20–30M mask set sits next to a $500M+ design cost in the economics of a 2 nm chip (Module 19).

> **Why it matters downstream:** The mask is imaged at 4× reduction, so a 1 nm registration error on the mask is 0.25 nm of misplacement on the wafer. Module 08's overlay budget, the allowed misalignment between one layer and the next, is on the order of 2 nm at the 2 nm node, so the mask's ~1 nm registration eats an eighth of it before the scanner has done anything. That is why registration is measured to the nanometre on every EUV mask, and why the LTEM substrate's ±5 ppb/°C matters: a mask that stretches by 5 ppb per degree moves its features ~0.7 nm end to end across the 132 mm quality area for every degree it warms.

## Why Japan dominates materials

Stepping back over the categories: Japanese firms hold ~90% of photoresists, ~100% of EUV mask blanks, ~55% of silicon wafers (Shin-Etsu and SUMCO; industry estimate), the majority of high-purity HF, colloidal silica for slurries, quartz, sputter targets, DRAM high-k precursors, **dicing tape** (the sticky film a wafer sits on while it is sawn into dies) and **die-attach film** (the glue layer under a die in its package; Lintec, Nitto Denko, Resonac), and package substrates' **build-up film** (the insulating layers of the chip package substrate; Ajinomoto, Module 16). Estimates put Japan's overall share of wafer-fab materials at ~half by value, a share that has not fallen even as its share of chip production collapsed from ~50% in 1988 to under 10%.

The reasons are structural. Materials do not scale like chips; they scale like craft. The value in a resist or a blank is a decades-long accumulation of small process improvements against purity and defect specifications that tighten every node, in an industry where qualification takes 6–18 months and the customer will not requalify unless forced. That favors incumbents with patient capital, a supply base that grew up next to the dominant chipmakers of the 1980s (NEC, Toshiba, Hitachi, Fujitsu) and kept improving after they faded, and chemical majors sitting atop deep domestic fluorine, silicone and specialty-monomer chains. A supplier may therefore qualify a resist for one wavelength, layer and customer while still developing another formulation. KrF, ArF immersion and EUV resists require different chemistry and defect control; progress in one category does not establish qualification in the others. This product-level distinction applies to established suppliers and new entrants alike.

The implication for the supply chain is that the fab's risk register looks nothing like its cost sheet. Consumables are maybe 10–15% of the cost of a leading-edge wafer (an industry estimate), but the Tohoku earthquake of March 2011 (which took out Shin-Etsu's Shirakawa wafer plant and MEMC's Utsunomiya plant, ~25% of world wafer supply, for weeks, and Mitsubishi Gas Chemical's **BT resin** plant, the laminate in memory package substrates), the 2019 Japan–Korea spat, the 2022 neon shock and the 2023 Ga/Ge controls all demonstrated that a fab is only as robust as its least substitutable bottle.

## Summary

- Silicon wins on its oxide, its clean melt, its abundance and 60 years of ecosystem, not on mobility or bandgap; every rival fixes one weakness and pays for it in defects and integration cost.
- SiGe on Si is mismatched by 4.2% × the Ge fraction; below the critical thickness (~35–40 nm at x = 0.25) it is strained and defect-free, above it misfit dislocations form and thread up through the channel.
- Embedded SiGe source/drain squeezes the pMOS channel for +20–30% drive current (Intel, 2003, and every node since); Si/SiGe superlattices are the scaffold from which nanosheets are released.
- SiC has no melt: PVT sublimation at ~2,400 °C at 100–500 µm/h grows a boule in a week, then diamond sawing; that, plus dislocation yield, is why a 150 mm SiC substrate costs tens of times a silicon wafer.
- A 10× higher critical field means a ~10× thinner, ~100× heavier-doped drift layer and an on-resistance hundreds of times lower (Baliga figure of merit ∝ ε µ E_c³, ~300× at 1,200 V): why 800 V EVs use SiC.
- GaN's transistor is the polarization-induced 2DEG at an AlGaN/GaN interface (~10^13 cm⁻², ~300–400 Ω/sq); almost all GaN is a few microns grown by MOCVD on silicon (power), SiC (RF) or sapphire (LEDs).
- GaAs (LEC/VGF, 150 mm) serves RF devices and VCSELs; InP (75–100 mm, high-pressure LEC) supplies lasers for many single-mode 800G/1.6T links, including silicon-photonics designs. Source choice depends on the link; an indirect bandgap limits ordinary silicon light emission rather than forbidding all silicon lasers.
- Gallium and germanium are Chinese-controlled inputs (export licensing since August 2023, US ban December 2024).
- Beyond ~2035 the channel candidates are high-Ge SiGe, 2D MoS2/WS2 (contact resistance and wafer-scale growth unsolved) and oxide semiconductors for DRAM.
- The materials market is ~$67.5 B (2024); Japan holds ~half by value and ~90% of resists and ~100% of EUV blanks; qualification takes 6–18 months, which is what makes these positions sticky.
- Resists: one chemistry per wavelength; EUV's limit is that only ~10–20 photons land per nm², so edges become statistical.
- Gases are byproducts of other industries whose purification is the chokepoint (Ukraine neon 2022); a fab's on-site ASU makes hundreds of thousands of tonnes of nitrogen a year.
- Wet chemistry is 1960s RCA recipes at ppt purity; UPW is 18.2 MΩ·cm water at 6–9 m³ per wafer, and the reclaim rate is what a city negotiates.
- Slurries, targets, precursors and quartz are small line items from oligopolies; an EUV mask is a 40-bilayer Mo/Si mirror from a Hoya/AGC duopoly, written on IMS/NuFlare tools, inspected on Lasertec, ~$300–500k each and ~$20–30M per set; a 90% pellicle costs ~19% of throughput.
- The fab's risk register is not its cost sheet: consumables are ~10–15% of wafer cost but are the least substitutable inputs (2011 Tohoku, 2019 Japan–Korea, 2022 neon, 2023 Ga/Ge).

## Common Misconceptions

- **"SiC wafers are expensive because SiC is a rare material." → Reality:** Silicon and carbon are two of the most abundant elements on Earth. SiC substrates cost tens of times more than a silicon wafer of the same diameter (up to ~100× before the 2023–2025 price collapse) because SiC cannot be melted and pulled; it must be sublimed onto a seed at ~2,400 °C at a few hundred microns per hour, then sawn with diamond, and the yield is set by dislocations and polytype inclusions.
- **"GaN chargers use gallium nitride wafers." → Reality:** Almost all power GaN is a few microns of GaN grown by MOCVD on a 150–200 mm silicon wafer, processed in a silicon fab. Bulk GaN substrates exist but are small, cost thousands of dollars, and are used for lasers and R&D vertical devices.
- **"Strained silicon means silicon grown on SiGe." → Reality:** That was the 1990s virtual-substrate approach, which failed on defect density. Production strain since 2003 comes from embedded SiGe source/drain that squeezes the pMOS channel, from stress liners, and from the fin or nanosheet geometry, all without a relaxed SiGe buffer.
- **"Ultrapure water is just distilled water." → Reality:** Distilled water is ~1 MΩ·cm with ppm-level organics and dissolved gases. UPW is 18.2 MΩ·cm with organics below 1 ppb, metals below 1 ppt and particles counted per liter at 20 nm, produced by a nine-stage train (RO, EDI, mixed beds, 185 nm UV, ultrafiltration) and never allowed to stand still in a pipe.
- **"The EUV scanner is the chokepoint; everything else is commodity." → Reality:** An EUV layer also depends on Hoya/AGC blanks (a duopoly), a multi-beam writer (IMS/NuFlare duopoly), Lasertec actinic inspection (a monopoly), Mitsui pellicles, and TOK/JSR/Shin-Etsu resists. Any one of these is as hard to replace as ASML, and several are single-source.
- **"Mask cost is a rounding error at $20–30M when a fab costs $20B." → Reality:** It is a rounding error for a product shipping 100,000 wafers (~$250/wafer) and a business-killer for one shipping 2,000 (~$12,500/wafer, half the wafer price). Mask-set cost is what determines which chips can economically exist at a leading node.
- **"Purity is the raw material's problem." → Reality:** Neon, HF and colloidal silica are all cheap and abundant as raw materials. The chokepoint is the last purification and packaging step, which is why two Ukrainian purifiers, two Osaka HF plants and one Japanese silica maker each mattered more than the mines and air plants behind them.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 03 ended with a polished, epi-ready 300 mm silicon wafer; this module adds the other substrates that enter fabs beside it (150–200 mm SiC, GaN-on-Si and GaN-on-SiC epiwafers, 100–150 mm GaAs, 75–100 mm InP) and the entire stream of consumables that the front end will pour over those wafers: Japanese resists and ancillaries from JSR, TOK and Shin-Etsu; bulk nitrogen from an Air Liquide or Linde plant next door and NF3/WF6/HBr from SK Specialty and Resonac; ppt-grade acids from BASF, Stella Chemifa and Morita; ultrapure water from an on-site plant; slurries and pads from Entegris, Fujimi and DuPont/Qnity; targets from JX and Honeywell; precursors from Merck and Adeka; quartz and SiC parts from Heraeus and CoorsTek; and photomasks built on Hoya/AGC blanks, written on IMS/NuFlare tools and inspected on Lasertec, either in TSMC's own mask shop or at Photronics, Tekscend and DNP. Module 05 walks into the fab that receives all of this, and Modules 06 through 12 consume it step by step: the epi and ALD/CVD chapters use the SiGe, precursors and gases, the lithography chapters use the resists and masks, etch uses the fluorocarbons and HBr and the focus rings, and interconnect uses the targets and slurries. The InP lasers and SiC/GaN power devices resurface in Module 19, in the optics and power delivery of a GB200 rack, and the Japan-concentration and Ga/Ge/neon dependencies return as the risk register of Module 20.
