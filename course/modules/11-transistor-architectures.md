# Module 11: Building a Transistor: Planar to FinFET to GAA to CFET

Picture a slab of silicon. Near its surface two small pockets have been doped so that they conduct well: the **source**, where current enters, and the **drain**, where it leaves. Between them lies a strip of ordinary silicon, today about 15 nm long; on top of it sits an insulator ~1 nm thick, and on top of that a conductor, the **gate**. Put a voltage on the gate and its electric field pulls electrons to the silicon surface, opening a thin conducting **channel** between source and drain; take the voltage away and the channel closes. The channel's **length** is the distance current travels from source to drain, set by the gate; its **width** is the size across, perpendicular to the current, and it is what you enlarge for more current. That is a transistor, and a modern logic transistor has to do two competing things: carry current easily when selected, and suppress current when deselected. This balance lets one small control voltage switch a useful signal without wasting too much power while idle.

Everything in this module follows from that one problem. As the channel shrinks, the drain competes with the gate for control of the channel, the off state gets leaky, and the only cure is to surround the channel with more gate on more sides. That is the entire physical reason the industry went planar → FinFET → gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheet → and is now heading toward the complementary FET (CFET). The other half of the module is the process-integration reality: how you actually make a 6 nm wide fin, replace a sacrificial gate with a hafnium-oxide/metal stack, or suspend three silicon ribbons in mid-air and wrap them in a gate. Modules 06 through 10 gave you the unit processes; this module strings them together into a transistor.

By the end you will be able to explain why room-temperature physics caps how low the supply voltage can go (the 60 mV per decade wall), why every leading-edge gate is built twice (the replacement metal gate), what the inner spacer in a nanosheet does and why it is the hardest step in the flow, why the power grid is moving to the back of the wafer, and what a node name like "N2" or "18A" really measures (nothing on the chip; the honest metrics are gate pitch, metal pitch, cell height and transistors per square millimetre).

## Before you start

- **Doping** (Module 10). A few phosphorus or arsenic atoms per million, each donating a free electron, make silicon **n-type**; boron atoms, each leaving a missing electron, a **hole**, that moves like a positive charge, make it **p-type**. "n+" or "p+" means heavily doped, ~1,000× more than the body. A **p-n junction** passes current one way and blocks it the other (a diode).
- **Capacitors and the dielectric constant** (school physics, Module 06). Two conductors separated by an insulator store charge Q = C × V, with C = ε × area / thickness. The insulator's **dielectric constant κ** (kappa; the "k" in high-k and low-k) is how many times more charge it stores than vacuum: SiO₂ 3.9, silicon 11.7, HfO₂ ~20.
- **Thermal energy** (school physics). At room temperature every particle carries ~kT ≈ 26 meV of random thermal energy (milli-electron-volts; an **electron-volt (eV)** is the energy an electron gains falling through one volt) (k is Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature, ~300 K), and the number of electrons with an extra energy E above the average falls ~10× for every 60 meV. This sets the floor on transistor leakage.
- **Epitaxy, CVD and ALD** (Module 06). **Epitaxy** grows single-crystal material that continues the lattice of the surface below; **chemical vapour deposition (CVD)** grows films from reacting gases; **atomic layer deposition (ALD)** alternates self-limiting gas pulses to build a calibrated thickness increment (~0.1 nm in the representative example). A cycle need not equal one complete atomic plane, and deep surfaces need enough reactant exposure.
- **Lithography pitch and pitch multiplication** (Modules 07–08). **Pitch** is the centre-to-centre repeat of identical lines. **Deep ultraviolet (DUV)** 193 nm immersion scanners ("193i") print ~80 nm pitch; **self-aligned double and quadruple patterning (SADP, SAQP)** grow spacers on the sidewalls of sacrificial lines to double or quadruple line density; **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** 13.5 nm light prints ~28 nm pitch in one exposure; **High-NA EUV** collects a wider range of light angles with reflective optics for ~1.7× finer resolution.
- **Plasma etch** (Module 09). Ions plus a reactive gas remove material: **anisotropic** straight down, **isotropic** equally in all directions including sideways. **Selectivity** is how much faster the target etches than its neighbours; **aspect ratio** is depth divided by width.
- **Ion implantation and annealing** (Module 10). Dopants are shot in as ions and must be **activated** at ~1,000 °C to take lattice sites. Everything already on the wafer must survive that heat: the **thermal budget**, which dictates the order of the whole flow.
- **Chemical mechanical polishing (CMP)** (Modules 03, 06). The wafer is pressed face-down on a rotating pad with abrasive, chemically active slurry until it is flat to about a nanometre, so each new layer starts on a plane.
- **Logic gates and cells** (programming background). An **inverter** outputs the opposite of its input, a **NAND** outputs 0 only when both inputs are 1, and a **flip-flop** stores one bit, updated once per clock tick. A chip is a few billion such gates wired together.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## 1. MOSFET Operation Refresher

### 1.1 Inversion and threshold voltage

A **MOSFET** (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) is a capacitor with a semiconductor as one plate. The metal plate is the gate, the insulator is the gate oxide, and the silicon body is the other plate. For an n-channel device (**NMOS**) the body is p-type silicon (boron-doped, conducting by holes) and the source and drain are heavily n-doped (n+) pockets. With zero gate voltage the two n+ pockets are separated by p-type material, which means by two p-n junctions pointing in opposite directions: two back-to-back diodes, no current either way. Now apply a positive gate-to-source voltage, written **V_GS**. The gate's field first pushes the holes away from the surface, leaving a layer that contains only the fixed, negatively charged boron atoms and no mobile carriers: **depletion**. Raise V_GS further and, above a critical voltage, the field pulls in enough electrons from the source to form a thin n-type layer at the surface: **inversion** (the surface has inverted from p-type to n-type). The inversion layer is a conducting sheet, ~1–2 nm thick, that connects source to drain. The gate voltage at which the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density is the **threshold voltage V_T**. The supply voltage of the chip, the voltage a logic "1" sits at, is **V_DD**; a gate driven to V_DD is fully on.

For a bulk device,

V_T = V_FB + 2φ_F + Q_dep / C_ox

Three terms, each a voltage. **V_FB**, the flat-band voltage, is the gate voltage at which there is no field in the silicon at all; it is set by the **work function** of the gate material (the energy needed to pull an electron out of it, Section 3.2) relative to silicon's, plus any stray charge trapped in the oxide. **φ_F**, the **Fermi potential** of the body, is the voltage by which doping has pushed the body's carrier balance away from undoped silicon, in other words a measure of how strongly p-type the body is (~0.3–0.45 V for typical doping); to take the surface from as-p-type-as-the-body to as-n-type-as-the-body, the electric potential at the silicon surface must be shifted by 2φ_F relative to the body (engineers say the energy bands are "bent"; Section 3.2 fills in the band picture). **Q_dep** is the depletion charge per unit area, the fixed boron charge the gate must hold at bay, and **C_ox** = ε_ox / t_ox is the gate-oxide capacitance per unit area (oxide permittivity over thickness), so Q_dep / C_ox is the voltage needed to support that charge across the oxide, Q = C × V rearranged. A worked number (illustrative, ~): a body doped at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ has φ_F ≈ 0.4 V, so 2φ_F ≈ 0.8 V; a gate metal with a work function of ~4.5 eV against that body gives V_FB ≈ −0.5 V (V_FB is the gate's work function minus silicon's, divided by the electron charge q so that eV becomes volts; p-type silicon doped at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ has an effective work function of ~5.0 eV, because its electrons sit φ_F ≈ 0.4 eV deeper than mid-gap, which in silicon is ~4.6 eV below vacuum, Section 3.2; so 4.5 − 5.0 = −0.5 V); and the depletion charge of that body under a 1 nm oxide needs Q_dep / C_ox ≈ 0.05 V; total V_T ≈ −0.5 + 0.8 + 0.05 ≈ 0.35 V, in the range of a real low-V_T device. Two knobs control V_T in production: the gate work function (the metal you choose in the gate stack) and the channel doping (Q_dep). Modern devices use the work function almost exclusively, because heavy channel doping kills mobility (defined next) and adds random dopant fluctuation (Section 4.1).

A MOSFET above threshold has two operating regions, and both names recur through the module. At small drain-to-source voltage V_DS the channel behaves like a resistor whose value the gate sets (the **linear region**, also called the triode region); once V_DS exceeds the **overdrive** V_GS − V_T (how far above threshold the gate is driven), the channel pinches off at the drain end and the current stops rising with V_DS (**saturation**), which is the on-state a switching logic gate lives in. Above threshold, the drain current in saturation is, in the simplest long-channel model,

I_D,sat = (1/2) μ C_ox (W/L) (V_GS − V_T)²

where W is the channel width, L the channel length, V_GS − V_T is the overdrive defined above, and μ is the carrier **mobility**: the drift speed a carrier picks up per unit of electric field, in cm² per volt-second. Electrons in bulk silicon have μ ≈ 1,400 cm²/V·s; squeezed against a rough oxide interface in a real channel they manage ~300–400 (~), and holes do worse. Read the equation as: current grows with capacitance (more charge per volt), with mobility (that charge moves faster), with width, and with the square of the overdrive, and it falls with length. In a real short-channel device the field is so high that carriers stop accelerating and hit **velocity saturation**, a top speed of ~10⁷ cm/s in silicon (a different use of the word: here it is the carrier's speed that saturates, not the current with V_DS). That is 100 km/s: an electron crosses a 15 nm channel in ~0.15 ps. Then I_D ≈ W C_ox v_sat (V_GS − V_T), linear in overdrive and independent of L: making the channel shorter no longer buys current, only density. This is why per-micron drive current (mA per µm of effective width) is the standard figure of merit; a good 2 nm class NMOS delivers ~1.0–1.3 mA/µm at V_DD ≈ 0.7 V, with PMOS nearly matched thanks to strain (deliberately stretching or squeezing the crystal to speed up carriers, Section 3.1).

The current budget makes the purpose of these quantities concrete. When a transistor’s gate is on it must pass on the order of 1 mA per micron of channel width: a three-fin device at the 5 nm class (fins are the vertical silicon ridges of Section 4; three of them, side by side, make one transistor) has ~0.3 µm of **effective width**, the total length of channel edge the gate touches, added up over all three fins (Section 4.1 does the arithmetic), so it passes ~0.3 mA, enough to charge a 1 fF wire (a femtofarad, 10⁻¹⁵ F, is the capacitance of a few microns of local wiring, Module 12) by 0.7 V: that takes Q = C × V = 0.7 fC of charge, delivered in 0.7 fC / 0.3 mA ≈ 2 ps (picoseconds, 10⁻¹² s), which is what a multi-GHz clock demands.

When its gate is off it must leak less than about 1 nA per micron: 1 nA/µm × ~0.1 µm of width per device × 10¹¹ devices ≈ 10 A of standby current on a GPU, ~7 W at 0.7 V, which is tolerable; ten times worse would be ~70 W of heat doing nothing, which is not. The ratio between those two states, I_on/I_off, therefore has to be roughly a million, and it has to be achieved with a control voltage of well under one volt, in a device whose channel is now shorter than the diameter of a virus (~12–16 nm of physical gate length at the 2 nm class of nodes).

### 1.2 Subthreshold swing and the 60 mV/decade wall

Below threshold the transistor is not truly off, and the reason is worth a mental picture before the equation.

> **Intuition: a thermal tail over a hill.** Think of the electrons in the source as air molecules and the source-to-channel boundary as a hill. Above absolute zero the molecules do not all have the same energy: most sit near the average, but a thin tail of fast ones has an extra energy E, and that fraction falls off exponentially, ~10× for every 60 meV at room temperature. Those tail electrons clear the hill and drift to the drain even when the gate says "off". The gate can raise the hill, but not directly: the gate oxide and the depleted silicon under the channel behave as two capacitors in series, which divide a voltage between them, so only a fraction C_ox / (C_ox + C_dep) of every millivolt on the gate actually raises the hill. That is the whole physics of leakage: a thermal tail (fixed by temperature) times a capacitive divider (fixed by geometry).

Electrons in the source have a **Boltzmann energy distribution**, the exponential tail just described, and the fraction that can surmount the source-to-channel barrier falls exponentially with the barrier height. The gate lowers that barrier only through the capacitive divider: a change ΔV_GS at the gate moves the surface potential by only ΔV_GS × C_ox / (C_ox + C_dep), where **C_dep** is the capacitance of the depletion layer under the channel. The result is the **subthreshold swing** (SS), the gate voltage needed to change I_D, the drain current, by a factor of ten. A **decade** here means a tenfold change in current, not a period of time. A smaller swing means the gate can change the current more sharply with voltage; it does not directly specify a switching time. The equation expresses that voltage requirement:

SS = (kT/q) ln(10) × (1 + C_dep / C_ox) = 59.6 mV/dec × m at 300 K

Here k is Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature, q the charge of one electron, and kT/q = 25.9 mV at 300 K is the thermal energy expressed as a voltage; ln(10) ≈ 2.3 converts "per e-fold" into "per factor of ten"; and m = 1 + C_dep/C_ox is the **body factor**, the inverse of the divider ratio. Worked: a well-designed device with C_dep/C_ox ≈ 0.1 has SS ≈ 59.6 × 1.1 ≈ 65 mV/dec. Because m ≥ 1 and kT/q is set by thermodynamics, no conventional MOSFET at room temperature can change its current more sharply than **~60 mV per decade**. Real production devices sit at 65–75 mV/dec. This number governs everything: if you need six decades between I_on and I_off, you need ~6 × 65 mV ≈ 0.4 V of gate swing just for the off-to-on transition, before you add the overdrive (V_GS − V_T) needed for drive current. That is why V_DD cannot fall much below ~0.6–0.7 V, and why V_T cannot be lowered without an exponential leakage penalty: every 65 mV of V_T reduction costs 10× the off-state current.

### 1.3 Short-channel effects, DIBL, and the scale length

In a long-channel device the surface potential in the middle of the channel is controlled entirely by the gate. In a short one, the depletion regions of the source and drain **junctions** (the boundaries where the n+ pockets meet the p-type body) reach into the channel and the drain's electric field lines terminate on channel charge that the gate "thinks" it owns. Consequences, collectively called **short-channel effects (SCE)**:

- **V_T roll-off**: V_T falls as L shrinks because the source/drain already deplete part of the channel; the gate has less charge to supply.
- **Drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL)**: raising the drain-to-source voltage V_DS lowers the source barrier and thus V_T. Measured as ΔV_T/ΔV_DS in mV/V; good FinFETs are at ~30–50 mV/V, a poorly scaled planar device at 100+ mV/V. What that means in practice: at V_DD = 0.7 V, 40 mV/V lowers V_T by ~28 mV whenever the drain is high, which by the 65 mV/dec rule is roughly half a decade, ~3× more leakage, on exactly the devices that are holding a logic 1 against a high drain. DIBL directly increases I_off at high drain bias and degrades the **output resistance**, a measure of how little the current changes when the drain voltage changes (a good switch should not care), which matters for analog circuits and for **SRAM**, the six-transistor on-chip memory cell of Section 8.2, whose stability depends on well-matched devices.
- **SS degradation**: the effective C_dep rises because the drain now couples to the channel capacitively.
- **Punch-through**: at the extreme, the source and drain depletion regions merge and current flows deep in the body where the gate has no control at all.

> **Intuition: the drain's field as a fog.** Think of the drain's electric field as a fog that seeps into the channel from the drain end and thins with distance; the distance over which it fades is the scale length λ. In a planar transistor the gate sits on one side only and the fog drifts deep under the channel where the gate cannot reach. Put gate on three sides (a fin) or four (a nanosheet) and the gate's own field crowds the fog out from every direction, so λ shrinks. The gate must be several λ long so the middle of the channel stays fog-free and answers only to the gate; when the gate length L_g falls to a few λ, the drain starts switching the transistor on by itself. Everything in Sections 4 and 6 is about shrinking λ.

The cleanest way to think about all of this is the **electrostatic scale length** λ. **Poisson's equation** is the rule that links charge to how the electric potential curves in space; solving it in the channel with the gate as a boundary condition gives a characteristic length over which the drain potential decays into the channel. For a single-gate planar device it is approximately

λ_planar ≈ sqrt( (ε_Si / ε_ox) × t_ox × t_dep )

where ε_Si and ε_ox are the permittivities of silicon and of the gate oxide (their ratio is the ratio of the dielectric constants, 11.7 / 3.9 = 3.0), t_ox is the oxide thickness, and t_dep is the depletion depth under the channel (tens of nm in bulk silicon). Read it as: the fog penetrates further the thicker the oxide and the deeper the uncontrolled silicon beneath the channel. For a double-gate or fin device with body (fin) thickness t_Si, the depletion depth is replaced by the geometric half-thickness of the body, and a widely used form (Frank, Taur, and Wong, 1998) is

λ_DG ≈ sqrt( (ε_Si / (2 ε_ox)) × t_Si × t_ox )

For a cylindrical gate-all-around nanowire of diameter d the scale length is smaller still, roughly λ_GAA ≈ sqrt( (ε_Si / (4 ε_ox)) × d × t_ox ) plus a logarithmic correction. To keep SCE under control the gate length must satisfy roughly

L_g ≥ 5–6 λ

The argument is now obvious. You can shrink λ by (a) thinning t_ox (which ran into the gate-leakage wall at ~1 nm, see Section 3), or (b) thinning the body t_Si and controlling it from more sides. A planar bulk device has t_dep ~30–50 nm and cannot get λ much below ~8–10 nm, so it cannot go below L_g ≈ 40–50 nm without leakage disaster; historically the planar era stalled at ~30 nm physical gate length around the 28/22 nm nodes. A fin 7 nm wide with gates on three sides behaves almost like a double-gate device with λ ~3–4 nm, allowing L_g ~16–20 nm. A nanosheet ~5–6 nm thick with the gate on all four sides pushes λ below 3 nm, allowing L_g ~12–14 nm.

> **Worked example: scale length for a FinFET vs a nanosheet.**
> Take ε_Si/ε_ox = 11.7/3.9 = 3.0 and an **equivalent oxide thickness** t_ox = 0.9 nm. (The real gate insulator is a HfO₂ stack ~2.4 nm thick; its equivalent oxide thickness is the thickness of pure SiO₂ that would give the same capacitance, which is what the formula's t_ox and ε_ox describe. Section 1.5 gives the conversion.)
> FinFET, fin width t_Si = 7 nm, double-gate approximation: λ = sqrt(3.0/2 × 7 nm × 0.9 nm) = sqrt(9.45 nm²) ≈ 3.1 nm. Minimum gate length ≈ 5 × 3.1 ≈ 15–18 nm. That is right where 5 nm class FinFETs sit (L_g ≈ 16–18 nm).
> Nanosheet, thickness 5.5 nm, four-sided gate: λ ≈ sqrt(3.0/4 × 5.5 nm × 0.9 nm) ≈ sqrt(3.7 nm²) ≈ 1.9 nm. Minimum gate length ≈ 10–12 nm. Intel's research RibbonFETs (its name for nanosheets, Section 6.1), reported at the IEDM conference in December 2024 (Section 2.2), reached L_g = 6 nm with 1.7 nm thick ribbons, exactly the regime this formula predicts (λ ≈ 1.1 nm). The catch is **quantum confinement**: in a sheet only a few atoms thick the electrons are squeezed into a **quantum well**, a region so thin that quantum mechanics allows an electron confined in it only certain discrete energies, much as a guitar string of a given length can only sound certain notes, and the shorter the string the higher the lowest note; so the lowest energy the electrons are allowed to occupy rises, and it takes more gate voltage to fill it, so V_T climbs steeply as the sheet thins below ~4–5 nm. At that point the thickness variation of a single atomic layer (0.14 nm) shifts V_T by ~10–20 mV (~) from device to device, which is why production sheets stay at ~5–7 nm.

### 1.4 Dennard scaling and why it ended

In 1974 Robert Dennard's group at IBM showed that if you scale every dimension of a MOSFET (L, W, t_ox) by 1/s, scale the voltage by 1/s, and raise the doping by s (we write the scaling factor as s to avoid a clash with Boltzmann's constant k of Section 1.2), then the electric fields stay constant, delay falls by 1/s, transistor density rises by s², and the power density (W/mm²) stays exactly constant. That is **Dennard scaling**, and it worked for thirty years: s = √2 every generation gave 2× transistors, ~1.4× frequency, and the same heat per square millimetre.

It broke around 2004–2005 because voltage stopped scaling. V_DD can only come down if V_T comes down (you need V_DD − V_T overdrive for current), and V_T cannot come down because of the 60 mV/dec wall: I_off grows 10× per 65 mV. Once V_DD stalled at ~1 V while dimensions kept shrinking, power density rose every generation. The public symptom was Intel's 90 nm Prescott Pentium 4 (2004) at 100+ W and 3.8 GHz, with leakage (subthreshold plus gate tunnelling) eating tens of watts (~); the 4 GHz part was cancelled and the industry pivoted to multi-core. Every node since has had to buy its improvement from new physics (strain, high-k, FinFET, GAA) rather than geometry alone.

### 1.5 The classic scaling levers

Three levers carried the industry from 90 nm to 22 nm without changing the planar shape of the transistor:

1. **Gate oxide EOT.** The **equivalent oxide thickness** (EOT) is the thickness of pure SiO₂ that would give the same capacitance as the actual dielectric: EOT = t_phys × (3.9/κ), where 3.9 is SiO₂'s dielectric constant and κ the actual material's. Thinner EOT → higher C_ox → more inversion charge per volt and better electrostatics. SiO₂ was scaled from ~10 nm at 0.5 µm to 1.2 nm at 65 nm, at which point **direct tunnelling** stopped it: quantum mechanics lets an electron pass straight through an insulating barrier thinner than ~2 nm without ever having the energy to climb over it, with a probability that rises ~10× for every 0.2–0.3 nm removed. At 1.2 nm the leakage reached ~100 A/cm². To give that a scale: a 100 mm² chip has on the order of 1–2 mm² of total gate area (~), so 100 A/cm² is ~1–2 A flowing through the gate insulators, several watts at 1.2 V, and every one of those watts is pure waste.
2. **Strain engineering.** Mechanically stretching silicon along the channel increases electron mobility; compressing it increases hole mobility. Gains of 20–50% in drive current for "free" (Section 3.1).
3. **High-k/metal gate (HKMG).** A **high-k dielectric** is an insulator whose dielectric constant is several times SiO₂'s 3.9, so it can be physically thick (little tunnelling) yet electrically thin (high capacitance). Replace SiO₂ with a κ ≈ 20 material and you get both at once (Section 3.2).

> **Why it matters downstream:** the subthreshold swing and the menu of threshold voltages set the leakage-versus-speed trade that every chip design makes. When Module 19 describes a **synthesis tool** (the software that turns a designer's logic description into a list of standard cells to be placed and wired) choosing between a fast, leaky cell and a slow, tight one for each path in a GPU, the two options differ by exactly the 65 mV/dec arithmetic of Section 1.2, and the choice is available only because the gate stack of Section 3.3 can be built in several V_T flavours.

## 2. CMOS Basics and What a "Node" Actually Measures

### 2.1 NMOS, PMOS, the inverter, and the standard cell

A digital output must be driven toward one of two voltages: the positive supply, written V_DD, or ground, the reference voltage. An inverter uses two complementary switches. A low input turns on the path to the supply and turns off the path to ground, so the output becomes high. A high input reverses those roles, so the output becomes low. The transistors control the output voltage by deciding which path conducts; the input does not simply flow through to become the output.

**CMOS** (complementary MOS) pairs an NMOS and a **PMOS** (p-channel, p+ source/drain in an n-type body, turned on by a negative V_GS) so that in either logic state one of the two is off and no static current flows from V_DD to ground. The inverter is a PMOS on top (source to V_DD) and an NMOS below (source to ground) sharing a gate (input) and a drain (output). Because holes are slower than electrons in unstrained silicon (~2–2.5× lower mobility), classic designs made the PMOS wider; in FinFET and nanosheet processes with SiGe strain the p/n current ratio is close to 1:1 and cells typically use equal fin/sheet counts.

Logic chips are not drawn transistor by transistor. They are assembled from a library of a few hundred **standard cells**, pre-designed layouts of small logic functions: INV (inverter), NAND2 and NOR2 (two-input gates), **AOI** (and-or-invert, a compound gate), **flip-flops** (1-bit storage elements updated once per clock tick) and their **scan** variants (a flip-flop with an extra test input so that the chip's state can be shifted out and inspected, Module 14), all of the same height and varying width, placed in rows. Above the transistors sit the **metal layers**, the copper wiring stack of Module 12, numbered upward from **M0** (the lowest, tightest layer) through M1, M2 and so on. Cell **height** is measured in **tracks**: the number of minimum-pitch horizontal metal lines (M0 or M1, whichever is the first routing layer parallel to the cell) that fit within it; one track is one wiring lane. A 6-track (6T) cell with a 40 nm metal pitch is 240 nm tall. (Beware a naming collision: "6T" here means six tracks; in Section 8.2 "6T SRAM" means six transistors. The two are unrelated.) Cell **width** is a multiple of the **contacted poly pitch (CPP)**, also called contacted gate pitch: the centre-to-centre distance between adjacent gates. The name is historical: gate lines were polysilicon for forty years and are still called "poly" even though they have been metal since 2007. One CPP must accommodate a gate, two **spacers** (the insulating sidewalls that separate each gate from its source and drain, Section 3.3), and a source/drain contact. Physically a cell is a strip of PMOS devices above a strip of NMOS devices; each gate line runs vertically across both strips, so one line makes one PMOS and one NMOS at once, and the metal tracks run horizontally along the row. A NAND2 has two gate lines (one per input) and therefore four transistors: two PMOS in parallel pulling the output up and two NMOS in series pulling it down. It is typically 3 CPP wide (two gates plus a half-CPP boundary on each side); a scan flip-flop is ~20–24 CPP.

### 2.2 CPP, MP, fin pitch, cell height: the true node metrics

**Moore's law** is the observation (Gordon Moore, 1965, revised 1975) that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. Since about 2009 the "nm" in a node name has had no fixed relationship to any dimension on the chip. It is a marketing continuation of the old Moore's-law cadence (each name ≈ 0.7× the previous), chosen so that a "5 nm" node delivers roughly the density the trend line would have predicted. Decoding the names: TSMC's "N7", "N5", "N3", "N2" mean "node 7 nm" and so on, with suffixes for variants (N3E enhanced, N2P performance), and from "A16" the number is in angstroms (1.6 nm); Intel renamed its nodes in 2021 to match ("Intel 7", "Intel 4", "Intel 3") and then moved to angstroms too ("18A", "14A"); Samsung's "7LPP" and "5LPE" are 7 nm and 5 nm "low-power plus" and "low-power early", and "3GAE" / "3GAP" are 3 nm gate-all-around early and plus, later rebranded "SF3E" / "SF3" (Samsung Foundry 3). Nothing on a TSMC N3 chip is 3 nm: the physical gate length is ~16 nm, the fin width ~6 nm, the tightest metal pitch 23 nm, the gate oxide ~1 nm EOT. The honest metrics are:

- **CPP** (gate pitch): sets cell width. 90 nm (TSMC N16) → ~45–48 nm (N3/N2), and now nearly stuck: below ~45 nm you cannot fit gate (~14 nm) + two spacers (~5 nm each) + contact (~15 nm) + margins.
- **Metal pitch (MP)**: the tightest interconnect pitch (M0/M1); sets cell height via track count. 64 nm at N16 → 23 nm at N3; sub-25 nm needs EUV double patterning (two EUV exposures interleaved) or High-NA.
- **Fin pitch (FP)**: determines how many fins fit in a cell height. 48 nm (N16) → 26–28 nm (N5/N3). For nanosheets it is replaced by sheet width plus n-to-p separation.
- **Cell height**: tracks × MP, or (fins per device × 2, one NMOS and one PMOS, plus the empty fin pitches needed to separate the n device from the p device) × FP, whichever binds. 9T (~576 nm at N16) → ~5–6T (~160–180 nm) today.
- **Transistor density** in **MTr/mm²** (millions of transistors per mm²). Intel's 2017 standard: 0.6 × (NAND2 density) + 0.4 × (scan flip-flop density), each cell's transistor count divided by its area. Marketing figures assume the densest library at 100% **utilization** (the fraction of the die area actually filled with cells rather than left open for wiring); real chips achieve 50–70%.

Each process is offered as more than one library: a **high-density (HD)** library with short cells for low power and area, and a **high-performance (HP)** library with taller cells (more fins or wider sheets) for speed. Foundries disclose their pitches, when they disclose them at all, at two conferences: **IEDM** (the IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting, each December) and the **VLSI Symposium** (each June). **High-volume manufacturing (HVM)** is the year a node first shipped product in volume. Two lessons jump out. First, Intel 10 nm (2018) and TSMC N7 (2018) are the same density class; Intel 7 is a rename of 10 nm Enhanced SuperFin, made to align the marketing name with TSMC's. Second, the density gain per node has fallen from 2× (N16 → N10 → N7) to ~1.15× (N3E → N2). CPP has hit its floor and MP is limited by resistance and EUV double patterning, so the remaining density lever is cell height. Every row of cells has a V_DD wire running along its top edge and a ground wire along its bottom edge, the **power rails**, and they occupy about two of the six tracks; if power arrives from below the wafer instead (backside power, Section 7.3), those two tracks can go. That, and CFET (which stacks n over p, Section 7.2), are the next steps.

> **Worked example: transistor density from CPP, MP, and cell height.**
> Estimate the NAND2 density for TSMC N7: CPP = 57 nm, MP = 40 nm, 6-track cell.
> Cell height = 6 × 40 nm = 240 nm. A NAND2 is 3 CPP wide = 171 nm.
> NAND2 area = 171 nm × 240 nm = 41,040 nm² = 0.0410 µm². It contains 4 transistors (two gate lines, each crossing the PMOS strip and the NMOS strip, Section 2.1), so NAND2 density = 4 / 0.0410 µm² = 97.5 MTr/mm².
> A scan D flip-flop with ~24 transistors in ~20 CPP: area = 1,140 × 240 = 273,600 nm² = 0.274 µm², density 87.7 MTr/mm².
> Intel-formula density = 0.6 × 97.5 + 0.4 × 87.7 ≈ 93.6 MTr/mm², a good match to the 91.2 MTr/mm² figure quoted for N7 HD.
> Repeat for N5 (CPP 51, MP 30, 6T = 180 nm): NAND2 = 153 × 180 = 27,540 nm² → 145 MTr/mm². TechInsights' measured value on Apple silicon was ~138 MTr/mm²; TSMC's 171 MTr/mm² claim used a 1.8× scaling factor from N7 that assumed the tightest library.

> **Worked example: transistors on an 800 mm² die.**
> An AI accelerator die of 800 mm² on a 5 nm class process: at the 138 MTr/mm² library peak it could hold 800 × 138 M = 110 billion transistors. NVIDIA's H100 (TSMC 4N, an N5-derived process tuned for NVIDIA; 814 mm²) holds 80 billion, i.e. 98 MTr/mm², ~70% of peak. The gap is real and structural: ~30–40% of the die is **SRAM** (static random-access memory, the on-chip cache, whose six-transistor bit cell is not on the logic scaling curve), 10–15% is **I/O** (the circuits that drive signals off the chip), **HBM PHYs** (the physical-layer interfaces to the stacked high-bandwidth memory next to the die, Module 17) and analog blocks that use much larger devices, and logic placement rarely exceeds 70–80% utilization because of routing congestion and the power grid. On N3E at ~210 MTr/mm² peak the same die could hold ~170 B at peak, or ~115–125 B at a realistic 70%. For a 2025 comparison, NVIDIA's Blackwell puts ~104 B transistors on each of its two reticle-sized dies (as large as a scanner can expose in one shot, Module 07) on the N4P-derived 4NP process (~), the same ~100 MTr/mm² effective class as H100.

> **What can go wrong: claimed versus measured density.** A foundry's density claim is a library number: the tightest cell, 100% utilization, and a scaling factor applied to the previous node. A teardown is a measurement on a real product. For N5 the claim was 171 MTr/mm² and TechInsights measured ~138 on Apple silicon, a 20% gap, and across recent nodes the teardown houses have found measured densities landing 15–25% below the claims. The engineer's habit is to ask which library, at what utilization, and whether the number was measured or chained from a "1.8× over the previous node" statement. The numbers for N3E, N2, A16 and Intel 18A in the table are chained estimates and should be read that way.

> **Why it matters downstream:** MTr/mm² times a realistic utilization tells you how big a die a given design needs, and die area drives everything in the back half of this course: the number of dies per 300 mm wafer, the yield (a fixed defect density kills a larger fraction of large dies, Module 13), whether the design must be split into chiplets (Module 17), and ultimately cost per chip (Module 20). A 15% density gain per node, against a 50% wafer-price increase, is the arithmetic behind the industry's cost anxiety.

## 3. The Planar Era: Strain and HKMG

### 3.1 90 nm: strained silicon (2003)

Why strain: by 90 nm the classic levers were nearly spent, and the industry needed more current from the same geometry. Silicon's mobility is not a fixed constant of nature; it depends on the exact spacing of the atoms, and you can change that spacing by pulling or squeezing the crystal. Intel's 90 nm process (Prescott, 2003–2004) was the first to ship **uniaxial strain**, strain applied along one direction, the direction of current flow. Two techniques, still used today:

- **Embedded SiGe source/drain for PMOS.** After gate and spacer formation the PMOS source/drain (S/D) regions are etched out (~60–80 nm recess) and refilled by **selective epitaxy** (epitaxial growth that **nucleates**, that is, forms its first few atomic layers and starts growing, only on exposed silicon and not on the surrounding oxide or nitride) with Si_{1−x}Ge_x, a silicon crystal in which a fraction x of the atoms are germanium. Germanium's **lattice constant**, the repeat spacing of atoms in the crystal, is 4.2% larger than silicon's (0.543 nm), so SiGe forced to match the silicon lattice wants to expand and squeezes the channel between the two S/D regions, putting it under **compressive** strain along the current direction; this lifts hole mobility (see the box below). Intel used 17% Ge at 90 nm, 23% at 65 nm, 30% at 45 nm; today's SiGe:B source/drains (the ":B" means boron-doped during growth, "in situ", rather than implanted) are graded to 50–65% Ge with boron at ~1 × 10²¹ cm⁻³. That concentration is worth pausing on: silicon has 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so 10²¹ cm⁻³ means roughly 2% of all atoms are boron, far beyond what equilibrium solubility allows, frozen in place by fast growth at low temperature. Gains: ~25% drive current at 90 nm, > 50% cumulative later.
- **Tensile nitride stress liner for NMOS.** The silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) film deposited over the finished gate by **plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD)**, a low-temperature CVD in which a plasma rather than heat drives the reaction (Module 06), also serves as the contact etch-stop layer (**CESL**). Its internal stress can be tuned to ~1–2 GPa of intrinsic **tensile** stress via hydrogen content and plasma conditions; 1 GPa is roughly 10,000 atmospheres of pull. The film pulls on the gate and transmits tension into the channel, raising electron mobility ~10–20%. A **dual stress liner** flow patterned tensile film over NMOS and compressive over PMOS; Si:P or SiC:P embedded S/D (phosphorus-doped silicon or silicon-carbon, whose smaller lattice pulls the channel) later provided NMOS tensile strain directly.

> **Intuition: why stretching a crystal changes how fast carriers move.** An electron or hole moving through a crystal is not a free particle; it is constantly interacting with the periodic array of atoms, and the net effect is that it behaves as if it had a different mass, the **effective mass**, set by the atomic spacing and symmetry of the lattice. A lighter effective mass means more acceleration in the same field: higher mobility. Holes in silicon come in a heavy, slow flavour and a light, fast one, and in unstrained silicon most are heavy. Squeezing the lattice along the channel shifts more holes into the light flavour and reduces the scattering between the two, which is why compressive strain can give PMOS 25–50% more current; stretching does the analogous favour for electrons. The full story is band theory (Taur and Ning, Further Reading); the picture to keep is that mobility is a property of the crystal's geometry, and geometry can be engineered.

Strain scales badly with pitch: as CPP shrinks, so does the S/D volume available to push on the channel, one reason nanosheet nodes are re-engineering strain through the channel itself (SiGe channels for PMOS; "stress engineering" is a listed lever in TSMC's N2 disclosure). The epi reactors that grow these source/drains are Applied Materials' Centura and ASM's Intrepid single-wafer chambers (Module 06).

### 3.2 45 nm: high-k metal gate (Intel, 2007)

By 65 nm the gate dielectric was **SiON**, silicon oxynitride, SiO₂ with some nitrogen added for a slightly higher κ and a better barrier against dopant diffusion, and it was 1.2 nm thick (~4 atomic layers); tunnelling leakage was ~100 A/cm², a significant fraction of chip power. A second, less obvious problem was **polysilicon depletion**. **Polysilicon** is a deposited film made of many tiny silicon crystals, doped heavily so that it conducts; it had been the gate material since the 1970s because it survives the source/drain anneal and can be doped n-type or p-type to set V_T. But it is still a semiconductor: under inversion bias a ~0.3–0.5 nm depletion layer forms in it at the oxide interface, adding electrically to the EOT; on a 1.2 nm oxide that is a ~33% loss of gate capacitance. Metal gates have no depletion layer.

Intel's 45 nm process (Penryn, November 2007) replaced SiON with hafnium-based high-k. **HfO₂**, hafnium dioxide, κ ≈ 20–25, is grown by **atomic layer deposition (ALD)**: the wafer is exposed alternately to hafnium tetrachloride (HfCl₄) vapour and to water vapour at ~300 °C, each pulse reacting only with the surface the previous one left, so the film builds one atomic layer (~0.1 nm) per cycle with perfect uniformity (Module 06). The stack was ~1.0 nm EOT and ~2–3 nm physical thickness over a ~0.5 nm SiO₂ **interfacial layer**: a thin oxide that inevitably forms between silicon and HfO₂ and is in fact needed, because electrons move far better along a silicon/SiO₂ interface than along a silicon/HfO₂ one. Poly was replaced with metal. Gate leakage fell more than 10× while C_ox rose. Two problems had to be solved first, and they define how the gate is built today:

> **Intuition: work function, band edges and pinning.** An **electron-volt (eV)** is the energy an electron gains falling through one volt. The **work function** of a solid is the energy, in eV, needed to pull an electron out of it into vacuum. In silicon the electrons that matter live in two bands separated by a 1.1 eV gap: the top of the filled band (the valence band, home of the holes) sits ~5.15 eV below vacuum and the bottom of the empty band (the conduction band, home of the free electrons) at ~4.05 eV. These two **band edges** are the targets: a gate whose work function matches the conduction-band edge (~4.05–4.3 eV, "n-like") gives an NMOS a low V_T, one matching the valence-band edge (~4.9–5.15 eV, "p-like") gives a PMOS a low V_T, and one in the middle (**mid-gap**, ~4.6 eV) gives both a V_T that is too high. Doped polysilicon could be made either flavour; metals cannot be doped, so you need two different metals. And there is a trap, **Fermi-level pinning**: charged defects at the HfO₂ interface (oxygen vacancies, sites where an oxygen atom is missing, and **dipoles**, layers of separated positive and negative charge) drag the metal's effective work function toward mid-gap, so the metal you deposit is not the work function you get. Gate-stack engineering is largely the art of un-pinning it.

1. **Fermi-level pinning and V_T.** As the box explains, pinning drags a metal's effective work function toward mid-gap, so two different, un-pinned metal stacks are needed to reach the band-edge targets: ~4.1–4.3 eV for NMOS and ~4.9–5.1 eV for PMOS.
2. **Thermal budget.** The work-function metals and the high-k are not stable through the ~1,000 °C source/drain activation anneal: the metal work function drifts toward mid-gap and the interfacial layer regrows. This is the reason for gate-last.

### 3.3 Gate-first vs gate-last, and the replacement metal gate flow

**Gate-first** (used by IBM, Samsung, and GlobalFoundries at 32/28 nm) deposits high-k and metal early, in the same position as the old poly gate, then runs the full S/D implant and anneal over it. It is simpler and cheaper but the V_T window is compromised by the anneal, and it was abandoned by everyone at 20/14 nm.

**Gate-last**, or **replacement metal gate (RMG)**, is Intel's 45 nm approach and the industry standard since. A sacrificial ("dummy") polysilicon gate is used to define the transistor, all high-temperature steps are done, and only then is the poly pulled out and replaced. Step by step:

1. Grow a thin dummy oxide (~2 nm thermal SiO₂) and deposit ~80–100 nm of **amorphous** silicon (deposited cold enough that its atoms have no crystalline order at all, like glass; it turns into polysilicon on later heating) or polysilicon, plus a silicon nitride (SiN) or SiO₂ **hardmask**, a hard film that carries the pattern into the etch because photoresist alone would erode before an 80 nm gate etch finished.
2. Pattern the gate lines (litho + etch, Modules 07/09). The poly line width defines L_g.
3. Form spacers by ALD of a **low-k** dielectric (κ below SiO₂'s 3.9, so the gate-to-contact capacitance is smaller): silicon oxycarbonitride (SiOCN) or silicon boron carbonitride (SiBCN), ~5–8 nm per side, deposited everywhere and then anisotropically etched back so that it survives only on the vertical gate sidewalls.
4. Recess and epitaxially regrow source/drain (SiGe:B for PMOS, Si:P for NMOS), then anneal: a **spike anneal**, a lamp anneal that ramps to ~1,050 °C and straight back down so the peak lasts a fraction of a second, activating the dopants while barely letting them diffuse, or a millisecond laser anneal (Module 10). This is the last > 900 °C step the gate will ever see.
5. Deposit the CESL nitride and the **ILD0** (interlayer dielectric zero, a flowable or **high-density-plasma (HDP)** CVD oxide; HDP-CVD etches and deposits at once so it fills gaps without voids), then **CMP** the oxide down until the tops of the poly gates are exposed (the "poly open" polish). Planarity here is critical: over-polish and you thin the gates unevenly; under-polish and some gates stay buried and cannot be replaced (a killer defect).
6. **Remove the dummy poly** by a selective wet etch (hot ammonium hydroxide, NH₄OH, or tetramethylammonium hydroxide, TMAH, both of which dissolve silicon but not oxide or nitride) or a dry etch, stopping on the dummy oxide, then strip the dummy oxide in dilute hydrofluoric acid (HF). You are left with an empty trench between the spacers, ~20 nm wide and ~80 nm deep, with the bare fin (or planar channel) at the bottom.
7. Grow a controlled **interfacial layer** (chemical oxide, ~0.5–0.8 nm, by ozone or **SC1** chemistry, the standard ammonia-plus-hydrogen-peroxide-plus-water clean) and deposit **HfO₂ by ALD** (~1.5–2 nm). Some flows ("high-k first") deposit the high-k under the dummy gate and keep it; most leading-edge flows are "high-k last" as described here so the dielectric never sees the S/D anneal. A short post-deposition anneal (~700–900 °C for milliseconds to seconds, in nitrogen or ammonia, NH₃) densifies the film and passivates **traps**, defects that catch electrons and shift V_T.
8. Deposit the **work-function metal (WFM) stack** by ALD. A typical sequence: a titanium nitride (TiN) cap (~1 nm) to protect the high-k, then for PMOS a thick TiN (or TiN/tantalum nitride, TaN) that gives a high work function (~4.8–5.0 eV); for NMOS a thin TiN, then a titanium aluminium (TiAl) or titanium aluminium carbide (TiAlC) layer (~2–4 nm) whose aluminium lowers the effective work function to ~4.2–4.4 eV, then a TiN cap. The PMOS-only layers are deposited everywhere, patterned, and wet-etched off the NMOS trenches before the NMOS layers go down, so a modern flow has 4–8 metal depositions and 2–4 masking steps inside the gate trench.
9. **Multiple threshold voltages** come from this same stack. The WFM thickness sets V_T continuously over ~100–200 mV (a thicker TiN shifts PMOS V_T; the TiAl thickness and its Al diffusion shift NMOS V_T), and **dipole engineering** adds more: an ultrathin (~0.3–0.5 nm) lanthanum oxide (La₂O₃ or LaO) layer driven into the HfO₂/SiO₂ interface creates a dipole, a sheet of separated positive and negative charge that acts like a tiny built-in battery adding or subtracting ~100 mV from V_T, and it lowers NMOS V_T; an aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) dipole shifts V_T the other way, which for a PMOS (whose V_T is negative) brings it closer to zero, i.e. lowers its |V_T|. Combining thickness and dipole options gives the 4–6 V_T flavours a foundry's **process design kit (PDK)**, the rulebook and cell library it hands to chip designers, offers: extra-low, low, standard, high and ultra-high V_T (eLVT, LVT, SVT, HVT, uHVT), each with its own leakage/speed trade-off, and all made in the same trench with different mask combinations.
10. **Fill** the remaining trench with a low-resistance metal: originally tungsten (W) by CVD (tungsten hexafluoride, WF₆, with diborane, B₂H₆, for nucleation, seeding the first few atomic layers of tungsten on the barrier, where the bulk chemistry cannot start growing by itself, then WF₆ + H₂ for bulk, on a TiN barrier), later cobalt (Co), and in nanosheet nodes W or molybdenum (Mo) with fluorine-free precursors because WF₆ attacks thin WFMs. Fill must be void-free in a trench whose **aspect ratio** (depth ÷ width), after the WFM layers narrowed it, may exceed 5:1.
11. **CMP** the metal back to the ILD0 surface, then **recess** the metal gate ~20–30 nm below the surface with a dry etch and fill the recess with SiN: the **self-aligned contact (SAC) cap**. Its purpose: when the S/D contact trench is etched later it can overlap the gate without shorting to it, because the nitride cap and spacers are etch-resistant relative to the ILD oxide. Without SAC, contact-to-gate **overlay** (the misalignment between one patterned layer and the one beneath it, ~2–3 nm 3σ, three standard deviations, on an EUV scanner) would need to be better than ~5 nm at 50 nm CPP; with it, ~10 nm is tolerable.

> **Worked example: the EOT of a real gate stack.** Take step 7's numbers: 1.8 nm of HfO₂ at κ = 20 over a 0.6 nm SiO₂ interfacial layer. The HfO₂ counts as 1.8 × 3.9 / 20 = 0.35 nm of equivalent oxide; the interfacial layer is SiO₂ and counts at face value, 0.6 nm. EOT ≈ 0.35 + 0.6 = 0.95 nm, from a physical stack 2.4 nm thick. Notice that the "unavoidable" interfacial layer is now two thirds of the electrical thickness, which is why so much effort goes into scavenging it down to 0.5 nm. On the leakage side: a naive extrapolation at 10× per 0.2–0.3 nm from a 1.2 nm SiO₂ film to a 2.4 nm stack would predict a reduction of ~1,000× or more (~); the measured gain at Intel's 45 nm was "more than 10×", smaller because HfO₂ presents a lower energy barrier to electrons than SiO₂ does, so each nanometre of it blocks less than a nanometre of oxide would. Either way, the stack went from 1.2 nm to 2.4 nm physical while getting electrically thinner.

RMG costs roughly 20–30 extra process steps over gate-first and brought CMP, ALD, and selective wet etching into the very centre of the transistor. Its virtue is that the fragile gate stack is the last thing built in the front end.

> **What can go wrong: the replacement gate.** *Buried gates*: an under-polished poly-open (step 5) leaves some gate tops covered by oxide; those gates are never opened, never replaced, and the transistor is dead, which is caught by scribe-line test structures (Section 8.4) that show a cluster of open circuits, and by cross-section imaging in a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Section 8.4). *Work-function metal thickness*: V_T shifts ~10–20 mV per 0.1 nm of TiAl or TiN, so a ± 0.1 nm ALD drift across the wafer is a ± 10–20 mV V_T spread; it is monitored by V_T arrays in the scribe line and by X-ray fluorescence on blanket monitor wafers. *Fill seams and voids*: tungsten grows inward from both walls of the trench and meets in the middle; a seam or void there raises gate resistance and slows the gate's own charging; TEM finds it. *Interfacial-layer regrowth*: oxygen diffusing through the HfO₂ during the post-deposition anneal thickens the SiO₂ layer and the EOT grows by a few tenths of a nanometre, visible as a drop in gate capacitance on test structures. Who supplies the steps: ASM and Applied Materials (ALD high-k and WFM), Lam and Applied (W, Co and Mo fill), Applied and Ebara (CMP), TEL and SCREEN (the wet dummy-poly strip and cleans).

## 4. FinFET

### 4.1 Why fins, and Intel's 22 nm tri-gate (2011)

The FinFET, first demonstrated by Chenming Hu's group at UC Berkeley in 1999 under a program funded by **DARPA** (the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency), turns the channel on its side: the channel is a thin vertical **fin** of silicon and the gate wraps over its top and both sidewalls. Two sidewalls give double-gate electrostatics; the top adds a third (Intel's "tri-gate"). The fin thickness t_Si becomes the body thickness in the scale-length formula. And because the fin is **fully depleted**, meaning the gate's field reaches all the way through the 7 nm body so there is no uncontrolled silicon left for leakage to hide in, channel doping can be nearly **intrinsic** (essentially undoped, ~10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻³). That eliminates **random dopant fluctuation**, the V_T scatter that comes from counting statistics on a handful of dopant atoms, and it raises mobility because there are fewer charged atoms to scatter off. The count makes the point: a fin 7 nm wide, 50 nm tall and 16 nm long (one gate length) has a volume of 5,600 nm³ and holds ~2.8 × 10⁵ silicon atoms. At the planar era's 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ channel doping that is ~6 dopant atoms per transistor, and a counting-statistics spread of ± √6 ≈ 2–3 atoms shifted V_T by tens of mV from one device to the next; at 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ it is ~0.06 atoms, that is, usually none.

Intel shipped it first at 22 nm (Ivy Bridge, 2011–2012): fins 8 nm wide, 34 nm tall, 60 nm pitch, 90 nm gate pitch. Every Intel node since, and every foundry node from 16/14 nm (TSMC and Samsung/GlobalFoundries, 2015), is a FinFET until the 3/2 nm nanosheet transition. FinFET geometry evolved as follows:

| Node | Fin width (nm) | Fin height (nm) | Fin pitch (nm) | Patterning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intel 22 (2011) | ~8 | 34 | 60 | 193i SADP |
| Intel 14 (2014) | ~8 | 42 | 42 | 193i SADP |
| Intel 10 (2018) | ~7 | ~46–53 | 34 | 193i SAQP |
| TSMC N7 (2018) | ~6–7 | ~52 | 30 | 193i SAQP |
| TSMC N5 (2020) | ~6–7 | ~50–55 | 28 | EUV single |
| TSMC N3E (2023) | ~6 | ~50 | 26 | EUV single |

Fins got taller, narrower (better electrostatics), and closer together. Taller means more effective width per footprint: the gate touches the two sidewalls and the top, so the conducting perimeter of a 50 nm tall, 6 nm wide fin is W_eff = 2H + W = 2 × 50 + 6 = 106 nm (~110 nm per fin), even though the fin occupies only 26–28 nm of pitch. Width is limited by two things: below ~5 nm, quantum confinement (Section 1.3) and **line-edge roughness**, the random ~1–2 nm wiggle in the edge of any patterned line, make V_T uncontrollable; and the fin becomes too fragile to survive wet cleans. The rinse water trapped between two fins 20 nm apart has a curved surface whose surface tension pulls the fins together with a pressure of tens of atmospheres, the same **pattern collapse** that afflicts tall photoresist lines (Module 07), bending or snapping them. Height is limited by etch aspect ratio and by mechanical stability under the stress of the gate metal and epi.

### 4.2 Fin quantization, depopulation, and the fin cut

Because fins come only in integer numbers, device width is **quantized**: a transistor is 1, 2, or 3 fins wide, never 1.4. At 16/14 nm a typical cell used 3–4 fins per transistor (9T at 48 nm FP). **Fin depopulation** delivered most of the cell-height scaling since: taller fins carry more current each, so N7 moved to 2–3 fins per device (6T at 30 nm FP), N5 to 2, and N3E's **FinFlex** offers 2-1 (cells in one row use 2-fin devices and cells in the next row use 1-fin devices, an average of 1.5 fins per device), 2-2, and 3-2 libraries. You cannot go below one fin per device, and a 1-fin transistor has poor drive and high variability; that floor ends the FinFET and motivates nanosheets, where width is continuously tunable again.

> **Worked example: what fin quantization does to drive current.** A 2-fin NMOS at N5 with 106 nm of effective width per fin and 1.2 mA/µm delivers 2 × 0.106 µm × 1.2 mA/µm ≈ 0.25 mA; a 1-fin device delivers ~0.13 mA; a 3-fin device ~0.38 mA. Suppose a designer wants a cell that needs 0.19 mA, halfway between one and two fins: it does not exist. Two fins wastes area and switching energy; one fin is too slow. In a planar process you would simply draw the width 1.5× wider. FinFlex's 2-1 library is the workaround: alternate rows of 2-fin and 1-fin devices give an average of 1.5 fins per device across the cell, at the cost of a library that has to be laid out around two row types.

Fins are patterned as a continuous sea of lines across the die at one pitch (what SADP/SAQP produce; Module 07) and removed where unwanted by a **fin cut** mask: litho plus etch that segments the lines and deletes fins between n and p devices and between cells. The cut's **edge-placement error** (its overlay to the fin layer plus its own line-width error: where an edge actually lands relative to where it should, ~2–3 nm 3σ on EUV) becomes width variation of the end fins, making it one of the most overlay-critical steps in the flow (EUV single exposure at N5+).

### 4.3 Fin etch, STI, and fin reveal

Why these three steps exist: the fin must be carved out of the wafer, its neighbours must be electrically isolated from it through the substrate (otherwise current leaks from one transistor's fin to the next through the bulk silicon), and then a precise height of fin must be exposed above that isolation, because the exposed height is the transistor's width.

**Fin etch.** The fins are etched ~100–130 nm into the bulk (the active fin is only the top ~50 nm) in an **inductively coupled plasma (ICP)** etcher, one in which a coil wound around the chamber drives the plasma so that its density can be set independently of the energy of the ions hitting the wafer (Module 09); the industry calls the chambers tuned for silicon and metal **conductor etchers** (Lam Kiyo or TEL), as opposed to the dielectric etchers used for oxide. The chemistry is hydrogen bromide, chlorine and oxygen (HBr/Cl₂/O₂). The mechanism is a cycle: the halogens etch silicon downward under ion bombardment, while the oxygen and bromine build a thin oxide-like passivation film on the sidewalls that the vertical ions do not remove, so the sidewalls stop etching sideways. Tuning that balance holds the profile to 88–90° and the width to ± 0.5 nm over a ~120 nm depth, an aspect ratio of ~15:1 at a 6–8 nm target width.

**Shallow trench isolation (STI).** The trenches between fins are filled with oxide by **flowable CVD** (Applied's Producer Eterna FCVD, Module 06): a polysilazane-like liquid film that flows into the gaps like a varnish and is then cured by steam anneal and ultraviolet light into SiO₂, because nothing else fills a 20 nm wide, 120 nm deep gap void-free. The oxide is CMP'd flat.

**Fin reveal.** The oxide is then **recessed** by a tightly controlled etch to expose the fin top: dry plasma, vapour HF, or a dry chemical oxide removal such as Applied's SiCoNi or TEL's Certas, in which a low-temperature reaction with ammonia and nitrogen trifluoride converts a set thickness of oxide into a salt that is sublimed away by a mild heat, giving nanometre control and no liquid. The revealed height is the active fin height, so this etch depth (± 1–2 nm) is a direct device parameter. The buried fin below the STI gets a **punch-through stopper (PTS)** implant so current cannot flow under the gate-controlled region.

> **What can go wrong: fins.** *Taper*: a fin 2 nm wider at the base than the top has a bottom that is harder to turn off (thicker body, larger λ), so the device leaks at the base while the top is fine; it is measured by the critical-dimension scanning electron microscope (CD-SEM) and cross-section TEM (both in Section 8.4) and fixed by rebalancing the etch/passivation cycle. *Bending*: after a wet clean, fins that lean together show up as **pitch walking** in top-down SEM (alternating wide and narrow gaps between neighbouring fins, so the pitch "walks" instead of repeating evenly) and as shorted or merged S/D epi later; the fix is a drying step that avoids a liquid surface (Section 6.4). *Reveal depth*: a 2 nm variation in STI recess is a 4 nm variation in effective width (both sidewalls), i.e. ~4% of drive current, and it maps across the wafer with the CMP and etch non-uniformity; optical critical-dimension (OCD) scatterometry, which fits a model to the light diffracted by the fin array (Section 8.4), tracks it on every lot.

> **Why it matters downstream:** fin height, through the effective width, sets each transistor's on-current, and the on-current spread across a wafer becomes the frequency spread of the finished chips. When Module 14 describes wafer sort binning dies into speed grades, a good part of the distribution it is sorting was created here, by ± 1–2 nm of STI recess.

## 5. A Complete FinFET FEOL/MOL Process Flow

The following is a representative ~40-step flow for a 7/5 nm class FinFET logic process. Each line is a "major step"; each contains multiple unit operations (clean, deposition, litho, etch, strip, metrology). "FEOL" is the front end of line (the transistor), "MOL" is the middle of line (the local contacts), and the BEOL, the back end of line (the copper wiring stack, Module 12), follows.

### 5.1 How to read the flow

A legend for the recurring abbreviations, so the lists below can stay compact. **Cz** = Czochralski, the crystal-pulling method of Module 02. **<100>** = the crystal plane at the wafer surface, the orientation with the best oxide interface. **ohm-cm** = the unit of resistivity; ~10 ohm-cm is lightly doped. **p- epi** = a lightly doped p-type epitaxial layer grown on the wafer to give a cleaner, defect-free starting surface. **Pad oxide / pad nitride** = a thin thermal oxide plus a silicon nitride film that protect the fin tops and serve as a polish stop; nitride is hard and polishes slowly. **LPCVD** = low-pressure CVD in a furnace. **Mandrel** = the sacrificial line whose sidewalls carry the SADP spacers. **RTA** = rapid thermal anneal: lamps heat the wafer to ~1,050 °C for about a second. **SiH₄** = silane, **DCS** = dichlorosilane (SiH₂Cl₂), **PH₃** = phosphine, **GeH₄** = germane, the growth and dopant gases for epi. **In-situ doped** = the dopant gas is mixed into the epi growth rather than implanted afterwards. **TEOS** = tetraethyl orthosilicate, a liquid precursor for CVD oxide. **PDA** = post-deposition anneal. **PVD** = physical vapour deposition, also called sputtering: argon ions in a plasma knock atoms off a solid metal target and the atoms land on the wafer as a film (Module 06); unlike ALD it coats line-of-sight surfaces best, which is fine for the bottom of a contact hole. **Poly open** = the CMP that exposes the tops of the dummy gates. **MD / CA** = the trench contact that lands on the source/drain (TSMC / Intel names); **CB / VG** = the contact that lands on the gate. **COAG** = contact over active gate. **ρ_c** = contact resistivity in Ω·cm². **SiCN** = silicon carbonitride, an etch-stop and capping dielectric. **Å** (angstrom) = 0.1 nm, about one atomic layer.

### 5.2 The flow

**Substrate and fins**

1. **Start**: a 300 mm p-type Cz wafer, <100>, ~10 ohm-cm, often with a ~2 µm p- epi layer. The epi layer buries the wafer's grown-in oxygen precipitates and defects below the device layer.
2. **Pad oxide** (thermal, ~5 nm) + **pad nitride** (LPCVD SiN, ~30–40 nm) + fin hardmask stack. The nitride will stop the STI polish; the oxide cushions the stress between nitride and silicon.
3. **Fin patterning**: mandrel litho (193i or EUV) → SADP/SAQP spacers → fin pitch 26–34 nm. The spacers, not the scanner, define the final pitch (Module 07).
4. **Fin cut** litho + etch: removes unwanted fins between cells and at the n-p boundary, so that each device gets exactly its integer fin count.
5. **Fin etch**: Cl₂/HBr/O₂ plasma, ~120 nm deep, sidewall angle ~88–90°, width ~6–8 nm (Section 4.3).
6. **STI fill**: flowable CVD oxide, steam/UV cure, densification anneal. Liquid-like film fills the 20 nm gaps; the cure turns it into glass.
7. **STI CMP** to the pad nitride; then pad nitride strip in hot phosphoric acid, which dissolves nitride but not oxide.
8. **STI recess (fin reveal)**: controlled oxide etch exposes ~50 nm of fin, the active height.
9. **Well and punch-through-stopper implants**, through masks. A **well** is a region of the wafer counter-doped from the substrate so that a PMOS, which needs an n-type body, can sit on the p-type wafer next to an NMOS: an **n-well** (phosphorus or arsenic) is implanted under each PMOS and a **p-well** (boron) under each NMOS, so the wells set the body type under each device; the PTS blocks leakage under the fin.
10. **Well anneal / dopant activation**: spike RTA ~1,050 °C. Every dopant so far takes its lattice site; nothing heat-sensitive exists on the wafer yet.

**Dummy gate**

11. **Dummy gate oxide** (thermal or ALD SiO₂, ~2 nm) over the fins: the etch-stop the poly removal will land on.
12. **Dummy poly**: amorphous Si ~90 nm (LPCVD, SiH₄, ~550 °C) + poly CMP for planarity over the fins, because the gate lines must be printed on a flat surface.
13. **Gate hardmask** (SiN/SiO₂) and gate litho (SADP at 54–57 nm pitch; EUV single exposure at N5+).
14. **Gate etch**: HBr/Cl₂/O₂, stops on the dummy oxide, vertical profile over the ~100 nm fin topography. The line width printed here is L_g.
15. **Gate cut** litho + etch: cuts the long gate line into per-device segments.

**Spacers and source/drain**

16. **Spacer 1**: ALD low-k SiOCN or SiBCN (~5–8 nm), anisotropic etch-back; the fin sidewalls in the S/D regions are cleared so epi can grow there.
17. **NMOS S/D region**: PMOS masked; fin recess etch (~40–50 nm), pre-clean. The fin is cut down where the source and drain will be regrown.
18. **NMOS S/D epi**: Si:P (P ~2–3 × 10²¹ cm⁻³, in-situ doped, ~650–700 °C, SiH₄/DCS + PH₃ + hydrogen chloride, HCl, for selectivity). The HCl continuously etches away any silicon nuclei that try to form on oxide or nitride.
19. **PMOS S/D region**: NMOS masked; fin recess etch, pre-clean (dilute HF + SiCoNi).
20. **PMOS S/D epi**: SiGe:B graded 30 → 55–65% Ge, B ~1 × 10²¹; diamond-shaped merged or unmerged epi (the diamond is the crystal's natural facet shape).
21. **Spacer 2 / epi encapsulation** (thin SiN), then S/D activation (laser or flash anneal, milliseconds, ~1,100–1,200 °C): the last high-temperature step.
22. **CESL**: ALD/PECVD SiN ~3–5 nm, which is also the stress liner.
23. **ILD0**: flowable oxide, cure, TEOS cap; ILD0 CMP stopping on the dummy gate tops (poly open).

**Replacement metal gate**

24. **Dummy poly removal** (NH₄OH / TMAH wet, or a sulfur hexafluoride, SF₆, dry etch), dummy oxide strip (dilute HF): the trench is now empty down to the fin.
25. **Interfacial layer** growth (~0.6 nm chemical oxide) + HfO₂ ALD (~1.8 nm) + PDA anneal.
26. **WFM stack**: TiN cap; PMOS TiN/TaN (thick); litho + wet strip off NMOS; NMOS TiAlC + TiN. Repeated with dipole layers and thickness variants for each V_T flavour: 4–6 masks total.
27. **Gate fill**: W (WF₆/B₂H₆ nucleation + bulk) or Co / fluorine-free W on TiN, void-free.
28. **Metal gate CMP** to the ILD0 surface.
29. **Gate recess** etch (~25 nm) + SAC cap: SiN fill + CMP.

**Middle of line: contacts and local interconnect**

30. **Trench contact (MD / CA)** litho (EUV) + etch through ILD0, self-aligned to the SAC cap and spacers: the hole to the source/drain.
31. **Contact pre-clean**, Ti PVD/ALD + anneal → TiSi_x silicide on Si:P and SiGe:B (ρ_c ~1 × 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm²); Section 5.3 explains why.
32. **TiN barrier** (ALD, ~1–2 nm) + Co (or W; Ru at newer nodes) fill, CMP.
33. **Gate contact (CB / VG)** litho + etch, landing on the metal gate through the SAC cap (contact-over-active-gate, COAG, at Intel 10 nm+ lets it sit over the fin rather than at the cell edge).
34. **Gate contact fill** (W/Co), CMP.
35. **M0 / local interconnect** (the first, tightest metal level plus the **vias**, the short vertical metal plugs that connect one wiring layer to the one above or below it, which here link the trench and gate contacts up to M0): ILD deposition, EUV litho, etch, metal fill (Cu with TaN barrier, or Co / Ru for tight pitch), CMP.
36. **Cap / etch stop** (SiCN ~10 nm) → hand-off to the BEOL (Module 12): M1, V1, M2 ... M15+.

### 5.3 Middle of line: contacts, and why they now dominate

The problem: at a 45–50 nm CPP the source/drain contact is a hole ~15 nm wide, and its resistance now rivals the transistor's own. Metal touching silicon does not conduct freely. At the boundary the electrons face an energy step, the **Schottky barrier**, several tenths of an eV high, which they cannot climb at room temperature. The cure is doping: at concentrations above ~10²¹ cm⁻³ the barrier becomes so thin (a nanometre or so) that electrons tunnel straight through it, the same quantum effect that leaks through a thin gate oxide, now put to use. That is why the S/D epi is doped to 2–3 × 10²¹ (Si:P) and 1 × 10²¹ (SiGe:B), well beyond equilibrium solubility. The second ingredient is the **silicide**: a thin titanium film is deposited on the epi (PVD or ALD, ~3–5 nm) and annealed so that it reacts with the silicon to form titanium silicide, TiSi_x, an intimate, oxide-free metal-semiconductor interface with a lower barrier than bare metal. The quality of that interface is quoted as the **contact resistivity ρ_c**, in Ω·cm²: divide by the contact area and you get the ohms of one contact.

> **Worked example: the ohms in one contact.** A contact of 15 × 30 nm has an area of 450 nm² = 4.5 × 10⁻¹² cm². At ρ_c = 1 × 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm², R = ρ_c / A = 10⁻⁹ / 4.5 × 10⁻¹² ≈ 220 Ω. At 10⁻⁸ Ω·cm², the planar-era value, it would be 2,200 Ω. Compare with the transistor itself: a single-fin device delivering ~0.13 mA at 0.7 V has an effective on-resistance of V/I ≈ 5 kΩ, and its linear-region channel resistance (the resistance the channel presents at small V_DS, Section 1.1) is a few kΩ (~). So at 10⁻⁹ the two contacts (source and drain, ~440 Ω together) are ~10% of the device resistance; at 10⁻⁸ they would be ~4.4 kΩ, comparable to the channel and halving the drive current. That is why the 10× improvement in ρ_c between the planar era and N5 was worth on the order of 10% or more of drive current (~), and why every further 2× is chased.

The plug metal is the third ingredient. For decades it was CVD tungsten, but tungsten needs a thick fluorine-barrier liner and grows inward from every wall leaving a seam; in a 15 nm hole the liner takes most of the volume. Cobalt (CVD from a cobalt carbonyl precursor, reflowed by a short anneal so that it flows into the hole) replaced it at the 7 nm class in many flows because it fills seam-free with a thinner liner; ruthenium and molybdenum are next (Module 12). The tools: Applied Endura PVD/ALD chambers for the titanium and TiN, Lam Altus for tungsten, Applied Volta for cobalt, ASM and Applied for the ALD liners.

### 5.4 What goes wrong in the FinFET flow

The failure modes that belong to a single unit step have been covered where their physics lives: fin taper, bending and STI-recess depth in Section 4.3; work-function-metal thickness (~10–20 mV of V_T per 0.1 nm), fill voids and interfacial-layer regrowth in the replacement-gate box of Section 3.3; and contact resistance (an incomplete silicide, a single atomic layer of residual oxide under the titanium, or a dopant-poor epi surface pushing a ~220 Ω contact to several times that, caught by Kelvin contact-resistance structures and contact chains in the scribe line, Section 8.4, and by e-beam voltage-contrast inspection, where an open contact charges up and looks bright) in Section 5.3. What is left, and what this section is for, are the failures that come from the sequence itself: one step's output being the next step's starting surface.

> **What can go wrong: epi defects.** Selective epi must nucleate only on exposed silicon (HCl in the gas mix etches nuclei off dielectrics); facets, stacking faults (a misplaced atomic plane in the crystal), and voids between merged fins become resistance variation and yield loss. Detection: optical and e-beam inspection for nodules on the dielectric, TEM for stacking faults, and S/D resistance test structures.

> **What can go wrong: poly CMP and poly-open CMP over fin topography (steps 12 and 23).** The dummy poly is deposited over fins that stand ~50 nm proud of the STI, and its surface must be flat before the gate litho, so it is polished (step 12); later the ILD0 is polished down until every dummy-gate top is exposed (step 23). Both polishes remove material at a rate that depends on what is underneath: oxide polishes faster than poly, and a region packed with gates (an SRAM array) polishes differently from a sparse logic region or an open area. The result is **dishing** (a wide soft region polished into a shallow bowl) and **erosion** (a dense array polished lower than its surroundings), so the gate height after the poly open varies by ~5–10 nm (~) across a die. That is not cosmetic: the gate height is the depth of the trench the replacement gate is built in, so it changes the metal-gate resistance, the SAC recess margin of step 29, and the WFM coverage of step 26; in the worst case a low region leaves gate tops buried (the killer defect of Section 3.3) while a high region polishes through the gate hardmask. Detection: the in-line film-thickness maps that the CMP tool's end-point system produces, e-beam inspection for unopened gates, and ring-oscillator frequency maps (Section 8.4) that reproduce the polish pattern. Fix: **dummy fill**, non-functional gate and fin patterns inserted into open areas so that every region of the die presents the same pattern density to the pad, plus end-point detection that stops the polish on the poly signal rather than on a timer.

> **What can go wrong: gate-cut placement (step 15).** Every gate line runs across both the NMOS and the PMOS strip of a cell (Section 2.1) and on into the next row, and it must be cut wherever two devices need separate gates: between the n and p device of some cells and always at the row boundary. The cut has to land in the n-to-p gap, ~40–50 nm wide, and the gate has to extend past each fin by a **gate end-cap** of ~10–20 nm (~) so that the cut's edge-placement error (~2–3 nm 3σ, Section 4.2) can never clip a fin. Clip one and that transistor's gate no longer covers its full fin: less width, and a leaky, poorly gated fin corner. Miss on the other side and a sliver of poly survives at the foot of the cut, a **stringer**, which after the replacement gate becomes a metal bridge that shorts two gates that were supposed to switch independently. Over-etch the cut and it punches through the dummy oxide into the STI, leaving a hole the gate metal later fills. Detection: e-beam voltage-contrast inspection (a shorted gate pair charges differently from its neighbours) and gate-to-gate leakage structures in the scribe line. The industry's answer on the newest nodes is the **self-aligned gate cut**, made after the metal gate through a hardmask that is aligned to the fins themselves, so the end-cap can shrink toward zero; the forksheet wall of Section 7.1 is the endpoint of that idea.

> **What can go wrong: punch-through-stopper implant shadowing (step 9).** The PTS implant arrives after fin reveal, when ~50 nm tall fins stand 26–34 nm apart, and ions are normally implanted at a tilt of several degrees to avoid channelling straight down the crystal's open directions. At a tilt, each fin shadows the base of its neighbour, so the dose reaching the fin bottom and the STI floor depends on the tilt angle, the fin pitch, and the wafer's rotation, and a fin at the edge of an array (next to a fin cut) sees a different dose from one in the middle. Under-dose the base and current punches through beneath the gate-controlled region, an I_off tail on the affected fins; over-dose it and boron diffuses up into the bottom of the fin during the well anneal of step 10, raising V_T there and re-introducing the random dopant fluctuation the undoped fin was supposed to have eliminated (Section 4.1). Detection: scribe-line I_off arrays that compare edge fins with interior fins, and dopant-versus-depth profiles on test structures by **secondary-ion mass spectrometry (SIMS)**, which sputters the sample away layer by layer and weighs the ions that come off. Fix: implant straight down with the wafer rotated in four quadrant steps, or implant before the STI recess while the oxide still surrounds the fins and there is nothing to shadow; the nanosheet flow removes the problem with a bottom dielectric isolation layer instead of an implant (Section 6.4).

## 6. Gate-All-Around Nanosheets

### 6.1 Why nanosheets

A **nanosheet** (Samsung: **MBCFET**, multi-bridge-channel FET; Intel: **RibbonFET**; imec/IBM: nanosheet; TSMC: nanosheet) is a FinFET turned on its side again and sliced: instead of one tall vertical fin, the channel is a vertical stack of three or four thin horizontal silicon ribbons, each ~5–7 nm thick and ~15–50 nm wide, spaced ~10–13 nm apart, with the gate filling the gaps so that each sheet is gated on all four sides. Three advantages:

1. **Electrostatics.** Four-sided gating gives the smallest λ for a given body thickness. In research-class comparisons at L_g ≈ 12–14 nm, SS improves to ~65–68 mV/dec and DIBL to ~30 mV/V, where a FinFET at the same gate length would be at 70+ mV/dec and 50+ mV/V (~; production values depend on the exact geometry and are rarely published side by side).
2. **Continuously tunable width.** Sheet width is set by litho, not by an integer fin count. A designer can use narrow (~15–20 nm) sheets for low-power cells and wide (~40–50 nm) sheets for drive-critical paths within the same cell height, which is what TSMC calls **NanoFlex** at N2 and Samsung exploits in MBCFET.
3. **More current per footprint at lower capacitance.** At equal footprint a three-sheet stack delivers ~10–20% more drive than two fins, with less **parasitic** gate capacitance (capacitance that must be charged at every switch but does no useful work) because the gate no longer wraps around the empty fin-to-fin gaps.

> **Worked example: sheet perimeter versus fins.** Effective width is the gated perimeter. A 30 nm wide, 6 nm thick sheet has a perimeter of 2 × (30 + 6) = 72 nm; three of them give 216 nm. Two fins of 106 nm each (Section 4.1) give 212 nm, in about the same ~50 nm footprint. So a three-sheet stack of narrow sheets already matches two fins, and it can be widened without touching the footprint's pitch: 50 nm sheets give 3 × 2 × 56 = 336 nm, ~1.6× two fins. Meanwhile the gate no longer has to fill the gap between the two fins, so the capacitance per unit of effective width falls. This is the arithmetic behind "more drive per footprint at lower capacitance".

The price is a substantially harder process, dominated by three new steps: the superlattice epi, the inner spacer, and the channel release.

### 6.2 The Si/SiGe superlattice

> **Intuition: SiGe as a lost-wax scaffold.** In lost-wax casting you carve the shape in wax, build a mould around it, melt the wax out and pour metal into the void. Silicon-germanium is the wax: the one material that (a) grows as a perfect single crystal on silicon, (b) is a good enough template for perfect silicon to grow back on top of it, and (c) dissolves in chemistries that leave silicon essentially untouched (better than 100:1). So you grow a sandwich of alternating SiGe and Si, carve it into fins, and at the two moments when you need a void (the inner spacer and the gate) you dissolve the SiGe and leave the silicon floating. The whole nanosheet process is "build the sandwich, then remove the filling", and every hard step is a place where the filling must come out without disturbing the bread.

The starting material is no longer a bare wafer. On top of the bulk silicon (or over a bottom isolation layer) a **Si/SiGe superlattice** is grown by epitaxy (Module 06; **reduced-pressure CVD (RPCVD)** in an ASM Intrepid or Applied Centura at ~600–700 °C from silane or dichlorosilane and germane, GeH₄): alternating Si_{0.7}Ge_{0.3} (25–35% Ge, ~8–12 nm, the future gate gaps) and pure Si (~5–7 nm, the future channels), three or four pairs plus a Si cap, ~50–70 nm total. The requirements: every interface must be abrupt to a **monolayer** (one atomic layer, 0.14 nm); the Ge fraction must be uniform to ± 1% across the wafer, because it sets the etch selectivity later; the SiGe must be fully strained with no **misfit dislocations**; and the Si layers must be atomically smooth, because their thickness is the channel thickness and each 0.14 nm atomic step is a V_T variation source. The dislocation constraint deserves a sentence: a crystal with a bigger lattice can be forced to fit onto silicon only up to a **critical thickness**, ~10–20 nm at 30% Ge (~); beyond that it relieves the strain by slipping, creating line defects, the misfit dislocations, that thread up through the stack into the channels and kill the device. That is why Ge is capped at ~35% at these layer thicknesses. The superlattice is then patterned into fins exactly as before (SAQP or EUV, etch through the stack into the substrate, STI fill and recess): early in the flow a nanosheet fin is a FinFET fin with stripes in it.

### 6.3 Inner spacers, the hardest step

In a FinFET the gate spacer sits on top of the fin and separates gate from S/D. In a nanosheet the gate will later occupy the SiGe gaps between sheets, and those gaps run all the way from the source to the drain. Without something in the way, the replacement gate metal would touch the S/D epi. The **inner spacer** solves this.

> **Intuition: planks, cardboard and putty.** Picture three wooden planks (the Si sheets) with cardboard (the SiGe) glued between them, the stack cut square at both ends. You want to plug the ends of every cardboard layer so that later, when you dissolve the cardboard from the middle and pour metal into the slots, the metal cannot reach the end grain (the source and drain). So you dissolve the cardboard inward from the ends by a few millimetres, leaving a shallow slot under each plank edge; press putty into every slot; scrape the putty off every surface except inside the slots. The putty plugs are the inner spacers, done at the nanometre scale, with slots 5–8 nm deep that must be the same depth for the top plank (easy to reach) and the bottom one (fed through a narrower gap).

1. After the dummy gate and outer spacer are formed and the S/D regions are recessed (etching through the entire superlattice to expose the stack edge), the SiGe layers are **laterally recessed** ~5–8 nm from the edge by a highly selective isotropic etch (vapour-phase HCl at ~500–650 °C, or a dry radical etch; selectivity SiGe:Si > 50:1 is required). The Si sheets stick out as tiny cantilevers.
2. A **conformal** dielectric (one that goes down at the same thickness on every surface it touches, flat, vertical or inside a cavity, which is what ALD delivers; ALD SiN, SiOCN, or silicon oxycarbide, SiOC; all low-k) is deposited to fill the lateral cavities, ~5–8 nm thick to pinch off the cavity entrances.
3. An isotropic **etch-back** removes the dielectric from all exposed surfaces but leaves it inside the cavities (because there the film is "protected" by geometry). The remaining plugs are the inner spacers.

The tolerances are brutal. The inner spacer width sets each sheet's gate-to-S/D separation and hence its effective gate length, and it must be identical for the top, middle, and bottom sheets even though the etchant reaches them differently. That difference is **loading**: the local etch rate depends on how much material is nearby and how open the access path is, and the bottom cavity is fed through a narrower gap and etches more slowly. A 1 nm difference is a 1 nm L_g mismatch between sheets of the same transistor. The recess must not thin the Si sheets (a V_T shift) and the etch-back must leave the sheet edges residue-free, since they seed the S/D epi. Every foundry treats its inner-spacer recipe as one of its most protected secrets, and imec, Applied, and Lam all cite the step as the largest yield-learning item in the nanosheet transition.

> **What can go wrong: inner spacers.** *Recess non-uniformity*: the bottom cavity ends up 1–2 nm shallower than the top, so the three sheets of one transistor have three different gate lengths; it shows up as excess DIBL and SS scatter on scribe-line device arrays, and cross-section TEM through the stack confirms it. *Residue on the sheet ends*: a film of spacer dielectric left on the exposed silicon where the S/D epi must nucleate gives faceted, voided or missing epi on one sheet; caught as S/D resistance outliers and by TEM. *Over-recess*: an etch that attacks the silicon as well as the SiGe thins the sheet ends, raising V_T there; detected as a V_T tail in the array data and by TEM thickness mapping. The fixes are recipe-level (temperature, pressure, cycle timing of the selective etch) and are the closest-held secrets in the flow.

### 6.4 Source/drain epi and channel release

The S/D epi now grows from the exposed sidewalls of the three or four Si sheets (and from the substrate at the recess bottom), and the separate growth fronts must merge into one void-free crystal contacting all sheets in parallel, a more delicate recipe than in a FinFET. Why it is delicate deserves the mechanism. After the inner spacers are in place, the S/D cavity is a box ~50–70 nm deep whose back wall is striped: the end faces of three or four Si sheets, each ~6 nm tall and as wide as the sheet, separated by the inner-spacer plugs, with the silicon substrate as the floor. Epitaxy needs a crystal to copy, so the only places it can start are those sheet ends and the floor; everything else in the cavity is dielectric, and the HCl in the gas mix (Section 5.2, step 18) keeps it bare. Each seed therefore grows its own crystal outward, and a crystal grows at different rates on different facets (the {111} planes, the ones that give FinFET epi its diamond shape, are the slow ones), so four or five separate faceted mounds advance into the cavity at once: the sheet fronts grow sideways toward the opposite wall and up and down toward each other, the substrate front grows upward. All of them descend from the same lattice, so where two fronts meet they should knit into one continuous crystal; whether they do depends on their arriving clean and at about the same time. **Merged** epi means a single void-free block that touches every sheet over its full end face; **unmerged** means some sheet is reached only by a sliver of its own growth, or by a front that arrived late with a seam of defects between them. The largest seed is the floor, so an unmanaged recipe fills the cavity from below and buries the upper sheet ends under a crystal that did not grow from them, and the seam between the two is a resistor exactly where the current has to enter the sheet. Production recipes therefore grow in two stages: a thin, lightly doped **seed layer** at lower temperature that wets every sheet end and the floor and merges the fronts into one lattice, then the heavily doped bulk (Si:P at 2–3 × 10²¹, SiGe:B at ~10²¹) grown faster on top of it; where the flow has bottom dielectric isolation (next paragraph) the floor is oxide, the substrate front disappears and the merge is sideways only (~; the recipes are proprietary). What goes wrong: a sheet end still coated with a film of inner-spacer dielectric or oxide does not seed at all, and that sheet is contacted only through a sideways seam from its neighbours' epi, showing up as a minority of transistors with a few per cent less drive and worse DIBL (~); a void trapped where two fronts meet, harmless until it sits on the path to one sheet; a facet that leaves a triangular gap between the epi and the inner spacer so the epi touches the sheet over less than its full end face, raising that sheet's series resistance; and phosphorus or boron incorporating differently on different facets, so the junction position, and hence the effective gate length, differs sheet by sheet. Detection is by TEM cross-section along the channel direction through the S/D, the only view that shows all three sheet-to-epi junctions, and by S/D resistance and DIBL scatter in the scribe-line arrays. That is why an epi step that was routine in the FinFET flow is listed with the inner spacer and the release as one of the three delicate steps of the nanosheet flow.

A **bottom dielectric isolation** (BDI) or a heavily doped punch-through stopper is needed under the bottom sheet, otherwise a parasitic planar transistor forms in the substrate, gated by the same gate, adding leakage without useful current. BDI is built by growing a higher-Ge SiGe layer at the very bottom of the superlattice, removing it selectively during the S/D recess (its higher Ge content makes it etch first), and refilling the slot with oxide. Nothing falls down while this happens: the stack stays anchored by the dummy gate and outer spacer clamped over it from above, the bottom layer is dissolved inward from both S/D openings exactly as the inner-spacer cavities are, and ALD oxide, which coats every surface it can reach however deep, fills the resulting slot under the stack from both ends.

After ILD0 deposition and the **poly-open CMP** (the polish that exposes the tops of the dummy gates), the dummy gate is removed as in a FinFET. Then comes the **channel release**: the SiGe between the sheets is etched away, leaving the Si sheets suspended in mid-air, anchored only at their ends by the inner spacers and S/D epi. The etch is isotropic, must reach the full gate footprint from both sides, and needs > 100:1 SiGe:Si selectivity: vapour HCl, remote-plasma or thermal fluorine chemistry (carbon tetrafluoride with oxygen, CF₄/O₂, or chlorine trifluoride, ClF₃, as in TEL's Certas), or wet HF/H₂O₂/acetic acid. Two failure modes dominate: **stiction** (sheets pulled together by capillary forces during wet steps) and **sheet thinning/rounding** (~1 nm of corner rounding is fine and even helps field uniformity; thickness loss is a V_T shift). The answer to stiction is to avoid a liquid surface altogether: dry etch chemistry, or **supercritical CO₂** drying, in which the rinse liquid is exchanged for carbon dioxide above its critical point (~31 °C, ~74 bar), where there is no liquid-gas boundary and therefore no surface tension to pull the sheets together. A short trim and clean follow, and then the gate stack goes in.

### 6.5 The wrap-around gate and the 10 nm gap problem

The released structure has ~10–13 nm gaps between sheets. Into that gap must go, from each sheet surface: interfacial oxide (~0.6 nm), HfO₂ (~1.5–1.8 nm), TiN cap (~1 nm), and the work-function metal. Counting both sheet surfaces facing the gap, the dielectric stack alone consumes ~6–7 nm, leaving ~4–6 nm for the WFM and fill from both sides, i.e. ~2–3 nm per surface. That is thinner than the TiAl/TiN thickness FinFET flows used to tune V_T.

> **Worked example: the gap budget.** Take an 11 nm gap. Each sheet surface facing it needs 0.6 nm of interfacial oxide + 1.7 nm of HfO₂ + 1.0 nm of TiN cap = 3.3 nm, and there are two surfaces, so 11 − 2 × 3.3 = 4.4 nm remains. Shared between the two surfaces that is 2.2 nm of work-function metal per surface. The FinFET flow tuned V_T with 2–4 nm of TiAl plus a variable TiN thickness (Section 3.3, step 9); there is no room for that here, and certainly no room for a second, thicker PMOS stack. Widen the gap to 13 nm and you gain 1 nm per surface but make the stack taller and every etch through it harder.

Consequences:

- V_T tuning shifts almost entirely to **dipole engineering** (La-based dipoles for NMOS, Al-based or fluorine treatments for PMOS) and to WFM composition rather than thickness; TSMC's N2 papers highlight multi-V_T by dipole for exactly this reason.
- The inter-sheet gap is completely filled by WFM; the W/Mo fill sits only above and beside the stack.
- The wet strip of PMOS metal off the NMOS trenches must clear metal from inside the gaps without undercutting the n/p boundary, only ~40–50 nm away.

Sheet spacing is thus a trade-off: wider spacing eases the gate fill but makes the stack taller and every etch harder. Published cross-sections show ~10–12 nm.

> **Why it matters downstream:** the dipole-based V_T menu of a nanosheet PDK is what a GPU design team's tools have to work with when they close timing (Module 19): each flavour is a fixed point on the leakage-versus-delay curve, and the spacing between flavours (~50–100 mV) sets how finely they can trade the two. If a foundry can only offer four flavours because the gap will not hold more options, that is a constraint on the chip's power budget that was decided by 2.2 nm of metal.

### 6.6 Who shipped what

- **Samsung 3GAE (SF3E), June 2022**: first GAA in production, three sheets, initially for crypto-mining **ASICs** (application-specific integrated circuits, chips designed for one customer's single job); the second generation SF3 (3GAP) in 2024 with a claimed 35% area reduction (~1.5× density) vs 5LPE and improved sheets; SF2 in 2025–2026 (as of 2025).
- **TSMC N2, Q4 2025** (as of 2025): three sheets, NanoFlex libraries, 1.15× density over N3E, and, in TSMC's IEDM 2024 figures as reported, 24–35% lower power at the same speed or ~15% higher speed at the same power versus N3E (TSMC's earlier symposium figure for speed was 10–15%; the power range spans its two libraries). N2P follows in 2026, A16 with Super Power Rail in H2 2026.
- **Intel 18A, 2025 (Panther Lake, HVM late 2025, as of 2025)**: RibbonFET (Intel's RibbonFET disclosures have shown four ribbons; Intel has not stated the production count) plus PowerVia; Intel skipped the planned 20A product node in 2024 and moved directly to 18A; 14A with High-NA EUV follows.

### 6.7 The GAA nanosheet flow

The nanosheet flow reuses the FinFET flow above with the following substitutions and insertions (numbering refers to the FinFET list in Section 5.2):

 1b. Si/SiGe superlattice epi: 3–4 x [SiGe(25–35 % Ge, 8–12 nm) / Si(5–7 nm)] + Si cap, RPCVD ~650 C

      (STI recessed to just below the bottom SiGe so the stack stands proud)

17b. Inner spacer dielectric ALD (SiOCN/SiN, 5–8 nm) fills the lateral cavities  <-- inner spacer 2

Two added mask layers (n/p WFM inside gaps, sheet-width variants) and roughly 10–15 extra process steps over a FinFET flow, plus the superlattice epi that increases starting-material cost. TSMC's reported N2 wafer price of ~$30,000 (vs ~$20,000 for N3) reflects this and the additional EUV layers; both figures are trade-press reports, not TSMC disclosures, and should be read as order-of-magnitude (~).

Who makes the tools for the nanosheet-specific steps: the superlattice and S/D epi run on ASM Intrepid ES and Applied Centura Prime Epi reactors; the inner-spacer ALD on ASM, Lam and TEL ALD chambers; the selective SiGe lateral recess and channel release on TEL Certas, Applied Selectra and Lam gas-phase or radical etchers; the fluorine-free W and Mo gate fill on Lam Altus and Applied chambers; the many CMP steps on Applied Reflexion and Ebara polishers. What closes the loop is the metrology of Section 8.4: OCD scatterometry tracks the stack profile on every lot, and TEM cross-sections through individual transistors are the only way to see whether the inner-spacer cavities filled and the sheets survived release at full thickness.

## 7. Forksheet, CFET, and the Roadmap Beyond

### 7.1 Forksheet

This is a one-paragraph side note on a device that is not yet in production. In a nanosheet cell the NMOS and PMOS stacks must be separated by enough space for the WFM patterning and the gate to be etched between them, ~40–50 nm of n-to-p spacing that is pure overhead. imec's **forksheet** (2017 proposal, demonstrated 2021) inserts a thin dielectric wall (~10–15 nm of SiN) between the n and p stacks before the gate is formed; the sheets are grown/etched up against the wall like the tines of a fork. The wall lets the n-to-p spacing shrink to the wall thickness, buying ~20% cell-height reduction (a 5T → 4T cell), and it also serves as a hard stop for the WFM wet etch, simplifying the n/p patterning. The sheets are gated on three sides rather than four, a minor electrostatic loss. imec places the forksheet at its A10 node (~2028, as of 2025) as the last cell-height scaling step before CFET; TSMC and Intel have both published forksheet research but neither has announced it as a production node.

### 7.2 CFET

Also a side note as of 2025, though it is where the roadmap ends. The **complementary FET (CFET)** stacks the NMOS on top of the PMOS (or vice versa) in the same footprint, so the cell height collapses from (n stack + p stack + spacing) to a single stack: a 4T or even 3T cell, the last ~1.5–2× density lever that geometry can offer. **Monolithic CFET** grows one very tall superlattice (~150–250 nm) with the pFET sheets, a thick middle dielectric isolation, and the nFET sheets; fins are etched through all of it (15–20:1 aspect ratio), S/D epi is grown separately for the bottom (SiGe:B) and top (Si:P) tiers with the other masked, and contacts must reach past the top device to the bottom one. Intel showed CFET inverters at 60 nm gate pitch (IEDM 2023) and with backside contacts (IEDM 2024); TSMC showed a 48 nm gate pitch CFET inverter at IEDM 2023 and 2024; the IEDM 2025 proceedings likely add to this list. **Sequential CFET** builds the bottom tier normally, bonds a second silicon layer on top (**Smart Cut** style: a hydrogen implant lets a thin film be split off a donor wafer and transferred, Module 03), and builds the top tier below ~500 °C so the bottom survives; simpler per tier, but low-temperature activation and epi and ~10 nm bonding overlay are hard, and it doubles FEOL cost. imec's roadmap places CFET at ~A7 (2030–2032, as of 2025); foundries have committed only to research. The wiring is as hard as the device: with n over p the MOL must contact gate, source, and drain at two levels, and backside power (next section) becomes a prerequisite so that at least power comes from below.

Since CFET is the fourth architecture in this module's title, here is the monolithic flow as demonstrated at imec, Intel and TSMC (research-stage, as of 2025), written as a taller version of Section 6.7:

1. **Tall superlattice epi**: the bottom-tier SiGe/Si pairs (say the pFET sheets), then a thicker, higher-Ge SiGe layer that will become the **middle dielectric isolation (MDI)**, then the top-tier pairs and a cap: ~150–250 nm in total against ~50–70 nm for a single-tier nanosheet, with every interface still abrupt to a monolayer and the whole stack still below the critical thickness for dislocations (Section 6.2).
2. **Fin etch through the full stack** into the substrate: at a sheet width in the ~15 nm class (~) and a depth of ~200–250 nm that is the 15–20:1 aspect ratio quoted above, and the width must hold to ± 0.5 nm from top to bottom because the top-tier and bottom-tier sheet widths are the n and p device widths.
3. **STI, dummy gate and outer spacer** as before, over a fin three to four times taller: the dummy poly must be polished flat over ~250 nm of topography (the 5.4 problem, magnified) and the gate etch must clear the full fin height without leaving stringers at its foot.
4. **S/D recess and inner spacers** through both tiers, with the lateral recess equal for six to eight sheets fed through very different path lengths.
5. **Middle isolation**: the higher-Ge middle layer is dissolved inward from the S/D openings, as BDI is in Section 6.4, and the slot refilled with dielectric, so that the two tiers share a gate but not a source/drain.
6. **Tiered S/D epi**: the bottom-tier sheet ends are exposed and grown (SiGe:B) while the top-tier ends are still covered by a sacrificial dielectric liner; the cavity is then partly refilled with dielectric to bury the bottom epi, the liner is stripped from the top sheet ends, and the top tier is grown (Si:P). Each of those masking steps is a selective etch inside a cavity ~250 nm deep.
7. **Release and gate**: dummy-gate removal, channel release of both tiers at once, then the gate stack. An n-over-p device needs the n-type work-function metal on the top sheets and the p-type on the bottom sheets of the same trench: the first metal is deposited over everything, recessed from the top down to the isolation level, and the second deposited over it, a vertical version of the n/p patterning of Section 3.3, step 8.
8. **Contacts on two levels**: the top-tier S/D is contacted normally; the bottom-tier S/D needs a contact that drops past the top device, ~200 nm deep at ~15 nm wide (> 10:1), or, in the versions Intel showed at IEDM 2024, a backside contact. The inverter's shared output needs a vertical strap joining the top drain to the bottom drain.

> **Worked example: the CFET gate trench.** A FinFET replacement-gate trench is ~80 nm deep and ~20 nm wide (Section 3.3, step 6), about 4:1, and ~5:1 for the fill once the WFM has narrowed it (step 10). A monolithic CFET trench spans the whole stack: ~250 nm of superlattice at the same ~20 nm width is ~12:1 before any liner goes in, and inside it the release must clear SiGe from six to eight gaps stacked on top of one another and the WFM recess must stop within a few nanometres of the middle-isolation level. The same ~250 nm reappears in the bottom contact and in the fin etch, so every vertical step is three to four times harder than its nanosheet equivalent while the electrostatics of each sheet are unchanged. That, not the device physics, is why imec places CFET at ~A7 (2030–2032, as of 2025).

> **What can go wrong: CFET.** *Width mismatch top to bottom*: any taper in a 15–20:1 fin etch makes the bottom tier wider than the top, an n/p drive imbalance built into every cell; caught by TEM and by inverter switching-threshold arrays in the scribe line. *Incomplete middle isolation*: SiGe left in the middle layer leaves a gate-controlled parasitic channel between the tiers, or a leakage path from the top S/D to the bottom; it appears as excess I_off on inverter arrays. *Tiered-epi cross-contamination*: bottom-tier SiGe:B nucleating on an imperfectly covered top-sheet end, or the bottom epi damaged when the liner is stripped; S/D resistance outliers and TEM. *WFM recess depth*: recess the first metal too little and the bottom sheets nearest the isolation get the wrong work function, too much and the top sheets do; a V_T tail on one device type, sheet by sheet. *Deep-contact landing*: the bottom contact must reach ~200 nm down within ~10 nm of overlay of a ~20 nm target without touching the top device on the way; e-beam voltage contrast finds the opens and shorts. For sequential CFET the added items are the ~10 nm wafer-to-wafer bonding overlay and a top-tier activation that must stay below ~500 °C, which rules out the spike anneal and forces laser annealing.

The tools are the ones already named for the nanosheet flow, epi from ASM and Applied, etch from Lam and TEL, ALD from ASM, Lam and TEL, CMP from Applied and Ebara, pushed to depths and aspect ratios beyond their nanosheet recipes, plus EV Group and SUSS MicroTec fusion bonders for the sequential variant; most of the published integration work has been done on imec's 300 mm pilot line with those vendors' chambers.

### 7.3 Backside power delivery

The problem: in a conventional chip both signals and power come down through ~15 metal layers from the top. The power grid consumes ~20% of the routing resources on the lowest layers, and V_DD travels through a dozen vias and thin wires before reaching a transistor, losing ~5–10% of its value on the way. That loss is **IR drop**: current I flowing through resistance R loses I × R volts, and the transistor at the end sees a lower supply than the one the regulator delivers, so it runs slower, or the designer raises the supply and pays in power.

> **Worked example: IR drop on an AI accelerator.** A 1,000 W class accelerator at 0.75 V draws 1,000 / 0.75 ≈ 1,300 A. If the effective resistance of the front-side grid from the solder bumps that connect the die to its package (Module 16) to the transistor is 50 µΩ, the drop is 1,300 A × 50 × 10⁻⁶ Ω ≈ 65 mV, ~9% of the supply (both numbers are illustrative orders of magnitude, ~). Thicker, shorter backside wires with far fewer vias cut that resistance and so the drop; Intel's reported 30% improvement in voltage droop is that effect measured on a real design.

**Backside power delivery (BSPDN)** moves the power network to the back of the wafer, directly under the transistors, and frees the two power-rail tracks in every cell row (Section 2.2).

**Intel PowerVia (Intel 18A, 2025)**, first demonstrated on the "Blue Sky Creek" test chip (Intel 4 process, VLSI 2023):

1. Build the front side normally, but form **nano-TSVs** early. A **through-silicon via (TSV)** is a vertical metal plug through the silicon itself; these are ~100 nm class, roughly 500× smaller than package TSVs, dropping from the M0 power rails through the STI to below the fin bottoms.
2. Bond the finished front side to a **carrier wafer** by oxide-to-oxide **fusion bonding**: two ultra-flat oxide surfaces pressed together bond spontaneously at room temperature and are then annealed to make the bond permanent (Module 17).
3. Flip and **thin the device wafer from the back**: grind ~750 µm of the 775 µm away, then CMP and a selective etch stopping on a buried etch-stop (SiGe layer or the STI oxide) so only a few hundred nanometres of silicon, the fins and STI, remain, uniform to tens of nm across 300 mm.
4. Expose the nano-TSVs from the back and build a coarse **backside metal stack** (2–3 thick Cu layers) with bump pads on the back.

Reported results: 30% lower platform voltage droop (IR drop), > 90% standard-cell utilization (vs ~80%) because power rails no longer compete with signals, and ~6% higher frequency at the same design (engineers write "iso-design": the same circuit, re-implemented with backside power and nothing else changed). Intel 18A ships PowerVia in Panther Lake (as of 2025).

**TSMC A16 Super Power Rail (SPR), H2 2026 (as of 2025)**: rather than nano-TSVs from M0, SPR makes **backside contacts directly to the source/drain epi**, so the path is backside metal → backside contact → S/D and the front side is entirely signal. It is the most area-efficient variant (7–10% density, 8–10% speed or 15–20% power over N2P) and the hardest to build, because the contact must land on a ~20 nm epi region from the other side of a thinned wafer with ~10 nm overlay to the front-side pattern. Samsung has announced backside power for SF2Z (~2027).

The costs are real: carrier bonding, extreme thinning, backside litho aligned to front-side marks, and a double-sided wafer that no existing test or handling infrastructure was designed for. And there is heat.

> **Intuition: where the heat used to go.** In a normal chip the transistors sit on ~700 µm of bulk silicon, which conducts heat well (thermal conductivity ~150 W/m·K, in the range of a metal such as brass), so a hot spot spreads sideways through the substrate on its way to the lid and heat sink. After backside thinning that silicon is gone: the transistors are sandwiched between two stacks of oxide (~1.4 W/m·K, like glass) laced with copper, and the heat must find its way out through the metal. Total power is unchanged, but local hot spots rise because the spreader is missing; some analyses put the penalty at 10–20 °C (~, strongly dependent on the design and on how much copper the backside carries), a serious concern for 1,000 W class AI accelerators.

The tools: fusion bonders from EV Group and SUSS MicroTec; grinders from Disco; backside CMP and the selective silicon etch on Applied and Lam tools; and backside lithography on ASML scanners that align to the front-side marks through the wafer using infrared, to which silicon is transparent.

> **What can go wrong: the backside.** *Thinning non-uniformity*: the target is a few hundred nanometres of silicon, and a variation of tens of nm across 300 mm is the entire budget, which is why the sequence ends on an etch stop rather than on the grinder and is checked by infrared interferometry. *Backside-to-front overlay*: the backside contact must hit a feature tens of nm wide that was patterned years of process steps earlier on the other face, to ~10 nm, through the silicon. *Bow*: a device wafer reduced to a film on a carrier warps under the stress of its own metal stacks, and handling and litho must cope. *Test access*: the pads are now on the back, and wafer probing (Module 14) has to be redesigned around a bonded pair.

> **Why it matters downstream:** a backside-power wafer is delivered to test and packaging upside down. Wafer sort (Module 14) must probe pads on the thinned back, the bump and hybrid-bond interfaces of Modules 16–17 land on the backside metal rather than the top of the BEOL, and thermal design in the package has to assume a glass-like path where there used to be silicon. Every module after this one inherits the flip.

### 7.4 Far-future options, briefly

One paragraph, because none of these is on a foundry's product roadmap. **2D semiconductors** (molybdenum disulfide, MoS₂, and the tungsten sulfide and selenide, WS₂ and WSe₂) form a crystal one molecule thick, 0.65 nm, with no **dangling bonds** (unpaired surface bonds that trap charge), so the scale length would allow L_g < 5 nm; Intel, TSMC, and imec have shown GAA 2D transistors (Intel at IEDM 2024), but contact resistance (~10× too high; **semimetal** antimony or bismuth contacts, materials between a metal and a semiconductor whose contact barrier is tiny, help), 300 mm single-crystal monolayer growth, and p-type mobility are unsolved; realistic timing is 2035+. The **negative-capacitance FET** puts a **ferroelectric** layer (an insulator with a switchable built-in polarization, here hafnium zirconium oxide, HfZrO₂) in the gate stack to amplify the surface potential and in principle beat 60 mV/dec; **hysteresis** (its switching threshold depends on which way it was last switched, so it has no single V_T, and a logic gate needs one) and the slow speed of the polarization switching have kept it out of production after a decade of papers. **Tunnel FETs** reach sub-60 mV/dec by **band-to-band tunnelling** (electrons crossing directly from the valence band to the conduction band) but have too little I_on; **vertical transport FETs** (IBM/Samsung VTFET) run the current vertically so the gate length is set by a film thickness rather than by lithography, trading CPP scaling for new contact problems; and **cryogenic CMOS** exploits the fact that SS scales with T (kT/q shrinks with temperature), which is real for quantum-computer control chips, not for GPUs.

### 8.2 The SRAM scaling stall

The six-transistor (6T) **SRAM** bit cell (two cross-coupled inverters that hold one bit plus two pass transistors that read and write it; not to be confused with the 6-track cell of Section 2.1) is the densest structure on a logic chip and 30–40% of a GPU or CPU die. It scaled with logic until N5: TSMC's high-density cell went 0.027 µm² (N7) → 0.021 µm² (N5) → 0.0199 µm² (N3B) → **0.021 µm² again on N3E** (relaxed for yield and cost). TSMC's [published 2025 N2 SRAM demonstration](https://research.tsmc.com/page/memory/4.html) uses a 0.021 µm² high-density bit cell and achieves 38.1 Mb/mm² at macro level, a reported 1.1× improvement over the previous node. This is a specific cell and macro design, not a universal area for every N2 memory option. Other node comparisons above are reported cell options and must be matched for density, speed and operating voltage. Intel's reported 18A HD option is 0.021 µm²; its macro density is a separate metric. To see why it stalled you need to know what the six transistors do.

**What the six transistors do.** Two inverters, each a PMOS **pull-up (PU)** and an NMOS **pull-down (PD)**, are cross-coupled: the output of each is the input of the other, so the pair has exactly two stable states (one internal node at V_DD, the other at 0) and holds either one for as long as the power is on, with no refresh; that is what "static" means. Two NMOS **pass gates (PG)** connect the two internal nodes to a pair of vertical wires, the **bit lines** (BL and its complement BLB), and their gates are driven by a horizontal **word line (WL)** shared by every cell in the row. Hold: WL low, pass gates off, the loop keeps itself. Read: both bit lines are pre-charged to V_DD, then WL goes high; the node storing 0 pulls its bit line down through its PG and PD in series while the other bit line stays high, and a **sense amplifier** at the foot of the column detects a difference of ~50–100 mV (~) between BL and BLB and amplifies it to a full logic level. Write: the bit line on the side to be forced to 0 is driven to ground with WL high; the PG on that side must overpower the PU holding the internal node at V_DD and drag it below the other inverter's switching point, after which the loop flips and finishes the job itself.

**Why V_T mismatch flips a bit.** During a read the internal 0 node does not stay at 0: its PG and PD form a voltage divider between the bit line at V_DD and ground, and the node rises to a fraction of V_DD set by their relative strengths. If that bump reaches the **trip point** of the other inverter (the input voltage at which its output switches, fixed by the V_T and strengths of its PU and PD), the cell overwrites itself: a **read disturb**. Read stability therefore wants PD stronger than PG (the **beta ratio**, PD strength over PG strength, was ~1.5–2 in planar cells (~)); writability wants PG stronger than PU; and every trip point moves with the V_T of the transistors that set it. A random V_T offset of a few tens of millivolts between the two halves of one cell pushes one trip point toward the read bump and the other away from it, and the margin is gone. Mismatch between two nominally identical transistors grows as their gate area shrinks (**Pelgrom's rule**: the spread of ΔV_T scales as 1/√(W × L)), and a single-fin, ~16 nm gate device has a V_T spread of a few tens of millivolts (~) from line-edge roughness and the grain-to-grain variation of its work-function metal. A 64 MB cache holds ~5 × 10⁸ cells, and for the cache to work essentially every one of them must, so the cell has to hold at the ~6σ tail of that distribution, one cell in a billion. That is why cells at N5 and beyond carry **assist circuits**, word-line under-drive or a briefly lowered cell V_DD for reads and a negative bit-line pulse for writes, spending area and energy to buy the margin the transistors no longer supply.

**The 2-CPP layout.** In the standard "thin cell" layout the cell is two gate pitches tall and several fin pitches wide. Two gate lines cross it, each cut into two pieces: one piece is the shared gate of one inverter's PU and PD (the two transistors of an inverter share a gate line exactly as in a standard cell, Section 2.1), the other piece is the pass gate of the opposite side, driven by the word line. Four active strips run across the two gate lines in the order NMOS, PMOS, PMOS, NMOS: each outer NMOS strip carries a PD and a PG in series, each inner PMOS strip a PU. The bit lines run vertically over the NMOS strips, V_DD over the PMOS strips, and the word line horizontally along the gate direction. At N5 that is 2 × 51 nm ≈ 0.10 µm tall by ~0.2 µm wide (0.021 µm² ÷ 0.102 µm), the width being four active strips plus two n-to-p spacings.

**Why it stopped scaling.** Three reasons, each visible in that layout. First, the HD cell already uses one fin (or one narrow sheet) for PU, PG and PD alike, a 1-1-1 configuration; there is no depopulation left to harvest, and with equal fin counts the beta ratio is 1, so the cell already leans on assists. Second, Pelgrom's rule: every shrink of the transistor raises the V_T spread, which the 6σ requirement turns directly into read and write failures; TSMC's step back from 0.0199 µm² (N3B) to 0.021 µm² (N3E) is this effect priced in yield. Third, the geometry has nothing to give: the cell height is 2 CPP and CPP is stuck near 45 nm (Section 2.2); the width is four active strips and two n-to-p spacings set by gate-cut and contact margins that scale slowly; and none of the DTCO tricks that carry logic (Section 8.3) apply, because an SRAM cell has no routing tracks to remove, no cell-to-cell isolation gate to shorten (the diffusion break of Section 8.3), and contacts that already sit as close as they can.

> **Worked example: what a cache costs in silicon.** A decimal 50 MB cache holds 400 Mb. At the published 38.1 Mb/mm² macro density it occupies 400/38.1 ≈ 10.5 mm² before system integration overhead. The physical 0.021 µm² cell alone would allow 47.6 Mb/mm², so the macro reaches about 80% of that ideal cell-only density. Decoders, sensing and other support circuits occupy the rest. A 1.1× macro-density improvement means about 9.1% less area for the same bits, not 10% smaller cells. Never infer a physical bit-cell area by inverting macro density, or assume a logic-density gain applies equally to SRAM.

Cache is therefore expensive silicon, which pushes AMD (**3D V-Cache**, a separate SRAM die bonded directly on top of the CPU die, Module 17) and others toward stacking SRAM made on an older node, and it is a big part of why "2× density per node" no longer means 2× chip capability.

### 8.3 DTCO, STCO, and V_T flavours

The problem: since ~10 nm the pitches barely move (CPP 51 → 48 → ~45 nm and MP 30 → 23 → ~23–25 nm across N5, N3E and N2, Section 2.2), yet each node still has to deliver 1.15–1.3× more transistors per square millimetre. The only place left to find that is inside the cell: change what a standard cell looks like, so that fewer pitches are spent on things that are not transistors. That cannot be done by the process team or the library team alone, because every layout trick needs a process feature to make it legal and every process feature is worthless unless the library uses it. Designing the two together is **design-technology co-optimization (DTCO)**, and every node since ~10 nm has been defined by it. To see what the tricks buy you need the picture of a cell boundary.

**The picture.** Section 2.1 gave the cell: a PMOS strip above an NMOS strip, vertical gate lines at CPP, horizontal metal tracks. Along a row of cells the strips of silicon that carry the sources and drains, the fins in a FinFET or the sheet stacks in a nanosheet cell, run continuously from cell to cell, and the gate lines march across them at a fixed pitch whether or not a transistor is wanted at that position. Engineers call the S/D active strip the **diffusion**, a name from the era when source and drain were made by diffusing dopant into the wafer from a hot gas; the fin cut of Section 4.2 is, in this vocabulary, a diffusion cut. Two neighbouring cells sharing one continuous diffusion would be electrically joined: the drain of the last transistor in one cell would be the source of the first transistor in the next. Something has to break the diffusion at every cell boundary, and how much room the break takes is the first trick.

**Diffusion breaks.** The **double diffusion break (DDB)** is the old way: the fin is cut at the fin-cut step, leaving a gap filled with STI oxide, and because the last transistor next to a cut fin end behaves differently from an interior one (epi grows differently on a fin end and the SiGe stress relaxes there), each cell keeps a full gate position of its own as a buffer at each edge, a permanent isolation gate over the fin end. The boundary sits between the two buffer gates, so a cell with N functional gates is N + 2 CPP wide. The **single diffusion break (SDB)** cuts the fin exactly under one gate position instead: after the dummy poly is removed (Section 3.3, step 6), the fin exposed at the bottom of that one trench is etched away and the trench is filled with dielectric, so the isolation sits where a gate would have been, is exactly one CPP wide, and is shared by both neighbours, each owning half; the cell is N + 1 CPP wide, which is where Section 2.1's "two gates plus a half-CPP boundary on each side" came from. Note the naming collision: this isolation "dummy gate" is a permanent structure in the finished chip (a gate-shaped block of dielectric, or in some flows a metal gate that is never driven), not the sacrificial polysilicon dummy gate of Section 3.3 that every real gate starts life as. A third option, **continuous diffusion** with a tied-off gate, leaves the fin uncut and uses a real transistor held permanently off as the isolation; it is the cheapest to make but the off transistor leaks (the ~1 nA/µm of Section 1), and there is one per cell boundary. The price of SDB: the transistor next to the break sees a relaxed stressor on one side and the dielectric's charge at its fin end, so edge devices lose a few per cent of drive (~) and the library characterises edge and interior devices separately. Intel introduced SDB at 10 nm (it called it a "single dummy gate"), and it is standard on the foundry FinFET and nanosheet nodes from the 7 nm class on.

> **Worked example: what a single diffusion break saves.** A NAND2 has two gate lines. With DDB it is 2 + 2 = 4 CPP wide; with SDB it is 2 + 1 = 3 CPP: 25% less area for the same four transistors, with no change in any pitch. A scan flip-flop with ~20 functional gates goes from 22 to 21 CPP, a 4.5% saving, so the trick pays most on the small cells that make up most of a design. On N7 (CPP 57 nm, 6T cell of 240 nm) the NAND2 goes from 228 × 240 nm to 171 × 240 nm, and its density from 73 to 97.5 MTr/mm². Add a 6T → 5T cell from buried or backside rails (240 → 200 nm at 40 nm MP; see the power-rail paragraph below) and the NAND2 area falls to 171 × 200 nm, 0.625 of the DDB, 6T cell: 1.6× density from two layout changes and zero lithography scaling. That is the arithmetic that keeps node-to-node density gains at 1.15–1.3× while the pitches move by a few per cent.

**Contact over active gate (COAG).** The gate has to be reached from M0 by a gate contact, and classically that contact landed on the part of the gate line that extends past the fins over the STI, at the cell edge or in the n-to-p gap, because a contact etched down onto the gate directly above the fin risked punching through the gate into the fin or shorting sideways to the S/D contacts on either side. The extension plus the contact's overlay margin is a strip roughly one track wide that carries no transistor. The self-aligned-contact flow of Section 3.3 (step 11) makes the gate cap and the S/D contact cap of different materials, so that each contact etch stops on the other's cap; with a tall enough cap and a selective etch, the gate contact can land directly on the gate above the fin, the extension shrinks to the overlay margin alone, and the cell loses a track. Intel introduced COAG at 10 nm and credited it with ~10% of that node's area reduction (~); the risk it carries, a gate contact that punches through the cap into a neighbouring S/D contact, is one of the features widely reported to have slowed Intel's 10 nm yield learning, and every later node treats the cap heights as a critical dimension.

**Buried and backside power rails.** The V_DD and ground rails that run along the top and bottom edges of every row take about two of a 6T cell's tracks (Section 2.2). A **buried power rail (BPR)** sinks each rail into a trench in the STI below the fin level, made of tungsten or ruthenium because copper cannot be exposed to the front-end thermal steps, and reaches it from above through a via; the two tracks are freed and the cell drops to 5T. Backside power (Section 7.3) does the same from below and adds thick, low-resistance wires; imec's roadmap uses BPR as the step to 5T and backside delivery as the step to 4T (~).

**Gate-cut placement.** Each gate line crosses both device strips and must be cut wherever the n and p devices need separate gates and at every row boundary (Section 5.4). The cut needs room: a gate end-cap of ~10–20 nm (~) beyond each fin so that the cut's edge-placement error can never clip one, plus the cut width itself, and all of it sits in the n-to-p gap, which is why that gap is ~40–50 nm of pure overhead in every cell. Placing the cut where it shares space with the isolation already needed between the n and p device, and making it self-aligned to the fins so the end-cap can shrink, is worth a fraction of a track per cell; the forksheet wall of Section 7.1 is the trick taken to its limit.

**Mixed rows.** FinFlex (Section 4.2) and NanoFlex (Section 6.1) let one block mix rows of different fin counts or sheet widths, so that a design pays for wide devices only on the paths that need them.

**System-technology co-optimization (STCO)** extends the same idea from the cell to the package: SRAM, I/O and logic now scale at different rates (Section 8.2), so they go on separate chiplets built on the nodes that suit each and are reassembled in the package (Module 17); AMD's stacked cache dies and separate I/O dies are the visible examples. And the V_T menu is DTCO too: a modern PDK exposes 4–6 threshold-voltage flavours per device type (ultra-low to ultra-high V_T, spanning ~250–300 mV), realized by the WFM/dipole options of Section 3.3; the synthesis tool of Section 1.5 swaps cells between flavours to meet timing on critical paths while holding leakage everywhere else, and a high-performance design ends up ~10–20% low-V_T cells.

### 8.4 Device metrology: seeing what you built

None of these dimensions can be imaged optically; a 6 nm fin is roughly a hundred times smaller than the wavelength of visible light. In-line, the **critical-dimension scanning electron microscope (CD-SEM)** scans an electron beam over the wafer and gives gate/fin widths and line-edge roughness to ~0.5 nm, and **optical critical dimension (OCD) scatterometry** (Module 13) measures how a periodic array of fins or gates diffracts light and fits a geometric model to it, yielding fin height, STI recess, and sheet-stack profiles as model-fitted averages, fast and non-destructively. The definitive tool is the **transmission electron microscope (TEM/STEM)**, which shoots electrons through a slice of the sample thin enough for them to pass and resolves individual atomic columns. That slice, a **lamella** ~50–100 nm thick, is cut from a specific transistor with a **focused ion beam (FIB)**, a fine beam of gallium or xenon ions that mills away material, then lifted out, thinned, and imaged at 200–300 kV. This is how you see the 0.6 nm interfacial layer, count HfO₂ monolayers, check that the inner-spacer cavities filled, or find 1 nm of thinning on one side of a sheet. **Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)** and **electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS)** turn the same instrument into an element mapper, adding the Ge, Hf, Ti, Al, and La distributions, which is how a teardown house reads a competitor's WFM stack. A leading-edge fab runs on the order of thousands of TEM samples per week (~) on Thermo Fisher/Hitachi FIB-TEM lines, and the 1–2 day turnaround of a "TEM answer" gates the yield-learning cycle. Electrical structures in the **scribe line**, the strip between dies that is sawn away at dicing, supply the numbers that ultimately define the node: **ring oscillators** (an odd-numbered loop of inverters that oscillates at a frequency set directly by the gate delay), V_T/I_off/DIBL/SS arrays, **Kelvin** contact-resistance structures (a four-terminal measurement in which current and voltage use separate leads, so the lead resistance drops out), and SRAM macros.

## Summary

- A transistor is a gate-controlled channel between source and drain; it must deliver ~1 mA/µm on and leak < ~1 nA/µm off, an on/off ratio of ~10⁶, at under 1 V.
- Below threshold, current falls only ~10× per 60–65 mV of gate voltage because carriers have a thermal (Boltzmann) tail and the gate acts through a capacitive divider; this floor sets V_DD at ~0.7 V and forbids lowering V_T for free.
- The drain's field penetrates a distance λ into the channel; the gate length must be ≥ ~5–6 λ, and λ shrinks only by thinning the oxide or thinning the body and gating it from more sides: planar (1 side) → FinFET (3) → nanosheet (4).
- Dennard scaling ended ~2004–2005 when V_DD stopped falling; every node since has bought its gain from strain, high-k/metal gate, FinFET and GAA rather than geometry alone.
- Strained silicon (embedded SiGe for PMOS, tensile liners and Si:P for NMOS) raises mobility 20–50% by changing the carriers' effective mass; high-k HfO₂ (κ ~20) at ~1 nm EOT cut gate tunnelling > 10×.
- Replacement metal gate exists because HfO₂ and work-function metals cannot survive the ~1,000 °C S/D anneal: a sacrificial polysilicon gate defines the device, is polished open, pulled out, and replaced by IL/HfO₂/TiN/TiAl/W, with V_T set by metal thickness and dipoles.
- FinFETs turn the channel into a 6–8 nm wide, ~50 nm tall fin gated on three sides, fully depleted and nearly undoped (~0.06 dopant atoms per fin); width is quantized in fins, and fin depopulation (4 → 2 → 1.5 fins) drove cell-height scaling until it ran out.
- Node names are marketing; the real metrics are CPP (~45–50 nm floor), metal pitch (23 nm), cell height (tracks × MP) and MTr/mm²; logic-library, SRAM-macro and whole-chip density gains differ; comparisons require a matched circuit mix.
- Nanosheets stack 3–4 Si ribbons from a Si/SiGe superlattice; SiGe is a sacrificial scaffold, the inner spacer is the hardest step (5–8 nm lateral recess, equal for every sheet), and channel release needs > 100:1 selectivity without stiction.
- The ~10–12 nm inter-sheet gap holds only ~2 nm of work-function metal per surface, so V_T tuning moved to dipole layers; Samsung shipped GAA first (2022), TSMC N2 and Intel 18A followed in 2025.
- Contacts now rival the channel: a 15 × 30 nm contact at 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² is ~220 Ω, reached by doping above 10²¹ cm⁻³ so electrons tunnel through the Schottky barrier and by a titanium silicide interface.
- Backside power (Intel PowerVia, TSMC SPR) bonds, flips and thins the wafer to hundreds of nm to bring V_DD from below, freeing rail tracks and cutting IR drop at the cost of a harder thermal path and a flipped wafer for test and packaging.
- With pitches nearly frozen, each node's 1.15–1.3× comes from DTCO layout tricks: a single diffusion break takes a NAND2 from 4 to 3 CPP (25% less area), contact over active gate and buried or backside rails each remove a track, and none of them applies to the 6T SRAM cell.
- Forksheet and CFET are the last geometric cell-height levers (~2028–2032 on imec's roadmap, as of 2025); SRAM has already stopped scaling at ~0.02 µm² because its single-fin transistors must hold a 6σ V_T-mismatch margin in a 2-CPP layout, which is why caches migrate to stacked dies.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"A 3 nm chip has 3 nm transistors."** → Nothing on an N3 die is 3 nm. Gate length ~16 nm, fin width ~6 nm, metal pitch 23 nm. Node names are a marketing cadence; CPP, MP, and MTr/mm² are the real metrics.
- **"FinFETs and nanosheets are faster because they are smaller."** → They are better because the gate controls the channel from more sides (smaller scale length λ), which reduces leakage and allows shorter gates at the same I_off. At the same gate length a planar device may even have higher drive per unit of footprint; the win is electrostatic.
- **"The gate is deposited when the transistor is built."** → In every leading-edge process since 2007 the gate you see in the final chip is the second gate; the first (polysilicon) is a sacrificial placeholder removed after the source/drain anneal (replacement metal gate).
- **"Strain was a planar-era trick that FinFETs and nanosheets no longer need."** → Embedded SiGe:B source/drain is still the main PMOS drive lever at every node, and nanosheet processes are re-engineering strain through the channel itself because the shrinking S/D volume at 45 nm CPP pushes less hard on the channel.
- **"Transistor density doubles every node, so a new GPU has twice the transistors."** → TSMC reports >1.15× chip density for its N2 platform, while its 2025 SRAM demonstration reports 1.1× macro-density improvement. Neither is a universal logic-library ratio; real die density also depends on SRAM, interfaces and unused routing area.
- **"Backside power is just moving wires to the back."** → It requires wafer-to-wafer bonding, thinning the device wafer to a few hundred nanometres, nano-TSVs or backside contacts landed with ~10 nm overlay to features on the other side, and a whole new backside litho/metal module; and it makes heat removal harder.
- **"GAA is a small tweak to FinFET."** → It adds a Si/SiGe superlattice starting material, inner spacers, and a channel-release etch, three of the hardest steps in the flow, and forces V_T tuning to switch from metal thickness to dipoles because the ~10 nm inter-sheet gap cannot hold the old stack.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module consumes everything Part II has built so far: the epitaxial 300 mm wafers of Module 03 (now with a Si/SiGe superlattice grown on top), the ALD/CVD/epi films of Module 06, the SAQP and EUV patterning of Modules 07–08, the fin/gate/recess/release etches of Module 09, and the well, PTS, and S/D activation steps of Module 10. Its output is a wafer with ~100–250 million finished transistors per square millimetre and their first local interconnect (MOL), delivered to the copper/low-k BEOL of Module 12 which will add 15–18 metal layers, and, on 18A and A16 class wafers, a bonded and thinned backside power network that Module 12 and the packaging modules (16–17) will have to contact from below, and that changes how wafer sort (Module 14) probes the finished wafer, since its pads are now on the back of a bonded pair. The economics flow the other way: the foundry (TSMC, Intel, Samsung) owns the transistor recipe, buys the epi, ALD, etch, CMP, bonding and grinding tools from Applied, Lam, TEL, ASM, Ebara, EV Group and Disco, licenses the standard-cell libraries and PDK to fabless customers via Synopsys/Cadence flows (Module 19), and the transistor density and drive current defined here set the die size, yield (Module 13), and cost per function for every chip that follows.
