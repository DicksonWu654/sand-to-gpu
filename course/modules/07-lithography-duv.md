# Module 07: Photolithography I: Photoresist and DUV

Every layer of a chip begins as a pattern that has to be transferred onto the wafer, and **lithography** is the step that does it: the only process in the fab that *creates* geometry rather than modifying a film already there. Its working material is **photoresist**, a light-sensitive polymer film spun onto the wafer whose solubility in a developer changes wherever light hits it, so that after development the wafer carries a stencil of the design. A chip is a stack of ~60–80+ such patterned **layers**, each needing its own lithography pass. A repeating line pattern is described by its **pitch**, the centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring lines, and its **half-pitch**, half of that, which equals the line width when lines and spaces are equal, so "40 nm half-pitch" and "40 nm lines at 80 nm pitch" are the same statement; the printed width of the smallest feature on a layer is its **critical dimension (CD)**; and **overlay** is how accurately one layer's pattern sits on the layer beneath it. In those words the job is brutal: print 40 nm half-pitch (80 nm pitch) in one exposure and, with multi-patterning, pitches down to ~30–40 nm in production, across a 300 mm wafer, placed within ~1.5–2 nm of the layer underneath, at ~275–300 wafers per hour, 60–80+ times per wafer.

It is hard for two reasons. The light used, at 193 nm, is four to ten times *longer* than the features it prints, and light spreads (diffracts) around any opening comparable to or smaller than its wavelength, so a mask opening several times smaller than the wavelength casts no shadow; the lens can collect only a few of the diffracted beams and recombine them into a soft pattern of fringes rather than a crisp copy. The second reason is chemistry: the resist has to turn that soft fringe, whose brightness slides from light to dark over tens of nanometres, into a hard edge that lands within a nanometre or two of where the designer put it, at a hundred billion edges on every wafer.

This module stops short of **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** lithography at 13.5 nm (Module 08). Its subject is **deep ultraviolet (DUV)** lithography: the 248 nm light of the **krypton fluoride (KrF)** laser, the 193 nm light of the **argon fluoride (ArF)** laser, and the older 365 nm **i-line** of the mercury lamp that still prints the coarse layers. By the end you will be able to explain why 193 nm light can print 40 nm lines, what a chemically amplified resist is and why the bake after exposure matters more than the exposure, what a scanner does to a wafer in 12 seconds, why the 157 nm generation died, how SADP and SAQP double and quadruple a pattern without a second exposure, and why EUV took only ~15–25 of the ~80 mask layers on a leading-edge chip while the rest stayed on DUV and i-line. Hold onto that last fact: a **mask set** for a leading node (one photomask per layer) is still ~75–85% DUV/i-line/KrF layers.

## Before you start

- **Waves, wavelength and interference.** Light is a wave. Two waves add up where their crests coincide and cancel where a crest meets a trough. The **refractive index** n of a material is how much slower light travels in it than in vacuum, and the wavelength inside the material shrinks to λ/n (school physics).
- **Diffraction.** A wave passing through an opening comparable to its wavelength spreads out instead of travelling straight. A *repeating* pattern of openings sends the light into a few distinct directions, which is why the grooves of a CD throw a rainbow.
- **Acids, bases and solubility.** Polymers carrying –OH (phenol) or –COOH (carboxylic acid) groups dissolve in a basic solution; oily, **nonpolar** polymers do not, but dissolve in organic solvents. A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction and comes out of it unchanged, ready to act again (school chemistry).
- **The wafer and its dies.** A 300 mm wafer carries a few hundred chips (**dies**) separated by narrow streets called **scribe lanes**; the scanner exposes it one rectangle, or **field**, of 26 × 33 mm at a time, each holding one or several dies (Module 03, Module 05).
- **Thin films and hard masks.** Oxide, nitride and carbon films are deposited by chemical vapour deposition (CVD) or **atomic layer deposition (ALD)**; ALD lays down one atomic layer per gas pulse, so film thickness is counted rather than guessed (Module 06).
- **Plasma etch and selectivity.** A plasma removes material **anisotropically** (downward only, not sideways), and every material etches at its own rate; the ratio of the rate of the material you want removed to the rate of the mask protecting the rest is the **selectivity** (Module 09, ahead).
- **Photomasks and pellicles.** A photomask is a quartz plate carrying one layer's pattern in a chromium or molybdenum-silicide film at 4× the final size, protected from dust by a thin transparent membrane, the **pellicle** (Module 04).
- **Node names and statistics.** "N7" or "3 nm" is a marketing label for a process generation, not a feature size (Module 00). The industry quotes nearly every tolerance as **3σ**, three standard deviations, the spread that contains ~99.7% of measurements (Module 13).

## What a Litho Layer Has to Achieve

Before the machines, two new words and a sharper sense of two you already have. In process terms a layer is one patterning pass: one resist coat, one exposure, one develop, followed by whatever etch or implant the pattern is for, and the mask set is the collection of photomasks, one per layer, that defines a product (~80 for a leading-edge logic chip). A **field** is the 26 × 33 mm rectangle the scanner exposes in one shot, stepped across the wafer ~90–100 times. And **tone** says which way the resist responds: in a **positive-tone** process the exposed regions dissolve in the developer and the unexposed regions stay; in a **negative-tone** process the exposed regions stay.

Every layer is judged by three quantities. CD: is every feature the size the design asked for, everywhere on the wafer and along its whole length? It is measured with a **CD-SEM**, a scanning electron microscope that measures line widths at a few dozen sites per wafer with ~0.1 nm precision (Module 13). Overlay is measured on targets in the scribe lanes: a box or grating from the previous layer with the new layer's printed on top, so the offset can be read optically. Defects (particles, bubbles, missing or bridged features) are found by optical or electron-beam inspection. A layer is good only if all three are within budget at once.

> **Intuition: why 193 nm light can print 40 nm lines.** A mask carrying a fine grating of lines does to light what the grooves on a CD do to a lamp: it throws the light into a few distinct beams at distinct angles, and the finer the grating, the steeper the angles. The straight-through beam is uniform light and carries no information about the lines; the pattern lives in the *difference* between that beam and the first side beam. If a lens catches both and brings them back together, they interfere and produce bright and dark fringes whose spacing is exactly the grating pitch. That is an image, even though no beam ever "saw" a shadow of a line: the lens does not need to resolve the line, only to catch two beams. How steep an angle it can catch, and how much a wider lens or a tilted beam helps, is the story of the Optics section.

That argument in words, before the symbols: the smallest half-pitch a lens can print equals a cleverness factor, times the wavelength, divided by the lens's light-gathering aperture. Three levers: shorter light, a wider lens, or more cleverness with illumination, mask and resist. DUV lithography pulled all three, and each has a section below.

## The Litho Cell at a Glance

A **litho cell** is a photoresist **track** bolted to a **scanner** through a sealed interface, so that a wafer travels coat → expose → develop without leaving a controlled environment. The track is a coater/developer: a linked cabinet of spin cups, hot plates, chill plates and robots that puts the resist on, bakes it, and after exposure develops it. The scanner projects the mask pattern onto the wafer one field at a time. They are separate purchases from separate companies: the track market is led by Tokyo Electron (TEL, CLEAN TRACK LITHIUS Pro Z series, roughly 85–90% of the leading-edge coater/developer market) with SCREEN (SOKUDO DUO series) as the main alternative; the scanner is almost always ASML (TWINSCAN NXT series) at the leading edge, with Nikon (NSR-S635E and, since 2024, NSR-S636E) a distant second in ArF immersion and Canon strong in i-line/KrF. "Immersion" will recur constantly, so a first definition now: in **immersion** lithography the ~3 mm gap between the lens's last element and the wafer is filled with ultrapure water instead of air, which lets the lens deliver steeper beams into the resist and so print finer pitches (the Optics section explains why); an "ArF immersion" or "193i" scanner is the leading-edge DUV tool, and the water is why the wafer's resist has to be water-resistant, as the resist section will show.

The track has to match the scanner's throughput. A modern ArF immersion scanner runs ~275–300 wafers per hour (wph), so the interval between successive wafers leaving the cell, the **takt time**, is 3,600 s ÷ 300 ≈ 12 s. A wafer occupies a bake plate for 60–90 s, so 60–90 s ÷ 12 s ≈ 5–7.5 wafers are always baking at once and the track needs 6–8 plates per bake step in parallel, plus multiple coat and develop cups. The robots are scheduled so that every wafer sees identical delays between steps, because for the resists described next the delay between exposure and post-exposure bake changes the final CD measurably.

The sequence for one layer:

1. **Surface prep**: a vapour of hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS, an organosilicon liquid whose chemistry is given in the track section) makes the oxide surface water-repellent so the resist sticks, or an anti-reflective / hard-mask underlayer stack is spun on to kill reflections and carry the pattern into the etch.
2. **Spin coat**: resist solution is dispensed and the wafer spun at thousands of rpm, flinging off the excess to leave a film ~100 nm thick; solvent at the rim removes the **edge bead**, the ridge of resist that surface tension piles up at the wafer's edge.
3. **Soft bake (post-apply bake, PAB)**: a hot plate drives off residual solvent so the film's chemistry is stable.
4. **Chill**: a plate at 23 °C stops the bake reproducibly.
5. **Topcoat (immersion only, if used)**: a water-repellent overlayer so the scanner's water does not pull chemistry out of the resist; most modern resists are topcoat-free.
6. **Scanner: alignment, levelling, exposure**: the wafer is located to ~1 nm, its height mapped, and each field exposed through the mask, creating a latent chemical image.
7. **Post-exposure bake (PEB)**: heat lets the photogenerated acid catalyse the solubility change; for modern resists this is where the pattern is really formed.
8. **Chill**.
9. **Develop, rinse, spin dry**: a puddle of developer dissolves the soluble regions; water rinses it away.
10. **Hard bake (optional)**: toughens the resist for etch; often replaced by a UV cure or skipped.
11. **After-develop inspection (ADI)**: CD-SEM, overlay and defect inspection on separate metrology tools check the three quantities above.
12. **Disposition**: pass to etch, or strip and rework.

Steps 1–5 and 7–10 happen on the track, step 6 on the scanner, step 11 on separate metrology tools (Module 13). Total time in the cell is ~30–60 minutes per wafer, most of it queueing; the exposure itself takes ~12–15 s.

> **Why it matters downstream: the one reversible step.** Until the etch, nothing irreversible has happened to the wafer: a resist pattern that fails inspection can be stripped and the wafer sent round the cell again (**rework**), at a cost of ~1 hour. A wrong pattern that reaches etch costs the wafer. That is why so much of a fab's metrology sits after develop rather than after etch. The litho cell is also the fab's pacing item: with ~80 layers per wafer and ~300 wafers per hour per cell, the number of scanners a fab owns sets its wafer-start capacity, and the cells are its largest single capital line.

## Photoresist Chemistry

A photoresist must do three things well: be transparent enough that light reaches the bottom of the film but absorb enough to react; change its solubility by orders of magnitude on exposure; and survive a plasma etch afterward. The direction of the solubility change is the resist's **tone**: a **positive-tone** resist becomes *more* soluble where light hits it, so exposed regions dissolve and a clear opening in the mask becomes a hole in the resist; a **negative-tone** resist becomes *less* soluble, so exposed regions stay. Two chemical families have done this work for forty years.

### DNQ/Novolac: The g-line and i-line Workhorse

The resist used from the 1970s through the 350 nm node (and still used for most non-critical layers and in packaging) is a two-component system: a **novolac** resin (a phenol-formaldehyde polymer, soluble in aqueous base because of its phenolic –OH groups) plus 10–25 wt% (**wt%**, percent by weight) of a **diazonaphthoquinone (DNQ)** photoactive compound. The DNQ is a **dissolution inhibitor**: it hydrogen-bonds with the novolac and makes the mixture nearly insoluble in developer (dissolution rate ~1–2 nm/s versus 100+ nm/s for pure novolac). The g and i in "g-line" and "i-line" are simply the letters historically assigned to the mercury lamp's emission lines at 436 nm and 365 nm. In plain language, a photon kicks a nitrogen pair (N₂) off the DNQ molecule; what is left rearranges itself and grabs a water molecule to become an acid, and acids dissolve in the basic developer. In the chemist's language, the DNQ undergoes a Wolff rearrangement, releasing N₂ and forming a ketene, which reacts with trace water in the film to form an **indene carboxylic acid**, a *dissolution promoter*, so exposed regions dissolve 100–1,000× faster than unexposed ones. The change is one-to-one: one photon converts at most one DNQ molecule, and the **quantum efficiency**, the fraction of absorbed photons that actually cause the reaction, is ~0.2–0.3, so it takes three to five photons per converted molecule. This is why DNQ resists need a large exposure **dose**, the light energy delivered per unit area of resist, measured in mJ/cm² (millijoules per square centimetre): 100–300 mJ/cm², and why, with the 10× drop in available lamp/laser intensity at 248 nm, they were too slow for DUV (novolac is also nearly opaque at 248 nm).

DNQ/novolac keeps its place because it is cheap, insensitive to airborne amines, tolerant of long delays, and in thick (1–5 µm) films has excellent etch resistance; for a 6 µm-thick implant mask or a packaging redistribution layer, nobody uses anything else.

### Chemically Amplified Resists (CARs)

The concept, invented at IBM by Ito, Willson and Fréchet in the early 1980s, is that one photon should trigger many chemical events.

> **Intuition: a lit match passed hand to hand.** A catalyst is a helper that makes a reaction happen and comes out of it unchanged. In a chemically amplified resist the catalyst is a single proton (an acid, H⁺). Think of it as a lit match: it sets fire to one molecule's protecting group, is handed on intact, sets fire to the next, and so on, hundreds or thousands of times, until it is finally snuffed out by a molecule of base. One photon lights one match; the bake gives the match time to travel. That is the amplification, and it is also the problem: the further the match wanders, the blurrier the edge of the burnt region.

The resist has three components: a polymer backbone whose base-solubility is blocked by an acid-labile **protecting group** (**labile** means easily cleaved off); a **photoacid generator (PAG)**, a salt that falls apart into a strong acid H⁺ when it absorbs a photon (typically a triphenylsulfonium or diaryliodonium cation with a fluorinated sulfonate anion such as nonaflate or perfluorobutanesulfonate, at ~2–10 wt%); and a **quencher**, ~0.1–1 wt% of a base such as a hindered amine.

The mechanism, step by step:

1. **Exposure**: a photon absorbed by the PAG (or by the polymer, which transfers the energy) decomposes it and releases a proton. At 30 mJ/cm² and a typical absorbance this creates on the order of 10¹⁹–10²⁰ acid molecules per cm³, roughly one per (2–5 nm)³. Almost nothing has changed in solubility yet; this is the **latent image**.
2. **PEB**: at 100–120 °C the polymer is near its **glass transition**, the temperature above which it softens from a rigid glass into a rubbery state in which small molecules can move, and the acid becomes mobile. Each proton catalyzes the **deprotection** of a protecting group: for KrF-era resists the group is **tert-butoxycarbonyl (t-BOC)** on **poly(hydroxystyrene) (PHS)**, a polymer of phenol rings, which cleaves to release CO₂ and isobutylene and expose a phenol; for ArF resists (where PHS is opaque, because its flat aromatic rings absorb 193 nm light, so **aliphatic** methacrylate backbones, built from chain- and cage-shaped carbon skeletons with no aromatic rings, are used) it is a tertiary ester such as methyladamantyl or ethylcyclopentyl methacrylate, which cleaves to a carboxylic acid plus an alkene. Critically, the proton is regenerated at the end of each reaction, so one acid deprotects hundreds to a few thousand sites before it is lost. This is the **amplification**: the chemical gain is ~10²–10³.
3. **Quenching**: the quencher base neutralizes acid, which sets a *threshold*: in weakly exposed regions (the tails of the **aerial image**, the pattern of light intensity the lens projects onto the resist, which fades gradually rather than sharply at the edge of every feature) the little acid present is fully consumed and nothing deprotects, while in strongly exposed regions the acid overwhelms the base. This sharpens the chemical image beyond the optical image. Modern resists use a photodecomposable base (PDB), destroyed in exposed regions and surviving in dark ones, which sharpens the edge further.
4. **Develop**: deprotected polymer (now carrying phenol or carboxylic acid groups) dissolves in the standard aqueous developer, a 0.26 N solution of **tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH)** in water (**N** is normality, moles of hydroxide per litre; the Development section has the details), at hundreds of nm/s; protected polymer does not.

The quencher threshold is what gives a CAR its **contrast**, the sharpness with which it switches from insoluble to soluble as the dose rises. Contrast is read from the **characteristic curve**, a plot of the fraction of resist thickness remaining after development against the logarithm of dose: its slope, written here as γ<sub>R</sub> to keep it apart from the surface tension γ used in the pattern-collapse example later, is ~2–4 for DNQ resists and > 10 for good CARs. A resist with γ<sub>R</sub> > 10 behaves almost like a switch: below one dose nothing dissolves, a little above it everything does, and the printed edge lands wherever the light intensity crosses that threshold. That "fixed threshold" is the picture the process-window section relies on. Two doses read off the curve are used all through litho: **dose-to-clear** (E0), the dose at which a large open area just develops fully, and **dose-to-size** (Esize), the dose at which a line prints at its intended CD, typically ~1.5–2× E0 for positive tone; a layer's working dose is Esize, and the process-window measurement later in the module is centred on it.

**Photospeed** (how little dose a resist needs; a "faster" resist needs less) and resolution now trade against each other through **acid diffusion**. During PEB the acid random-walks a distance L = √(2Dt), where D is the acid's diffusion coefficient in the hot polymer (how fast it wanders, in nm² per second) and t is the bake time; L is typically 5–20 nm in modern ArF resists (older KrF resists, 20–50 nm). Put numbers in: for L = 10 nm and t = 60 s, D = L²/(2t) = 100/120 ≈ 0.8 nm²/s. Because L grows only as the square root of t, doubling the bake time increases the blur by just √2; but D rises steeply with temperature, and a bake ~10 °C hotter can roughly double it, which is why the plate is held to 0.1 °C. Longer diffusion means more amplification and smoother lines (the blur averages out shot noise and the standing wave), but it blurs the edge. To print 40 nm half-pitch with a 20 nm blur is impossible, so ArF immersion resists use bulky counter-anions and polymer-bound PAGs to keep L below ~10 nm, at a cost in sensitivity; PEB temperature and residual solvent also shift L, which is why the track controls them so tightly.

### Line-Edge Roughness and the RLS Triangle

Because the chemistry is stochastic (discrete photons, discrete PAG molecules, discrete polymer chains whose coiled-up size, the **radius of gyration**, is ~5–10 nm), the developed edge is not straight. **Line-edge roughness (LER)** is the 3σ deviation of one edge from a straight line; **line-width roughness (LWR)** is the 3σ variation of the width, and LWR ≈ √2 × LER if the two edges are uncorrelated. For ArF immersion lines LER is ~3–5 nm 3σ, a substantial fraction of a 40 nm line, and it is worse at low dose and at short diffusion length (less averaging). The dose dependence is **shot noise**: the randomness of counting a small number of discrete events, which scales as the square root of the count, so 400 photons or acids vary by √400 = 20, i.e. 5%.

> **Worked example: the photon and acid budget of a 40 nm line.** A 193 nm photon carries 6.4 eV = 1.03 × 10⁻¹⁸ J, so a dose of 30 mJ/cm² delivers 0.030 J/cm² ÷ (1.03 × 10⁻¹⁸ J) ≈ 2.9 × 10¹⁶ photons/cm². A ~100 nm resist film absorbs only ~10% of them (the film must stay transparent, and the PAG is a minor component), and not every absorbed photon makes an acid, so the film ends up with ~10¹⁹–10²⁰ acid molecules per cm³, one acid in every cube of ~2–5 nm on a side. Now take a 20 nm-long segment of a 20 nm-wide line in a 100 nm-thick film: 20 × 20 × 100 nm = 4 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm³, which at ~10¹⁹/cm³ holds only ~400 acids. Its count fluctuates by √400 = 20, or ~5%, from one segment to the next, and that fluctuation is the seed of line-edge roughness. There is no engineering fix for counting statistics except more counts.

The result is the **resolution / LER / sensitivity (RLS) triangle**: you can get any two of high resolution, low roughness and high sensitivity (low dose), but not all three. The empirical scaling is roughly LER² × dose × resolution³ ≈ constant for a given chemistry, so at fixed resolution, halving the LER costs 4× the dose (diffusion length is the third knob: more blur smooths the edge but coarsens the resolution). This trade-off limits EUV throughput as much as source power does (Module 08).

> **Why it matters downstream: roughness becomes electrical variation.** At the transistor, LER on the gate line becomes variation in gate length, and gate length sets the **threshold voltage** (the gate voltage at which the transistor turns on, Module 11), so a rough gate is a population of transistors that switch at slightly different voltages, which erodes the noise margin of every circuit built from them. At metal lines, LWR becomes resistance variation and creates local hot spots for **electromigration** (metal atoms being pushed along by the current until the wire thins and opens, Module 12). A 3 nm 3σ LER on a 20 nm line is a 15% width variation.

### Immersion Resists and What Goes Wrong in Resist

In an immersion scanner the water film under the lens sweeps across the resist surface, and any point of the wafer is under water for ~1 second per field. Two things can go wrong: PAG and quencher can **leach** into the water (contaminating the lens and changing the resist), and water can leave **watermarks** and bubbles that print as defects. First-generation immersion used a fluorinated **topcoat** (an extra spin-coated layer); modern resists are **topcoat-free**, with fluorinated surfactants that segregate to the surface during soft bake and give a **receding contact angle** (the angle at the trailing edge of a moving droplet; the higher it is, the more cleanly the water film peels off) > 70°, so the meniscus under the lens leaves no droplets even at 500–800 mm/s scan speeds. Leaching is now specified below ~10⁻¹² mol/cm²: multiply by Avogadro's number, 6.0 × 10²³ molecules per mole, and that is 6 × 10¹¹ molecules per cm², or one PAG molecule per ~170 nm² of resist surface (a square ~13 nm on a side), small enough that the lens's last element stays clean for years.

> **What can go wrong: resist failure modes and how they are caught.** *T-top*: airborne amines neutralise the acid in the top few nanometres of a positive-tone line, so that skin never deprotects and never dissolves, leaving a cap that makes the cross-section look like a T; fixed with chemically filtered air and a short exposure-to-bake delay. *Footing*: acid is lost into a basic substrate (a nitride, for example), so the bottom of the line does not clear; fixed by underlayer choice or a thin barrier. *Pattern collapse*: tall narrow lines are pulled together by the surface tension of the rinse water (worked in the next section); fixed with surfactant rinses and by limiting the aspect ratio. *Outgassing*: volatile fragments (isobutylene, CO₂) leaving the resist during exposure deposit on optics; managed by resist design and purge. *Leaching and watermarks* under immersion: managed with topcoat-free resists and a high receding contact angle. Detection is by CD-SEM (top-down for CD, cross-section for T-tops and footing) and defect inspection; the fix is a resist, underlayer, air-filter or rinse-recipe change, and the bad wafers are reworked.

The resists come from a short list of suppliers: JSR, Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK), Shin-Etsu Chemical, Fujifilm and Sumitomo Chemical in Japan, with DuPont (USA) and Merck (Germany) as the main non-Japanese sources; a fab qualifies one or two resists per layer and changes them rarely, because every change means re-tuning the whole recipe.

## The Track Recipes, Step by Step

With the chemistry in hand, the track's steps stop being a list of bakes and become a set of controls on where the acid is, how far it moves, and what the developer can reach.

### HMDS Prime

Silicon oxide surfaces are terminated with hydroxyl (–OH) groups that make them hydrophilic, and resist polymers do not adhere well to a wet, polar surface; the result is pattern lift-off during development. The fix is **hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS)**, (CH₃)₃Si–NH–Si(CH₃)₃, delivered as a vapor onto a wafer held at ~120–150 °C. It reacts with surface silanols, 2 Si–OH + HMDS → 2 Si–O–Si(CH₃)₃ + NH₃, capping the surface with trimethylsilyl groups and making it hydrophobic. The measure of that is the **contact angle**, the angle a water droplet makes with the surface: ~0° means the water spreads flat because the surface loves water, 90° or more means it beads up. HMDS takes the oxide from near zero to ~60–70°, water-repellent enough that the resist, an organic, sticks instead. Vapor prime in a closed chamber (sometimes after a dehydration bake at 150–200 °C) is used rather than liquid because it gives a **monolayer**, a coating one molecule thick, and consumes microliters. On an organic underlayer (the anti-reflective coating or spin-on carbon described next) HMDS is skipped.

### BARC, Standing Waves, and the Tri-Layer Stack

Resist is transparent at the exposure wavelength (light must reach the bottom of the film), so light passes through it, reflects off the substrate, and interferes with the incoming wave.

> **Intuition: two waves travelling in opposite directions.** The wave coming down through the resist and the wave reflected back up from the substrate pass through each other. Where a crest of one meets a crest of the other the light is bright; where a crest meets a trough it is dark. Because the two waves move in opposite directions the bright planes do not move: they sit fixed in the film, one every half wavelength as measured *inside* the resist (λ/n). That is a standing wave, and the resist records it as a stack of bright and dark layers.

The result is a **standing wave**: a sinusoidal modulation of absorbed dose through the thickness of the resist, with period λ/(2n), where n is the resist refractive index (~1.7 at 193 nm), so 193.4/(2 × 1.7) ≈ 57 nm. The developed sidewall shows this as a corrugation, and worse, the *total* dose absorbed swings up and down as resist thickness changes (the **swing curve**), so a few nanometers of thickness variation becomes several nanometers of CD variation. On top of reflective layers like polysilicon, aluminum or tungsten, reflectivity can be 30–60%, and CD control is impossible without help.

A **bottom anti-reflective coating (BARC)** is a spin-on polymer, 30–90 nm thick, with a tuned refractive index and absorbance (n ~1.7–1.9 and **extinction coefficient** κ ~0.3–0.5 at 193 nm, κ being the imaginary part of the index, i.e. how strongly the material absorbs) that cancels the reflected wave both by absorption and by destructive interference between the resist/BARC and BARC/substrate reflections. A well-designed BARC brings substrate reflectivity below 1%, and it is opened by a short plasma etch before the underlying film is etched. PEB also smooths standing waves: acid diffusion of ~10 nm averages out a 57 nm-period modulation almost completely, one reason CARs give smoother sidewalls than DNQ resists.

At advanced nodes the resist is only ~80–120 nm thick, for two reasons: the lens keeps the image sharp over only ~60 nm of height (the **depth of focus**, explained in the Optics section), and tall narrow lines collapse from capillary forces during rinse. A film that thin cannot survive a deep etch. The answer is the **tri-layer stack**: a thick **spin-on carbon (SOC)** layer (100–300 nm, an amorphous-carbon-like polymer that **planarizes** the topography, i.e. fills the valleys so the top surface is flat, and is the actual hard mask), a thin **silicon-containing anti-reflective coating (SiARC)** (20–40 nm, a spin-on **siloxane**, a polymer whose backbone alternates silicon and oxygen atoms, with ~30–45% Si), and the thin resist. The resist pattern is etched into the SiARC with a fluorocarbon plasma, then into the SOC with an O₂/N₂ or H₂/N₂ plasma that etches carbon fast and SiARC very slowly (a **selectivity**, the ratio of the two etch rates, of > 10:1), and the tall SOC pattern then masks the etch of the real film. The SiARC doubles as the BARC. All three are spin-coated on the track in sequence, each with its own bake (SOC bake ~250–350 °C to **cross-link** it, i.e. to form chemical bonds between neighbouring polymer chains so the film becomes one rigid, insoluble network that the next coat's solvent cannot redissolve). The underlayer suppliers are listed under Key Players.

### Spin Coating

Resist is dispensed as a solution, typically 2–10% solids in a casting solvent such as PGMEA (propylene glycol monomethyl ether acetate) or ethyl lactate, and the wafer is accelerated to 1,500–4,000 rpm (typical acceleration ~10,000 rpm/s) and spun for 20–60 s. The bulk of the fluid is flung off within about a second; as the film thins to a few micrometers, solvent evaporation raises the viscosity sharply and freezes it. The classic result (Meyerhofer, 1978) is that final thickness t scales as

t ∝ C · η<sup>a</sup> / ω<sup>b</sup>, with a ≈ 0.4–0.6 and b ≈ 0.5,

where C is the solids concentration, η the viscosity and ω the spin speed. Thickness therefore goes as roughly 1/√(rpm): doubling spin speed reduces thickness by ~30%. The fab picks a speed near the middle of the vendor's "spin curve", where the slope is shallow, so that a 1% rpm error gives < 0.5% thickness error. Modern tracks hold thickness uniformity to ~1 nm 3σ across a 300 mm wafer for a 100 nm film.

> **Worked example: reading a spin curve.** A resist that gives 100 nm at 2,000 rpm gives 100 × √(2,000/3,000) ≈ 82 nm at 3,000 rpm. Because thickness goes as the −½ power of speed, a 1% speed error moves thickness by ~0.5%, i.e. 0.5 nm on a 100 nm film, which is why spin motors are controlled to a fraction of a percent. The cost side is just as simple: the more expensive resists are dispensed at ~0.5–1.5 mL per wafer, and at $1,000–3,000 per litre for ArF immersion resist that is ~$1–3 of resist per wafer per layer (EUV resist several times more), so a fab running 80 layers spends more on resist than the number suggests, and every reduced-volume nozzle pays for itself.

Two practical details. **Dynamic dispense** (while the wafer turns slowly at ~500 rpm) in closed **solvent-saturated cups** keeps the film from skinning over before it is uniform. And surface tension piles fluid up at the wafer edge into the edge bead, up to several micrometers tall, which would flake during handling, so an **edge bead removal (EBR)** nozzle dissolves the outer 1–2 mm (the edge exclusion is ~1.5–2 mm); the backside gets a rinse too, because any resist there becomes a particle that lifts the wafer on the scanner **chuck** (the flat plate on the stage that holds the wafer down, by vacuum, during exposure) and destroys focus.

### Soft Bake

After coating the film still contains 10–30% residual solvent, and the problem with that solvent is that it is invisible: the film looks finished, but how much solvent it holds decides how far the acid will wander two steps later, in the PEB. The **soft bake** (post-apply bake, PAB), 90–110 °C for 60–90 s, drives the residual solvent to ~2–5%, densifies the film and removes stress. Residual solvent **plasticizes** the film (softens it, the way water softens gelatin, so molecules move more freely), which increases acid diffusion during PEB and changes the dissolution rate, so the bake's real job is to leave every wafer with the *same* small solvent content, not to remove it all.

The bake is done on a **proximity hot plate**: the wafer floats ~0.1 mm above the plate on three pins, and heat reaches it by conduction through that thin gas gap, which brings the wafer to temperature within a few seconds without the particles and sticking that a wafer pressed onto a hot surface would suffer. It also means that the gap *is* the temperature control: a pin worn by a few tens of micrometres, or a warped wafer, changes the local heat flow and prints as a thickness and CD signature with the plate's geometry. Bake plates are held to ±0.1–0.2 °C across the plate, because for CARs the CD sensitivity to bake temperature can be 1–3 nm/°C (early KrF resists were closer to 8 nm/°C). A chill plate at 23 °C follows every bake to stop the process reproducibly.

How is residual solvent known? Directly only in the lab: **thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)** weighs a coated sample as it is heated and reads the solvent off the weight loss, and thermal-desorption mass spectrometry identifies what leaves. In the fab the monitor is indirect: the film shrinks as solvent leaves, so the post-bake thickness measured by **ellipsometry** (inferring thickness from the polarisation change of reflected light, Module 13) on sample wafers from every batch is the routine check, and the CD response at after-develop inspection is the final arbiter.

> **What can go wrong: the soft bake's own failure modes.** *Under-bake* (plate too cool, time too short, or a wafer that sat in a queue and cooled before it arrived): too much solvent stays, acid diffuses further in PEB, lines print wider and blurrier, and the soft film picks up and embeds particles. *Over-bake*: the PAG or quencher (or, in DNQ resists, the photoactive compound) begins to decompose thermally, which shifts the dose the resist needs, and the over-densified film develops more slowly. *Plate non-uniformity and pin wear*: a systematic thickness and CD map with the plate's shape that is the same on every wafer baked on that plate; found by comparing CD maps by plate, fixed by cleaning, re-calibrating or replacing it. *Coat-to-bake delay*: solvent already leaves the film at room temperature, so a variable wait between coat and bake shifts the starting point; the track holds that interval fixed to the second, exactly as it holds the exposure-to-PEB delay.

### Exposure

The wafer goes through the interface to the scanner, is aligned and leveled, and is exposed field by field. Each field, the 26 × 33 mm rectangle printed in one pass, takes ~0.1 s, and with ~90–100 fields (counting the partial ones at the wafer edge) the whole wafer's exposure is ~10–12 s. The dose (energy per unit area, defined in the resist section) is typically 20–40 mJ/cm² for ArF CARs, 20–50 mJ/cm² for KrF CARs, and 100–300 mJ/cm² for i-line DNQ resists, which are ~5–10× slower (hence the invention of chemical amplification). For scale, 30 mJ/cm² is the energy full sunlight delivers in ~0.3 s, but concentrated at a single wavelength and delivered to each point of the wafer in ~10 ms. The exposure creates the latent image of acid; nothing about the resist's solubility has changed yet.

### Post-Exposure Bake

For DNQ resists PEB is optional (it smooths standing waves); for chemically amplified resists it *is* the chemistry: the PEB (90–130 °C, 60–90 s) is where the acid diffuses and catalyzes the deprotection that changes solubility. PEB temperature, time, and especially the delay between exposure and PEB (the **post-exposure delay**, PED) are controlled to the second, because during the delay the acid can be neutralized by airborne amines and can diffuse before the bake. Even **ppb** levels (parts per billion, about one molecule in 10⁹) of ammonia from cleaning chemicals or humans are enough to neutralise the acid in the top few nanometres of the film and produce the T-top described in the resist failure-mode box above. Tracks and scanners run chemically filtered air for this reason.

### Development

Positive-tone development uses an aqueous base, almost universally the 0.26 N TMAH introduced in the resist section, i.e. 2.38 wt% tetramethylammonium hydroxide in water (0.26 moles of hydroxide, OH⁻, per litre), sometimes with a surfactant. The 0.26 N standard, an industry-wide convention since the 1980s, keeps resists and tracks interchangeable. Development is done as a **puddle**: developer dispensed through a slot nozzle covers the stationary wafer for 30–60 s (sometimes a double puddle with fresh developer), then the wafer is rinsed with deionized water and spun dry. A puddle is preferred over dipping the wafer in a bath because the developer is used once (no **loading effect**, the slowing of a developer that has already dissolved a lot of resist) and because in a still puddle the dissolution is **diffusion-limited**: its rate is set by how fast developer molecules and dissolved resist diffuse through the motionless liquid at the film surface, which is the same at every point of a stationary wafer, whereas a stirred or flowing bath is faster where the flow is faster. Exposed resist dissolves at ~100–1,000 nm/s, unexposed resist at < 1 nm/s, and the ratio determines how faithfully the latent chemical image becomes a physical edge.

Deprotection is also a change of polarity: it turns the polymer from an oil-like (**nonpolar**) state that dissolves in organic solvent into a water-loving (**polar**) acid that dissolves in aqueous base. Positive-tone development (PTD) exploits the second; **negative-tone development (NTD)** exploits the first. NTD uses an organic solvent developer (n-butyl acetate is the standard, from Fujifilm's and JSR's early work ~2010) on a standard positive-tone CAR: in the solvent the *unexposed* (still-protected, nonpolar) regions dissolve and the exposed (deprotected, polar) regions stay, flipping the tone of the same resist. The reason to bother is image quality for small holes. A layer of contact holes or vias can be drawn on the mask two ways: as a **dark-field mask**, mostly opaque with a small clear hole for each contact, or as a **bright-field mask**, mostly clear with each contact drawn as a small opaque post. The bright-field version gives a far better aerial image: most of the field is lit, and each dark post is a sharp, deep dip in an otherwise bright background, whereas the small clear holes of a dark-field mask pass little light and print as dim, soft spots whose edges the resist threshold cuts at a shallow slope; strong off-axis illumination (light arriving at steep angles, explained in the Optics and illuminator sections) sharpens the bright-field image further. But a positive-tone develop of that image leaves a resist *post* where each dark spot is, the opposite of the hole you want. With NTD the unexposed post regions are what dissolve, so the dark spot in the image becomes the hole in the resist: NTD lets the fab use the better image and still end up with a hole. NTD became the standard for ArF immersion contact and via layers and for the "block" and "cut" masks in multi-patterning; it also reduces pattern collapse, because the remaining resist is the deprotected material, which swells less in the solvent, and its rinse is another solvent (e.g. 4-methyl-2-pentanol), not water.

> **Worked example: why lines fold over during rinse (pattern collapse).** As rinse water evaporates from between two resist lines, the curved water surface pulls them together with a capillary (Laplace) pressure of ~2γ cos θ / spacing, where γ is the surface tension of water, 72 mN/m, and θ is the contact angle of water on the resist, close to zero after rinse so cos θ ≈ 1. For two 40 nm-spaced lines: 2 × 0.072 N/m ÷ (40 × 10⁻⁹ m) ≈ 3.6 MPa, or ~36 atmospheres, pushing two lines that are themselves only ~20–40 nm wide toward each other. Whether they survive depends on their **aspect ratio**, height divided by width: 100 nm-tall lines 40 nm wide are aspect ratio 2.5, and lines much taller than ~2.5× their width bend until their tops touch. Surfactant rinses lower γ; in production the usual answer is keeping the aspect ratio below ~2.5, which, with 40 nm lines, is why resist films are ~100 nm and no thicker.

### Hard Bake, Inspection, and Rework

A **hard bake** at 110–130 °C after development (or a UV cure) cross-links the resist for etch resistance; for tri-layer stacks it is often skipped. The wafer then goes to CD-SEM (a few dozen sites), overlay metrology (dozens to hundreds of targets) and optical inspection. Because nothing irreversible has happened yet, patterns that fail (out-of-spec CD, overlay or defects) can be **reworked**: strip the resist and BARC in a plasma **ash** (an oxygen plasma that burns the organic film off as CO₂ and water, Module 09) or a wet solvent, and run the wafer through the litho cell again.

> **What can go wrong: the track's own defects.** *Striations and comets*: a particle or gel speck in the dispensed resist leaves a radial streak or a comet-shaped void as the film spins out; caught by after-develop inspection (ADI) and traced to the resist filter or nozzle. *Edge-bead flakes*: an incompletely removed bead breaks off during handling and lands as a particle on the next wafer or the chuck. *Backside resist*: a droplet on the back of the wafer lifts it off the scanner chuck locally and throws that die out of focus (a "focus spot"); prevented by the backside rinse. *PED drift*: a queue that lets wafers wait longer than usual between exposure and PEB shifts CD by nanometres, which is why the track schedule is deterministic. *Bake-plate hot spots*: a contaminated or worn plate leaves a local CD signature at the same place on every wafer; found in the CD map and fixed by cleaning or replacing the plate. All are found at ADI, and the answer for the wafer in hand is the same: strip and rework.

## The Optics: Resolution, Depth of Focus, and the Wavelength March

A projection scanner images a **reticle** (the photomask: a 6-inch quartz plate carrying one layer's pattern at 4× the final size) onto the wafer through a lens. How can 193 nm light print 40 nm lines, and what does it cost?

> **Intuition: the lens is a bucket, and the pattern arrives as rain at an angle.** A lens accepts only light arriving within a certain cone of angles; think of it as a bucket held under the mask. A fine pattern throws its diffracted light out at wide angles (the finer the pattern, the wider), and if those beams miss the bucket they are gone and so is the pattern. So you need a wider bucket (a higher **numerical aperture**) or gentler angles (a longer pitch, or a shorter wavelength, since the angle scales with λ/pitch). Putting water in front of the lens widens the bucket in a sneaky way. The light travels from the lens *down* to the wafer, and the fineness of the fringes it draws there is set by how steeply the two beams cross. The last lens element can only bend light so far before it reflects back into the glass instead of leaving it, and that limit is reached sooner when the glass faces air than when it faces water (n = 1.44), because the jump in refractive index at the glass surface is smaller. So a beam pair that would need an impossibly steep crossing angle in air can be delivered into the resist through water; equivalently, the wavelength inside water is 193/1.44 = 134 nm, so the same crossing angle draws finer fringes.

The lens's light-collecting ability is described by the **numerical aperture** NA = n · sin θ, where θ is the half-angle of the cone of light reaching the wafer and n is the refractive index of the medium in front of the lens (1.0 for air, 1.44 for water at 193 nm).

> **Intuition: two beams make an image.** A grating of pitch p scatters light into discrete beams (diffraction **orders**) at angles given by sin θ = mλ/p, with m = 0, ±1, ±2, ...; this is the CD-rainbow effect. The straight-through beam (m = 0) alone is uniform light and carries no pattern information. The pattern is carried by the interference between it and the first side beam (m = ±1): where the two overlap on the wafer they make fringes, and the fringe spacing is exactly the pitch p. Catching only those two beams and letting them interfere is **two-beam imaging**, the least you can do and still get an image. With the light hitting the mask head-on, the lens must catch the 0th order in the middle and both ±1st orders at the sides. Tilt the incoming light so the 0th order enters the lens at one edge, and the +1st order only has to fit on the other side (the −1st is thrown away), so the pitch can be half as fine before it misses. That tilt is where the factor 0.25 below comes from.

The **Rayleigh criterion** for the smallest resolvable half-pitch is

**CD = k1 · λ / NA**

and the corresponding **depth of focus** (the range over which the image stays acceptably sharp) is

**DOF = k2 · λ / NA²**

k1 and k2 are dimensionless process factors: k1 captures how cleverly you use the light (illumination, mask, resist contrast), k2 how much image degradation you can tolerate (typically ~0.5–1). The physics of k1 follows from the box above: a grating of pitch p diffracts light into orders at angles sin θ<sub>m</sub> = m λ/p, and an image needs at least two orders. With on-axis illumination the 0th order goes straight through and the ±1st orders need λ/p < NA to enter the lens, so the smallest pitch is λ/NA and the smallest half-pitch is 0.5 λ/NA: **k1 = 0.5** for conventional illumination. Tilt the illumination so the 0th order enters the lens at one edge and only the +1st order needs to fit on the other side: now λ/p < 2 NA, so the pitch can be λ/(2NA) and the half-pitch 0.25 λ/NA: **k1 = 0.25 is the hard physical floor** for single-exposure two-beam imaging. The industry went from k1 ~0.8 (1980s, when the resist, not the lens, set the limit) to ~0.5 by the late 1990s to ~0.28–0.30 in production 193 nm immersion today, about as close to 0.25 as line-edge roughness and process window allow.

The three levers are therefore λ, NA and k1, and the history of lithography is the history of pushing all three:

| Era | Source | λ (nm) | NA (max, production) | Half-pitch reach (single exposure) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1980s | Hg g-line | 436 | 0.3–0.45 | ~1 µm to 0.5 µm |
| late 1980s–1990s | Hg i-line | 365 | 0.5–0.65 | 0.5–0.35 µm |
| 1995–2005 | KrF excimer | 248 | 0.6–0.8 (later 0.93) | 250–130 nm |
| 2001– | ArF excimer (dry) | 193 | 0.75–0.93 | 90–65 nm |
| 2007– | ArF immersion | 193 (134 effective in water) | 1.35 | ~40 nm half-pitch (80 nm pitch); ~76 nm pitch with the most aggressive k1 ≈ 0.27 |
| (cancelled ~2003) | F₂ excimer | 157 | (0.85 planned) | never shipped |
| 2019– | EUV (Module 08) | 13.5 | 0.33 (0.55 High-NA) | 13 nm (8 nm) |

> **Worked example: Rayleigh resolution for 193 nm immersion.**
> ASML NXT:2100i, λ = 193.4 nm, NA = 1.35 (water, n = 1.437). At an aggressive production k1 of 0.28:
> CD = 0.28 × 193.4 / 1.35 = **40.1 nm half-pitch**, i.e. an 80 nm pitch.
> At the theoretical floor k1 = 0.25: CD = 0.25 × 193.4 / 1.35 = 35.8 nm, a 71.6 nm pitch. In practice the tightest single-exposure 193i pitches in production are ~76–80 nm (at the 7 nm-class nodes the first metal levels printed in a single 193i pass sit at ~76–80 nm pitch precisely because of this limit, while the 36–40 nm-pitch M0/M1 levels below them needed SADP/SAQP; below ~76 nm, multi-patterning is mandatory).
> Depth of focus: DOF = k2 · λ / NA² = 0.6 × 193.4 / 1.35² = 0.6 × 106.1 nm = **~64 nm** total (±32 nm). That is the entire focus budget for topography, wafer flatness, chuck contamination, leveling error, and lens field curvature combined. It is why resist films are ~100 nm, why underlayers planarize, and why the scanner measures the wafer surface height at every field to nanometer precision.
> For comparison, an i-line tool at NA 0.6 and k1 = 0.6 gives CD = 365 nm, with DOF ~0.6 × 365/0.36 = 600 nm. The DOF shrank tenfold; the tolerable wafer non-flatness with it.

### Why 157 nm Died and Immersion Won

The obvious next wavelength after 193 nm was the F₂ excimer at 157 nm, and the industry spent ~1998–2003 on it. It failed on materials. **Fused silica**, glass made of pure SiO₂ and the material of every DUV lens element, is opaque at 157 nm, so every lens element would have had to be calcium fluoride (CaF₂), which turned out to have intrinsic **birefringence** (a refractive index that differs for the two polarisations of light, so that a single sharp focus cannot be formed) that scrambled polarization at the high NAs needed, and which could not be grown in large, defect-free **boules** (the single-crystal ingots from which lens blanks are cut) fast enough. No adequate pellicle material existed: polymers darkened, and thin fluoride "hard pellicles" introduced aberrations. Resist polymers had to be fluorinated to be transparent and had poor etch resistance. Meanwhile, in 2002–2004, Burn Lin at TSMC and others made the case that water between lens and wafer at 193 nm would deliver the same gain with existing lenses, masks and resists. Water at 193 nm has n = 1.437 and an absorbance of only ~0.01–0.05 per cm (an absorption length of tens of centimetres), so a ~1 mm-thick film absorbs well under 1%. Since NA = n sin θ, and sin θ is bounded by ~0.93–0.94 in practical designs, the achievable NA jumps from 0.93 to ~1.35, an effective wavelength of 193/1.44 = 134 nm. The F₂ programs were cancelled in 2003; ASML shipped the first production immersion tool (TWINSCAN XT:1250i) in 2004 and reached NA 1.35 with the XT:1900i in 2007. Proposals to go further with high-index fluids (n ~1.65) and a high-index crystal for the last lens element (LuAG, lutetium aluminium garnet, with n ~2 at 193 nm) toward NA 1.55–1.7 were abandoned by ~2008 in favor of double patterning and, eventually, EUV.

Immersion brought its own engineering: lens and wafer are separated by a ~3 mm gap filled by a water meniscus confined by an **immersion hood** that injects degassed, millikelvin-controlled ultrapure water and recovers it with a vacuum ring while the wafer scans at up to ~800 mm/s underneath. The temperature control is not fussiness: the rate at which water's refractive index changes with temperature, **dn/dT**, is −10⁻⁴/K, so a 1 mK change shifts n by 10⁻⁷, and over each millimetre (10⁶ nm) of water path that changes the optical path length by ~0.1 nm, a meaningful fraction of the focus budget. Bubbles, thermal gradients and resist leaching were the early yield killers, largely solved by 2008–2010.

Optics can also go wrong in ways the equations hide, and the scanner section deals with each: **aberrations** (the lens's wavefront departing from perfect, which shifts and distorts features by pattern type), **flare** (stray scattered light that fills in dark regions and lowers contrast), **lens heating** (the glass absorbs a little of the kilowatt-class UV flux and its index drifts during a **lot**, the batch of ~25 wafers that travels through the fab in one carrier and is processed together, so that the first and last wafers of the lot see a slightly different lens), and **polarization loss** (at NA 1.35 the wrong polarization simply does not form fringes). Each is measured on the tool and corrected in real time, and each is a reason the machine costs what it does.

## Anatomy of a 193 nm Immersion Scanner

The machine's job before its parts: it must image a 40 nm-half-pitch pattern over a 26 × 33 mm field, repeat that for ~100 fields per wafer and ~300 wafers per hour, hold every point of the wafer within ~±30 nm of best focus, and place the pattern within ~1 nm of where the previous layer's pattern lies, all while the wafer is moving at ~800 mm/s underneath the lens. That is nanometre mechanics at accelerations a car crash would recognise, a tonne of glass warmed by kilowatts of ultraviolet light, and a puddle of water dragged across the wafer without leaving a drop behind. The figure of merit for placement is **matched-machine overlay (MMO)**: the placement error when one tool prints a layer on top of a layer printed by a *different* tool, the case that matters in a fab with dozens of scanners. The machine is easiest to understand by following the light.

### The Light Source: ArF Excimer Laser

The **excimer laser** (Cymer, an ASML subsidiary since 2013, XLR series; Gigaphoton, a Komatsu subsidiary, GT series) sits in the **sub-fab**, the service floor beneath the cleanroom that houses the pumps, gas cabinets and power supplies the tools upstairs depend on (Module 05), with the beam piped up to the scanner. An **excimer** ("excited dimer") is a two-atom molecule that holds together only while excited and flies apart the instant it emits its photon. A mixture of argon, fluorine (~0.1%) and neon buffer at ~3–4 bar is excited by a pulsed high-voltage discharge; because the ArF* molecule's ground state is dissociative, always empty, its 193.4 nm transition has automatic **population inversion**, the condition every laser needs: more molecules in the upper state than the lower. Each pulse is ~10–15 mJ and ~100 ns long; at 6 kHz the output is ~60–90 W. The energy varies ~5% pulse to pulse, so each point on the wafer receives ~30–100 pulses and the scanner controls dose by counting and trimming pulses.

The natural linewidth of the ArF transition is ~500 pm, hopeless for a refractive lens because of **dispersion**: glass bends shorter wavelengths more than longer ones (a prism), so a lens made only of glass focuses each wavelength at a different height, and for fused silica at 193 nm the chromatic focus shift is ~0.3–1 µm per picometre of wavelength (an order of magnitude; it depends on the lens design), against a 64 nm depth of focus. The laser is therefore built as a **master oscillator / power amplifier (MOPA)**: a low-power oscillator with a prism-and-grating line-narrowing module reduces the bandwidth to ~0.3 pm (E95, the width containing 95% of the energy; the **full width at half maximum (FWHM)**, the width of the spectral peak measured at half its height, is ~0.1–0.15 pm), and a second discharge chamber amplifies the narrowed seed. Bandwidth is controlled per layer because it changes the contrast of small features, and the center wavelength is tuned (stability ~0.05 pm) to compensate for lens heating and barometric pressure. The gas is periodically refilled because fluorine reacts with chamber materials; the neon buffer (~96% of the fill) is why the 2014 and 2022 Ukraine crises briefly threatened lithography supply (Module 04).

> **Worked example: pulses, dose and throughput.** A point on the wafer passes under a slit ~8 mm tall at ~800 mm/s, so it is illuminated for 8/800 = 0.01 s = 10 ms; at 6 kHz that is 60 pulses. A 30 mJ/cm² dose is therefore delivered as 60 pulses of 0.5 mJ/cm² each. A 5% pulse-to-pulse energy jitter averages down as 1/√N: 5%/√60 ≈ 0.65%, and the scanner brings it lower still by counting pulses and trimming the energy of the last few, which is why the dose spec is ~0.3%. Throughput follows from the same geometry: a 300 mm wafer holds ~82 full 26 × 33 mm fields; with the partial fields at the edge that must also be exposed it is ~95–100 shots. Each shot is a ~0.05 s scan (41 mm of travel at 800 mm/s) plus ~0.05 s of stepping to the next field, so ~0.1 s per field and ~10 s of exposure per wafer. Add ~1 s for the stage swap described below and the machine spends ~12 s per wafer: 3,600/12 = 300 wafers per hour.

### The Illuminator and Off-Axis Illumination

The illuminator turns the laser's rectangular, partially coherent beam into a uniform, precisely shaped angular distribution at the reticle (**coherence** is whether the light waves at different points across the mask are in step with one another; the two ideas connect through direction: light arriving from a single direction is in step everywhere, i.e. fully coherent, and because every point of the mask then sees exactly the same interference, each edge in the image is surrounded by ringing fringes; a **partially coherent** beam, a mixture of directions, washes those fringes out because each direction puts them in a slightly different place, and that averaging is what an imaging system wants). The **pupil** is the set of directions from which light arrives at the mask, drawn as a disc: the centre is light arriving head-on, a point at the edge is light arriving at the steepest angle the lens can accept. The key element in modern ASML tools is **FlexRay**, a programmable array of several thousand micromirrors that can steer the beam into any pupil shape. The pupil map is drawn with radius σ = 1 being light at the edge of the lens NA; this pupil-fill radius σ is an optics symbol unrelated to the statistical 3σ. Conventional illumination fills a disk of σ ~0.3–0.8. **Off-axis illumination (OAI)** puts the light only at the edges of the pupil, and it is the "tilt the light so the 0th and +1st orders both just fit" trick of the Optics section, done for real:

- **Dipole**: two poles on the x (or y) axis. For a grating of lines perpendicular to the dipole axis, the 0th order from one pole and the 1st order from the other end up symmetrically placed in the lens, giving two-beam interference with the best contrast and DOF, down to k1 = 0.25. It only works for one orientation of lines, which is why 1D "gridded" layouts became the rule at 20 nm and below.
- **Quadrupole / Quasar**: four poles at 45° (quasar) or on-axis (quadrupole), a compromise for layouts with both horizontal and vertical lines.
- **Freeform / source-mask optimization (SMO)**: since source shape and mask pattern jointly determine the image, both are co-optimized numerically for a layer's critical patterns; the irregular pupil that results is what FlexRay reproduces. SMO is standard for every critical 193i layer.

The illuminator also sets the **polarization**. Two light waves only interfere if their electric fields oscillate along the same direction. At NA 1.35 the interfering beams meet at angles near 90° in the resist, and for **TM polarization** (electric field in the plane containing the two crossing beams) the two fields are then nearly perpendicular to each other and cannot cancel, so no fringes form. The illuminator therefore polarizes each pole tangentially (**TE**, electric field perpendicular to that plane, so the two fields stay parallel), which is necessary, not optional, for high-NA immersion contrast.

### The Reticle and Reticle Stage

The **reticle** (photomask) is a 152 × 152 × 6.35 mm fused-silica plate with a patterned **absorber**, the opaque chromium or molybdenum-silicide film that blocks the light (Module 04 covers making it). All modern scanners use **4× reduction**: features on the mask are four times larger than on the wafer, which relaxes mask-making tolerances by 4×. It is not quite that benign, because at low k1 the **mask error enhancement factor (MEEF)**, the ratio of the printed CD error to the mask CD error (at wafer scale), is 2–4. The mechanism is the shallow edge of a low-k1 aerial image: the intensity slides from bright to dark over a distance comparable to the feature itself, so a small change in the mask opening does not just move the edge, it changes how much light spills across the resist threshold on both sides of the feature, and the printed edge moves by more than the mask edge did. At high k1 the image edge is steep and MEEF is ~1; MEEF is largest for the smallest holes and spaces.

> **Worked example: MEEF.** A 4 nm CD error on the mask is 1 nm at wafer scale after 4× reduction. With MEEF = 3, typical for 40 nm contacts, it prints as a 3 nm CD error, 7.5% of a 40 nm feature. That is why mask CD tolerances at leading nodes are held to ~1 nm at mask scale, and why the mask shop's metrology is as demanding as the fab's.

The **pellicle**, a ~0.8 µm nitrocellulose or fluoropolymer membrane on a 5–6 mm frame, keeps particles far out of focus; a 26 × 33 mm wafer field corresponds to a 104 × 132 mm area on the reticle.

The exposure is a **scan**: the illuminator produces a slit ~26 mm wide (the full field width) and ~6–8 mm tall, and reticle and wafer are scanned through it in opposite directions (the lens inverts the image), the reticle stage at 4× the wafer speed: a 33 mm field with an 8 mm slit takes a ~41 mm wafer-stage travel at ~800 mm/s and a reticle-stage travel at ~3.2 m/s, with accelerations of several g. Scanning means the lens need only be diffraction-limited over the slit, not the full field, and it averages aberrations and illumination non-uniformity along the scan. Synchronization between the two stages must be held to ~1 nm (the average position error over the time a point spends under the slit) while both accelerate, which is why the stages are the most expensive mechatronics in the machine.

### The Projection Lens

The Zeiss (Carl Zeiss SMT, Oberkochen) projection lens for NA 1.35 is **catadioptric** (Starlith 1900i and successors): it uses both lenses and mirrors, because mirrors have no chromatic error and let the design reach NA > 1 without a metre-wide glass element. It is ~1 m tall and ~1 tonne, with ~30–40 fused-silica elements (the largest ~300 mm in diameter), a few CaF₂ elements for chromatic correction and two or more mirrors. Surfaces are polished to a **figure error** (departure from the ideal shape) of < ~0.5 nm **rms** (root mean square: square every deviation, average, take the square root, the standard way to reduce a wavy surface's ups and downs to one number), and the assembled lens has wavefront aberrations of ~1–2 mλ rms: the image-forming wavefront is within one or two thousandths of a wavelength (< ~0.5 nm) of perfect. Fused silica absorbs slightly and its index changes with temperature, so the lens heats during exposure and carries dozens of actuated elements (manipulators that move, tilt or deform elements) plus infrared heating of specific elements to counteract exposure-induced aberrations in real time; the NXT:2100i added a manipulator specifically to match DUV field distortion to the EUV scanners it must overlay with. Lens lifetime is limited by **compaction** and **rarefaction**, the slow densifying or loosening of fused silica under 193 nm irradiation, which changes its index and lets the lens drift over ~10 years.

### Wafer Stages: Dual, Measure-While-Expose

ASML's defining architecture since 2001 is **TWINSCAN**: two identical wafer stages on a common granite base. While one wafer is exposed under the lens, the second sits on the other stage under a separate metrology column, where the machine measures its alignment marks (~30–100 diffraction gratings per wafer in the **scribe lanes**, the ~60–80 µm-wide streets between dies that are later sawn through and where the fab hides its test and alignment structures; an interferometric sensor reads them at multiple colours with ~0.5 nm repeatability) and maps its surface height with a level sensor (optical triangulation at hundreds of thousands of points, producing the height map that drives the stage's z and tilt during exposure). When both finish, the stages swap places (~1 s), and the metrology results are used for the exposure without time penalty: ~300 wph despite ~10 s per wafer of metrology.

Since the NXT generation (~2010), the stages are **magnetically levitated planar motors**: a floating coil-carrying plate over a permanent magnet array, driven in six degrees of freedom with nothing touching. Position is measured by an **encoder grid plate**, a large grating under the lens read by four encoder heads on the stage with sub-nanometer resolution, replacing the laser interferometers of earlier generations, which were sensitive to air temperature along the beam path. The arithmetic for that sensitivity is short: air's refractive index changes by ~1 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin, so a 1 mK change along a 300 mm beam is 300 mm × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 0.3 nm of apparent position error. The whole system sits on active vibration isolation inside a temperature-controlled enclosure at ±1–5 mK.

### Overlay, Focus, and CD Uniformity

**Overlay** is measured on dedicated targets (box-in-box or diffraction-based gratings, Module 13). The scanner corrects it per wafer from its alignment measurements (translation, rotation and magnification per wafer, plus a smooth correction map across each field applied by tilting and bending lens elements), and per lot from feedback of measured overlay ("APC", advanced process control). Specifications: NXT:2100i matched-machine overlay ~1.3 nm, **dedicated-chuck overlay** (both layers printed on the same tool and the same wafer chuck, the best case) ~1 nm. On-product overlay including process effects (wafer distortion from film stress, etch, and **chemical mechanical polishing (CMP)**, the step that flattens each finished layer by polishing it with an abrasive slurry, Module 12) is ~2–3 nm 3σ at 7 nm-class nodes; EUV-generation nodes require ~1.5–2 nm, roughly the state of the art with DUV-EUV cross-matching. The overlay budget is typically ~20–25% of the minimum pitch. The quantity that combines everything is **edge placement error (EPE)**: the distance between where a feature edge was meant to be and where it actually ends up, folding together overlay, CD error and roughness; a 40 nm-pitch multi-patterned layer needs < 8–10 nm total EPE, of which overlay is one contributor.

> **Worked example: an EPE budget at 40 nm pitch.** With 20 nm lines and 20 nm spaces, a via that must land on its line and miss the neighbours has 10 nm to spare on each side. Suppose overlay is 2.5 nm 3σ, half the via's CD variation is 1.5 nm, and line-edge roughness contributes 2 nm. If the three are independent they add in quadrature: √(2.5² + 1.5² + 2²) = √12.5 ≈ 3.5 nm; if they happen to line up, the worst case is 2.5 + 1.5 + 2 = 6 nm. Both fit inside 10 nm, but only if every term holds its spec; let overlay slip to 5 nm and the worst case is 8.5 nm, and the via starts touching the wrong line on the tails of the distribution.

> **Why it matters downstream: overlay becomes contact resistance and shorts.** A via that lands only partly on its line has a smaller contact area and a higher resistance; one that lands too close to the neighbouring line leaks or shorts, and a chip with billions of vias fails on the tails of the distribution, not at the mean. That is why the interconnect design rules of Module 12 wrap an enclosure margin around every via, why that margin is sized from the overlay and CD budgets above, and why the cut and via layers were the first to move to EUV.

**Focus**: with a ~60 nm DOF, the scanner must hold each point of the wafer within ~±20–30 nm of best focus. The level sensor maps the wafer, but the map is corrupted by the wafer's own topography (metal-dense regions, edge roll-off in the outer 3 mm) and by chuck flatness. Focus is verified on product by **scatterometry** (inferring a grating's shape, including its height and sidewall, from the way it scatters light of several wavelengths) or diffraction-based focus targets, and fed back.

**CD uniformity (CDU)** across the wafer, field and slit is the sum of dose uniformity (< 0.3% within the slit), focus, lens aberrations, mask CD error × MEEF, PEB temperature uniformity and develop uniformity. Total CDU at ~40 nm half-pitch is ~1–1.5 nm 3σ, with the scanner's contribution ~0.5 nm. **Dose mapping** (adjusting dose per field and even within the slit from CD-map feedback) and per-field focus corrections compensate systematic track and etch signatures.

> **What can go wrong: scanner failure modes.** *Focus spots*: a particle under the wafer lifts it locally by more than the DOF and one die prints blurred; found by on-product focus metrology and defect inspection, fixed by chuck cleaning and backside rinse. *Alignment-mark damage*: CMP or a deposited film erodes or buries the scribe-lane gratings, so the alignment sensor reads a distorted position; detected as an overlay excursion, fixed by a different mark colour or a more robust mark design. *Lens heating drift*: a lot with a dense pattern heats the lens differently from the previous one, and the first wafers print with a focus and distortion offset; corrected by the lens manipulators using a heating model fed by the illumination and mask transmission. *Immersion bubbles and watermarks*: a droplet left behind by the meniscus or a bubble trapped at a wafer edge prints as a defect; fixed by hood tuning and resist contact angle. *Stage synchronisation errors*: a vibration or a controller fault leaves a smeared image along the scan; caught by the tool's own sensors and by CD-SEM. All are watched by on-product overlay and focus metrology and pushed back to the scanner through APC feedback.

The current tools, as of 2026, are the ASML TWINSCAN NXT:2050i, NXT:2100i (≥295 wph, matched-machine overlay spec ≤1.3 nm) and the NXT:2150i (≥310 wph, MMO ≤1.0 nm, with a new diamond wafer table), plus Nikon's NSR-S636E (2024; ≥280 wph, MMO ≤2.1 nm). Prices for the top ASML immersion tools are on the order of $60–80M (a trade-press estimate; ASML does not publish list prices); a KrF NXT:1470 or an i-line XT:400 is a small fraction of that. The machine occupies ~30 m² of cleanroom plus a sub-fab laser room.

## Process Windows: Bossung Plots and the Focus-Exposure Matrix

Every knob in the previous three sections jitters: the laser dose by a fraction of a percent, the resist thickness by a nanometre, the PEB plate by a tenth of a degree, the wafer height by tens of nanometres, the lens by whatever the last lot heated it to. A layer is manufacturable only if the printed CD survives all of those at once, every field, every wafer, every day. The **process window** is how much room there is, and measuring it is the first thing done with any new layer, resist or mask.

> **Intuition: a blurred edge and a fixed threshold.** The aerial image of a line is not a step but a slope: intensity falls from bright to dark over a few tens of nanometres. The resist, thanks to its quencher, switches at a fixed intensity threshold, so the printed edge sits wherever the slope crosses that threshold. Defocus flattens the slope (the blur spreads the light), so the crossing point moves and the line changes width; a dose change lifts or lowers the whole intensity curve, which also moves the crossing point. The **Bossung plot** is simply CD plotted against focus, one curve per dose. At the right dose the curve is flat across a wide range of focus; at the wrong dose it bends. The flattest curve wins.

The measurement is the **focus-exposure matrix (FEM)**: a wafer exposed with dose stepped along one axis and focus along the other (e.g. 11 focus steps of 20 nm × 9 dose steps of 3%, centred on the dose-to-size from the resist's characteristic curve), measured by CD-SEM and plotted as a Bossung plot. The process window is the rectangle (or ellipse) in the focus-dose plane inside which CD stays within ±10% of target *and* sidewall angle and resist loss stay within spec. Its width is the usable DOF and its height the **exposure latitude (EL)**, typically quoted as "8% EL at 80 nm DOF." In words: the dose may vary by ±4% and the focus by ±40 nm, in any combination, and the CD stays within ±10% of its target. Every source of dose error (laser, illuminator, resist thickness, PEB) and focus error (leveling, wafer flatness, topography) eats into this rectangle.

> **Worked example: an error budget against a 64 nm DOF and 8% EL.** The entries below are representative orders of magnitude, each flagged "~", not a specific tool's data, and every entry is a ± value so that it can be compared with the ±4% and ±32 nm half-widths of the window. *Dose-equivalent errors*: laser dose control ~±0.3%; illumination uniformity across the slit ~±0.3%; resist thickness variation acting through the swing curve ~±1%; PEB plate at ±0.2 °C with a sensitivity of ~2 nm/°C gives ~±0.4 nm of CD, which has to be converted to dose before it can be added to the others: the 8% EL definition above (±4% dose moves a 40 nm CD by ±10%, i.e. ±4 nm) means ~1 nm of CD per 1% of dose at this feature, so ±0.4 nm ≈ ±0.4% dose-equivalent. Added in quadrature that is √(0.3² + 0.3² + 1² + 0.4²) ≈ ±1.2%, and ±2.0% if they line up, against an available ±4%: it fits, but the mask CD error × MEEF and the etch bias, not counted here, take a further slice of what remains. *Focus errors*: leveling-sensor error ~±15 nm; wafer flatness and pattern topography ~±20 nm; chuck flatness and contamination ~±10 nm; lens heating and field curvature ~±10 nm. In quadrature √(15² + 20² + 10² + 10²) ≈ ±29 nm; as a straight sum ±55 nm. Against the ±32 nm half-range of a 64 nm DOF the quadrature sum leaves only ~3 nm of margin, and the straight sum overruns the budget by ~23 nm: the layer works only because the errors are independent and rarely line up, and only if every term holds its spec. This is the arithmetic behind every fab rule in this module: resists at ~100 nm and no thicker, planarizing underlayers, backside cleaning, and a level sensor that maps every field, each of them buying back a few nanometres of that budget.

Every feature type (dense lines, isolated lines, line-ends, contacts) has its own window, and the **overlapping process window** across them all is what SMO and OPC (optical proximity correction, the deliberate pre-distortion of the mask pattern described in the next section) are optimized to maximize. **Iso-dense bias** is the classic complication: a dense grating sends most of its energy into the ±1 orders while an isolated line spreads its energy over all angles, so at the same dose they print at different sizes with different best focus; OPC corrects the mask so both land on target, and the process window is the intersection.

> **What can go wrong: living inside the window.** *Hot spots*: a few pattern configurations on a layer have a smaller window than the rest and fail first when focus or dose drifts; they are hunted by simulation before the mask is made and by inspection on the first wafers. *Wafer-edge window collapse*: edge roll-off, immersion-hood turbulence and resist-thickness gradients in the outer few millimetres shrink the window there, which is why edge dies yield worse. *CD excursions*: a slow drift in dose or PEB shows up at ADI as a CD trend across lots and is corrected by a dose offset; a systematic CD signature along the slit or across the wafer is corrected by dose mapping. The CD-SEMs that feed all of this come mainly from Hitachi High-Tech and KLA, and OPC and SMO are optimised against the measured window, not a nominal image.

## Resolution Enhancement Techniques

By ~2000 the industry accepted that it would print features smaller than the wavelength, and that the mask could no longer be a faithful copy of the design. Three families of tricks emerged, all still in use at every node including EUV.

### Optical Proximity Correction (OPC)

The lens accepts only a limited range of angles, and the fine detail in a pattern lives in the wide-angle diffracted beams, so the lens throws that detail away, like a softened photograph: the image is a low-pass-filtered version of the mask. Corners round, line-ends pull back (~20–40 nm at 193i), and a line's printed width depends on what is next to it, because corners and line-ends are the finest detail there is. **OPC** pre-distorts the mask so that the *printed* pattern matches the design: line-ends are extended and given **hammerheads** (a widened, T-shaped tip that prints as a square end); corners get **serifs** (small squares added at outer corners and cut from inner ones, like the feet on a printed letter); line widths are biased segment by segment depending on the neighborhood; and **sub-resolution assist features (SRAFs)**, lines ~20–30 nm wide (at wafer scale) that are too small to print themselves, are placed next to isolated features so that they diffract like dense ones and share the dense-line process window. Early OPC was rule-based (a lookup table of biases versus spacing); since ~130 nm it is **model-based**: a physically exact model of how the lens forms the image with the actual source shape, lens and polarization, plus a resist model (acid diffusion, develop), simulates the image of every edge, and an iterative loop moves mask edge segments ("fragments" every ~20–50 nm) until the simulated edge lands on the design edge. A full-chip OPC run for a critical 193i layer takes thousands of CPU-hours (Synopsys Proteus, Siemens Calibre, and now GPU-accelerated flows from NVIDIA cuLitho with TSMC and Synopsys), and the result is a mask that looks nothing like the layout, with a data volume of hundreds of gigabytes.

**Inverse lithography (ILT)** goes further: instead of nudging the design's edges, it solves for the mask pattern that produces the best image, with no prior assumption of **Manhattan geometry** (only horizontal and vertical edges, like a city grid); the results are **curvilinear masks**, with SRAFs that are arcs and dots. ILT gives ~10–20% more process window on contact and cut layers but was historically limited by mask-writing time: a **variable-shaped-beam** mask writer exposes the mask one rectangle at a time with an electron beam, so a curve costs thousands of shots. Multi-beam mask writers (IMS Technology, since ~2016) write curvilinear shapes at constant time, and curvilinear ILT went into production for critical 193i and EUV layers around 2020–2023 (D2S, Synopsys, Siemens; multi-beam writers from IMS and NuFlare).

### Phase-Shift Masks (PSM)

> **Intuition: on, off, and upside-down.** A light wave has a height (its **amplitude**) and a timing (its **phase**). Delay one wave by half a cycle and it is upside-down: its crests sit where its troughs were, which is the same as flipping its sign. Two opposite-sign waves overlapping add to zero. A binary mask can only turn light on or off; a phase-shift mask can also flip its sign. Where light from two neighbouring openings would otherwise spill together and fill in the dark space between them, give one opening a flipped sign and the spill cancels instead.

A binary mask modulates only amplitude. If adjacent openings transmit light with a 180° phase difference, the fields cancel between them and the intensity minimum is forced to zero, sharpening the image and roughly doubling the resolution of dense lines. Two flavors:

- **Alternating PSM (alt-PSM)**: alternating clear openings have the quartz etched to a depth that delays the light by half a wavelength. It can reach k1 ~0.25 for gates but creates unwanted dark lines wherever a phase boundary occurs without an absorber, so every design needs a second "trim" exposure and must be phase-assignable: you must be able to alternate 0° and 180° around every loop of adjacent openings, which is impossible when a loop has an odd number of members. It was used for gate layers at 90–45 nm and is now rare.
- **Attenuated PSM (att-PSM, "embedded" PSM)**: the "opaque" absorber is replaced by a thin molybdenum silicide (MoSi) film that transmits ~6% of the light with a 180° phase shift. The weak light through the "dark" region is out of phase with the light through the openings, so it subtracts from the image tails and steepens the edges. **6% MoSi att-PSM is the standard mask type for essentially all critical 193 nm layers**; high-transmission (20–30%) variants are used for contact holes. The MoSi is ~70 nm thick, chosen for both transmission and phase.

> **Worked example: how deep to etch a phase shifter.** Light travelling a depth d through quartz instead of air is delayed, because it moves more slowly in glass: the extra optical path is (n − 1) · d, where n = 1.56 is the refractive index of quartz at 193 nm. A half-wavelength delay (180°) needs (n − 1) · d = λ/2, so d = λ/(2(n − 1)) = 193/(2 × 0.56) ≈ 172 nm. Etch the alternating openings 172 nm deep and their light arrives upside-down relative to their neighbours'; the depth must be held to a few nanometres or the cancellation is incomplete.

### Off-Axis Illumination, Diffraction Orders, and the 1D Layout

Combining a 6% att-PSM, dipole illumination with polarization, SMO, and a high-contrast NTD or PTD resist is how 193i reaches k1 ~0.28. But notice what dipole illumination assumes: all the critical features are lines in one direction at (close to) one pitch. The reason is where the diffraction orders land, and it is worth working through once, because it is the origin of every layout rule at the tightest layers.

> **Worked example: where the orders land in the pupil.** Use the pupil map of the illuminator section: a disc of radius 1, where 1 is the edge of the lens NA. A grating of pitch p displaces its ±1st orders by λ/(p · NA) pupil radii from wherever the illuminating beam sits, along the direction in which the grating repeats. For an 80 nm pitch: 193.4/(80 × 1.35) = 1.79 pupil radii, further than the pupil is wide. With head-on illumination the 0th order sits at the centre and both 1st orders land at 1.79, outside the lens: the grating does not print at all. Now put a dipole pole at σ = 0.9 on the left: the 0th order lands at −0.9, the +1st at −0.9 + 1.79 = +0.89, just inside, and the −1st at −2.69, lost. Two beams, one image, and the right-hand pole does the mirror image. Take a grating of the *same* pitch whose lines run the other way: its orders are displaced along y instead, to (−0.9, ±1.79), at a radius of √(0.9² + 1.79²) = 2.0, far outside; under an x-dipole that grating is invisible and prints as uniform grey. Could a four-pole illuminator serve both orientations? Put a pole at 45° at the same radius, (−0.64, −0.64): the +1st order of the 80 nm grating lands at (1.15, −0.64), radius 1.32, outside. On this simple two-beam model a four-pole source only reaches both orientations at pitches of ~100 nm and above (k1 ≈ 0.35); at the 193i limit there is no illumination that images two orientations at once.

That is why two-dimensional patterns are the problem. A line that turns a corner, or a random-logic layout with **jogs** (short sideways steps in a wire), is in optical terms a superposition of gratings of many pitches in both orientations; its diffraction orders are scattered across the pupil, the dipole collects the ones on its own axis and loses the rest, and the corner rounds while the jog smears. So the industry moved to **1D gridded layouts** for the tightest layers: lines and spaces on a fixed pitch printed with dipole in one exposure, and the desired 2D pattern created by *cutting* those lines with a second exposure (the **cut mask** or **block mask**, explained in the multi-patterning section) whose features are holes or short bars, printed with their own optimized illumination.

The resulting **restricted design rules (RDR)** are what the chip designer actually feels. Each tight metal level runs in one direction only, alternating level by level (M1 vertical, M2 horizontal, and so on), at a single pitch, with no jogs and with every wire starting and ending on the grid. Gates are parallel stripes at one fixed pitch, the **contacted poly pitch**, and a standard cell (the pre-designed logic gate the chip is assembled from, Module 19) is a row of such stripes with unidirectional metal over it; where a wire ends, or where two gates on the same stripe must be separated, is information carried entirely by the cut and block masks. Standard-cell libraries at 20 nm and below are designed this way, the rules are encoded in the foundry's design-rule manual and enforced by the place-and-route and design-rule-check tools (Cadence and Synopsys routers, Siemens Calibre), and it is part of why "design flexibility" shrank even as transistor counts grew: the layout is no longer a drawing but a set of parallel lines plus a list of places to cut them.

> **What can go wrong: when the corrections misbehave.** *OPC hot spots*: a configuration the model got slightly wrong prints with a pinched line or a bridged space; found by full-chip simulation against the process window and then by inspection of the first wafers, fixed by re-running OPC on that region. *SRAFs that print*: an assist feature drawn a few nanometres too wide, or a dose a little high, and the "sub-resolution" bar leaves a **scum line**, a thin ridge of undissolved resist where nothing should be, on the wafer; fixed by narrowing the SRAF or re-optimising the source. *Mask 3D effects*: at high NA the absorber's finite thickness shadows the steep-angle light and shifts the best focus by feature orientation; handled in the OPC model and by thinner absorbers. *Curvilinear data volume*: an ILT mask file can be terabytes and the mask write correspondingly long; handled by multi-beam writers and shape compression. When none of that suffices, the fix is a source re-optimisation (SMO) or, expensively, a mask re-spin.

## Multi-Patterning

Below an 80 nm pitch, 193i needs more than one patterning pass per layer. The three schemes in production use are LELE, SADP and SAQP, with cut/block masks layered on top.

### LELE (Litho-Etch-Litho-Etch)

The idea is the simplest of the three: a pattern too dense for one exposure can be split into two sparser patterns, each printable on its own, provided they can be recombined on the wafer without the second exposure destroying the first. That last clause is the whole difficulty. A resist pattern cannot survive being coated over with more resist, so the first pattern has to be stored in something durable before the second is printed, and then the second has to be placed on the first to within a fraction of the gap between them.

The flow, for a 40 nm-pitch metal layer made from two 80 nm-pitch exposures:

1. **Coloring** (the split): software assigns every feature to mask A or mask B so that no two features on the same mask are closer than the single-exposure limit; adjacent lines alternate A, B, A, B, and each mask sees a relaxed pitch of 2× the final. A feature that is too close to two neighbours which are also too close to each other (an odd-numbered loop of conflicts) cannot be two-colored and is flagged back to the designer as a rule violation.
2. **Hard-mask deposition**: a thin etch-resistant film (titanium nitride or silicon nitride, Module 06) is deposited over the film to be patterned; this is where the composite pattern will be stored.
3. **Litho A and etch A**: coat the tri-layer stack, expose mask A, develop, etch the resist pattern down through the SiARC and SOC into the hard mask, then strip the whole organic stack in a plasma ash. Pattern A now exists only as openings in the hard mask, with nothing organic left on the wafer.
4. **Litho B and etch B**: coat a fresh tri-layer stack over the patterned hard mask. The SOC fills the A openings and planarizes over them, so the resist on top is flat and the A pattern is protected under it; mask B is exposed, aligned to the same scribe-lane marks that mask A used, and etched into the *same* hard mask. During etch B the already-open A regions, exposed again once the SOC is cleared, simply etch slightly deeper into the film beneath, which the etch selectivity must make harmless.
5. **Final etch**: the hard mask, now carrying A and B together at 40 nm pitch, masks the etch of the target film, and is then removed.

The catch is that the spacing between a feature from mask A and one from mask B depends on the *overlay* between the two exposures, which self-alignment cannot help. Concretely: two 40 nm lines meant to be 40 nm apart, printed on different masks with a 3 nm overlay error, end up 37 nm or 43 nm apart, a ±7.5% error in that space before any CD error is counted; across a wafer the A-to-B spaces carry an extra ~3–5 nm of variation from overlay alone. The two exposures also have different CD signatures, and the A openings see two hard-mask etches while the B openings see one, so the layer has two populations of lines with slightly different widths and profiles. The A-to-B overlay is measured on dedicated targets at the after-develop inspection of litho B, before etch B, so a misaligned B exposure can still be stripped and reworked; a CD mismatch between the populations shows up at CD-SEM as a bimodal distribution and is corrected by biasing one mask's dose.

LELE runs on the same scanners, tracks and etchers as single patterning; its cost is a second litho pass, a second tri-layer coat and a second hard-mask etch and strip per layer (the per-pass numbers are in the worked example below), and the coloring is done by the design and OPC tools (Synopsys, Siemens Calibre, Cadence). LELE handles arbitrary 2D patterns, and triple patterning (LELELE) was reportedly used for the tightest 10 nm-class metal and contact layers at Intel and TSMC; it remains the workhorse for contact, via and cut layers, where the pattern is 2D and the self-aligned schemes below cannot be used.

### SADP (Self-Aligned Double Patterning, "sidewall image transfer")

> **Intuition: paint the fence posts, then pull them out.** Print a row of fence posts. Paint both sides of every post with a coat of exactly known thickness, then pull the posts out. What is left standing is twice as many walls as there were posts, each wall exactly as thick as the paint coat, and the paint thickness is set by the tin, not by how well you placed the posts. That is SADP: the "paint" is a film deposited by ALD, and its thickness, not the lithography, sets the final line width.

Instead of a second exposure, the second set of features is created by *deposition*. The vocabulary, refreshed: **ALD** (atomic layer deposition, Module 06) lays down one atomic layer per gas pulse, so thickness is set by counting pulses; a **conformal** film has the same thickness on top, sides and bottom of whatever it coats; an **anisotropic** etch removes material only downward; a **sacrificial** material is there only to be removed later; a **hard mask** is a film harder than resist that carries the pattern into the substrate.

1. Print **mandrels** (lines at the relaxed pitch P, e.g. 80 nm pitch printed as 40 nm lines, then narrowed by a plasma **etch trim** to ~P/4 = 20 nm width, since litho cannot print 20 nm lines directly) in a sacrificial material such as amorphous (non-crystalline) carbon or amorphous silicon (a-Si) on top of the hard mask.
2. Deposit a **conformal spacer** by ALD (SiO₂ or SiN, Module 06), with thickness equal to the desired final line width, e.g. 20 nm, coating the mandrel top and both sidewalls.
3. **Anisotropic etch-back** of the spacer: removes it from horizontal surfaces (mandrel tops and the floor), leaving a spacer on each mandrel sidewall.
4. **Mandrel pull**: selectively remove the mandrel (e.g. O₂ ash for carbon, or a wet etch for a-Si), leaving free-standing spacers.
5. Use the spacers as the hard mask to etch the target film.

Each mandrel yields two spacers, so the pitch is halved: an 80 nm mandrel pitch gives a 40 nm final pitch with 20 nm lines. The spacer width is set by ALD thickness (controllable to < 0.5 nm), *not* by lithography, so the CD uniformity of SADP lines is excellent, and the two spacers from one mandrel are perfectly aligned, so there is no overlay contribution to the A-B spacing. The costs: the space between spacers alternates between the "mandrel space" (set by the mandrel CD, litho-controlled) and the "gap space" (pitch minus mandrel minus 2 × spacer), giving **pitch walking** if the mandrel CD drifts; every feature is a closed loop (a spacer wraps around the end of a mandrel), so line-ends must be defined with a separate **cut mask**; and the process is only for 1D lines at a single pitch. SADP with cuts is the standard for the fins of FinFETs (the transistor whose channel is a thin vertical fin of silicon, Module 11) at 22/16/14 nm and for the tightest metal layers at 10/7 nm. The ALD and spacer-etch tools come from Lam Research, TEL and Applied Materials (Module 06, Module 09).

> **Worked example: pitch walking.** At an 80 nm mandrel pitch with 20 nm spacers and a mandrel trimmed to 20 nm, the spaces are 20 nm (mandrel space) and 80 − 20 − 2 × 20 = 20 nm (gap space): 20/20, perfect. Let the mandrel drift to 22 nm: the mandrel space is now 22 nm and the gap space 80 − 22 − 40 = 18 nm, so alternate spaces differ by 4 nm on a layer whose whole line width is 20 nm. The lines themselves are still exactly 20 nm; it is the *spaces* that walk, and the vias that must land in them notice.

### SAQP (Self-Aligned Quadruple Patterning)

Do it twice: the spacers from the first SADP become the mandrels for a second spacer deposition. Starting from an 80 nm litho pitch (a "mandrel 1" pitch at the 193i limit), SADP gives 40 nm and a second spacer cycle gives 20 nm pitch with 10 nm lines. After step 4 of the SADP flow (mandrel 1 pulled, spacer 1 standing free) the second cycle is:

6. **Spacer 1 becomes mandrel 2**: no lithography; the free-standing spacer-1 lines, at half the litho pitch, are the new posts.
7. **Spacer 2 ALD**: a second conformal film, of a *different* material from spacer 1 (an oxide over a nitride, or an oxide over a-Si) so that the two can later be etched selectively, with thickness equal to the final line width.
8. **Etch-back** of spacer 2 from the horizontal surfaces, leaving it on both sidewalls of every spacer-1 line.
9. **Spacer 1 pull**: remove the spacer-1 material selectively, leaving spacer 2 as free-standing lines at a quarter of the litho pitch.
10. Spacer 2 is the hard mask for the final etch.

The widths are not free choices: for all four spaces to come out equal they must be mandrel 1 = 3P/8, spacer 1 = P/8 and spacer 2 = P/8 for a litho pitch P: at P = 80 nm that is a 30 nm mandrel (a 40 nm printed line trimmed by 10 nm), a 10 nm first spacer and a 10 nm second spacer, giving four 10 nm lines and four 10 nm spaces in every 80 nm.

> **Worked example: the four flavors of space.** Take that 80 nm design and let the two things that can drift, drift: the mandrel-1 CD comes out 2 nm too wide (32 nm instead of 30) and spacer 1 comes out 0.5 nm too thick (10.5 nm instead of 10). Follow the geometry through both cycles. The two spaces that sit where spacer 1 stood before it was pulled are exactly the spacer-1 thickness, 10.5 nm. The space that sits in the old gap between two mandrels is squeezed by both errors: 10 − 2 − 2 × 0.5 = 7 nm. The space over the core of the old mandrel is widened by the mandrel error: 10 + 2 = 12 nm. All four lines are still exactly 10 nm, because spacer 2 set them. So a 2 nm lithography error and a 0.5 nm deposition error together produce spaces of 7, 10.5, 10.5 and 12 nm on a layer whose nominal space is 10 nm, in a repeating pattern of four; the 7 nm space is where a via bridges to the wrong line, and it is three lines away from the 12 nm space where a via has the most room. In SADP a mandrel error walked *two* spaces; here it walks four, and the spacer-1 error, which SADP did not have, walks them again.

SAQP is how the DUV-only 7 nm-class nodes (TSMC N7 in its original 2018 form, Intel's 10 nm) reached their tightest layers: fins at ~30–34 nm pitch and the lowest metal at ~36–40 nm pitch, both from a single ~120–160 nm-pitch exposure divided by four; the 20 nm final pitch is demonstrated in R&D, but production nodes used the extra margin, and the worked example shows why. SAQP's line-width control is even better than SADP's (both line widths are set by ALD), but the spaces come in the four flavors above, pitch walking compounds, and two further costs follow from the mechanism. *Defectivity*: the spacer-1 lines that serve as mandrel 2 are only ~10 nm wide and several times taller, so they bend under the stress of the spacer-2 film (**line wiggling**); the spacer etch-back leaves thin **stringers** of residue wherever a mandrel ended; an incompletely pulled mandrel or spacer leaves a foot that becomes a bridge between two lines; and every one of the added deposition and etch steps is another chance for a particle. *Cycle time*: between the single exposure and the final etch the wafer makes ~15 separate tool visits (two ALD runs, two etch-backs, two pulls, cleans and metrology after each), each with its own processing time and queue, which is how one layer comes to take several days of fab time.

### Cut and Block Masks

SADP and SAQP produce continuous loops of identical lines, and a circuit is not that: wires end, and two gates on one stripe must be separate devices. All of that two-dimensional information has to be added by one more exposure, and there are two ways to add it, which differ in what physically happens to the lines.

- A **cut mask** removes line segments. After the spacers are formed (and usually after they have been transferred into the hard mask), a fresh tri-layer stack is coated over the lines and a resist pattern of small openings is printed on top, each opening straddling one line where it must end; the line segments under the openings are etched away and the resist is stripped. The loops are broken and every opening becomes a pair of line-ends. The openings are contact-hole-sized features, which is why the cut layer is printed with the bright-field-mask-plus-NTD image described in the Development section.
- A **block mask** prevents line segments from being formed. It sits just before the final trench etch: resist bars are printed and left standing over the places where no trench may form, so the hard-mask openings beneath them are not transferred into the film, and the metal line simply does not exist there. A block adds material (dielectric stays) where a cut removes it, and which one a flow uses depends on whether the spacers define the metal lines or the gaps between them.

A variant, **cut-first**, makes some of the cuts in the mandrels before the spacer is deposited, where the cut litho faces the relaxed mandrel pitch rather than the final one; the spacer then wraps round each cut mandrel end, so a cut-last exposure is usually still needed to open the loops, and the flow trades an easier cut layer for a more complicated mandrel etch.

Either way the cut is 2D and small, so it is the hardest 193i feature. At a 40 nm metal pitch a cut must land on one 20 nm line and not its two neighbours, 20 nm away on each side, with an edge-placement error < ~8 nm including overlay, CD and LER (the EPE budget worked in the scanner section); as a contact-sized feature it also carries a MEEF of 2–4, so a 1 nm mask error (at wafer scale) is a 2–4 nm cut error. Two cuts on adjacent lines that are closer than the single-exposure limit cannot share a mask, so at 7 nm the cuts themselves needed LELE or LELELE (the "2 colors" in the SAQP worked example below). The cut and block layers were the first to move to EUV (TSMC N7+, 2019) precisely because a single EUV exposure replaces two or three 193i cut exposures with all their overlay budget.

> **What can go wrong: cuts that miss.** *Cut lands partly on the neighbour*: it nicks the adjacent line, raising its resistance or, if the nick is deep, opening it; the cause is overlay or a cut printed too wide. *Cut too small or under-dosed*: the line is not fully severed and two nets that should be separate stay shorted, a fault that inspection rarely sees and electrical test (Module 14) finds. *Cut too large or line-end rounding*: the line-end pulls back further than designed and the via meant to land on it misses. *Block bar too thin or collapsed*: a trench forms where none was allowed and two lines merge. All of them are edge-placement failures, which is why the cut layer's overlay and CD are measured at after-develop inspection, while the cut resist can still be stripped and reworked, and why the EPE budget, not the resolution, is what set the limit of DUV multi-patterning.

> **Worked example: process steps and cost, SAQP versus single EUV exposure, for one tight metal layer (~40 nm pitch).** The figures below are illustrative estimates, not foundry data.
> **SAQP route** (one representative flow; real ones vary):
> - Mandrel litho: tri-layer coat (3 coats + 3 bakes), 193i exposure, PEB, develop, ADI metrology: ~8 track/scanner steps, 1 exposure.
> - Mandrel etch (SiARC open, SOC open, mandrel etch, trim), strip: ~4 etch steps.
> - Spacer 1 ALD, spacer etch-back, mandrel 1 pull, clean: 4 steps.
> - Spacer 2 ALD, spacer etch-back, spacer 1 pull, clean: 4 steps.
> - Cut/block litho: because the cuts need ~2 colors at this pitch, 2 × (coat, expose, PEB, develop, etch, strip): ~12 steps, 2 exposures.
> - Final hard-mask and metal-trench etch: 2–3 steps.
> - Metrology after every etch and litho: ~6–8 steps.
> Roughly **40–45 process steps and 3 DUV exposures**, ~3–5 days of fab cycle time, five hard-mask/spacer depositions, and an edge-placement budget that must absorb the overlay of 3 exposures plus 2 ALD-defined CDs.
> **EUV route**: tri-layer coat (3 + 3), EUV exposure, PEB, develop, ADI, hard-mask open, trench etch, strip, metrology: ~**15 steps and 1 exposure**, ~1 day cycle time, and the line-ends come out of the exposure directly (or with one simple cut).
> **Cost**, as a rough depreciation-per-pass estimate: NXT:2100i at ~$70M over 5 years, ~275 wph actual × 7,000 productive hours/year ≈ 1.9M wafer-passes/year → ~$7 per pass in scanner depreciation; add track, resist and underlayers (~$10–20 for a tri-layer stack), metrology and mask amortization, and a 193i pass is on the order of $30–50. An NXE:3800E (ASML's current standard-NA EUV scanner, Module 08) at ~$200M over 5 years, ~150 wph actual × 7,000 h ≈ 1.05M passes/year → ~$38 per pass in depreciation alone, and an all-in EUV pass on the order of $100–150 (higher power, ~30–50 kW of electricity for the source, pellicle-less mask handling, more expensive resist and masks). So 3 DUV passes (~$100–150) plus ~10 extra deposition/etch/clean steps at ~$5–15 each (~$80–120), in total ~$200–250, versus ~$120–170 for the EUV route. The EUV route wins on cost per layer by ~30–40% *and* on cycle time, yield (fewer steps) and design freedom; this is the calculation that justified TSMC's N7+ and N5 EUV insertion. It does not favor EUV for layers that need only one or two DUV passes, which is why most layers are still DUV.

### The Cost and Step Explosion

A 28 nm process needed ~40–50 masks and ~1 litho pass per critical layer. TSMC N7 (2018, DUV-only) required ~75–80 masks and, because of multi-patterning, well over 100 litho exposures per wafer. This is the "step explosion": wafer cost per node rose ~1.3–1.5× per generation even as density doubled, and the per-transistor cost improvement fell from ~30% per node toward ~10–15%. Those two ratios are not separate facts; they follow from the per-pass numbers above.

> **Worked example: the step explosion in per-pass numbers.** Use the estimates from the SAQP-versus-EUV example: ~$30–50 all-in for a 193i pass, ~$5–15 for each extra deposition, etch or clean step. At 28 nm, ~45 masks at one pass each is ~45 passes, call it ~$1,800 of patterning per wafer. At DUV-only N7, ~100 passes is ~$4,000, plus ~10 extra spacer, etch and clean steps for each of the ~6–10 self-aligned or LELE-patterned layers, another ~$900: ~$4,900, about 2.7× the 28 nm figure. Three to four node generations separate the two (28 → 20 → 16 → 10 → 7), and 1.3–1.5× compounded over three to four generations is 2.2–5×, so the patterning arithmetic alone lands in the quoted range. The per-transistor consequence is one line more: if a node doubles density and raises wafer cost 1.3×, cost per transistor falls to 1.3/2 = 65%, a ~35% improvement; if it delivers the ~1.6–1.8× density gain of TSMC's public N7 → N5 → N3 figures and costs 1.5× more, it falls only to 1.5/1.7 ≈ 88%, a ~12% improvement. That is the whole economic case for EUV: not that it prints smaller, but that it takes ~25–30 steps and two exposures back out of a layer.

N5 was the first node in TSMC's history with *fewer* masks than its predecessor (~70 by analyst reconstruction, versus ~78 for N7) because up to 14 EUV layers replaced at least 4× that many DUV exposures at cut, contact, via and metal steps (TSMC, IEDM 2019). N3 uses ~20–25 EUV layers; N2 a similar or slightly higher number (exact counts are not disclosed). The remaining ~55–65 mask layers are still 193i, KrF and i-line: upper metals (pitches ≥ 80 nm need only one 193i exposure), implants, wells, pads.

## The Tool Market and Geography

**ASML** (Veldhoven, Netherlands) holds ~90% of the lithography market by revenue, 100% of EUV, and ~85–90% of ArF immersion (trade-press estimates; ASML does not report share). Its 2025 revenue was €32.7B, of which €12.0B was DUV systems (279 systems, 47% of them immersion), €11.6B EUV systems (48 systems), and €8.2B installed-base service and upgrades (ASML 2025 Annual Report). A notable feature of ASML's DUV business is that a very large share of unit shipments go to China: China was 29% of ASML's sales in 2023, 36% in 2024 (peaking at 49% in individual quarters), and 33% in 2025 (ASML annual reports), almost entirely DUV (NXT:1980i-class and older immersion plus KrF/i-line tools), because Chinese fabs (SMIC, Hua Hong, CXMT, YMTC) are barred from EUV and buy DUV instead, and because the Dutch export rules (September 2023, extended September 2024 to the NXT:1970i and NXT:1980i) require licenses only for the more advanced immersion models (NXT:1970i and above, with the NXT:2000i and above effectively unavailable to China) while the rest remain freely shippable. As of the latest public reporting (2025), SMIC's 7 nm-class and 5 nm-class processes, used for Huawei's Kirin and Ascend chips, are pure DUV multi-patterning (SAQP) exactly as described above, with correspondingly reported low yields and high cost.

**Nikon** (Japan) is ~5–8% of the market (trade-press estimate), with ArF immersion (NSR-S635E, 2018, and NSR-S636E, 2024; NA 1.35, ≥280 wph) sold mostly to Intel and to memory makers, and a larger KrF/i-line business. **Canon** (Japan) is ~5% by revenue (trade-press estimate) but ships many i-line and KrF tools (FPA-6300 series), and is pursuing **nanoimprint lithography (NIL)**: a stamp pressed into liquid resist has no diffraction limit, because no light forms the image; its limits are defects and the life of the template. The FPA-1200NZ2C, announced in October 2023 and first delivered in September 2024 to the Texas Institute for Electronics, presses a quartz template with ~14 nm patterns (equivalent to the "5 nm node" in Canon's marketing) into a liquid resist, which is UV-cured, transferring the pattern without any projection optics. Its throughput, defectivity and template lifetime have kept it out of leading-edge logic; the realistic targets are NAND and specialty devices. Chinese domestic lithography (SMEE, Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment, with a 90 nm-class ArF tool and claims of 28 nm immersion, per 2025 reporting) is many years behind ASML. Sub-suppliers concentrate the risk further: Zeiss SMT (lenses, ~100% for ASML), Cymer and Gigaphoton (lasers, a duopoly), and TEL/SCREEN (tracks).

Photoresist itself is a Japanese-dominated market (JSR, now owned by the Japan Investment Corporation; Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK); Shin-Etsu Chemical; Fujifilm; Sumitomo Chemical) with DuPont and Merck as the main non-Japanese suppliers; Japan holds ~90% of ArF and EUV resist supply, which it used as leverage in its 2019 export dispute with Korea (Module 04).

## Summary

- Lithography is the only step that creates geometry; each of the ~60–80 layers on a chip needs a resist coat, an exposure, a develop and a check, and spends ~1 hour in the litho cell, most of it queueing.
- Resolution = k1·λ/NA. With 193 nm light, water immersion (NA 1.35) and a production k1 ≈ 0.28, the single-exposure limit is ~40 nm half-pitch (~76–80 nm pitch); k1 = 0.25 is the two-beam floor, because an image needs at least two diffracted beams to interfere.
- Depth of focus = k2·λ/NA² ≈ 60–65 nm, which forces ~100 nm resists, planarizing tri-layer underlayers and per-field wafer levelling.
- A chemically amplified resist turns one photon into hundreds of solubility-switching reactions during the post-exposure bake; PEB temperature, delay and acid diffusion (5–20 nm) set CD and roughness, so they are controlled to 0.1 °C and to the second.
- Resolution, line-edge roughness and dose trade against each other (RLS); at 30 mJ/cm² a 20 × 20 × 100 nm resist volume holds only ~400 acid molecules, so LER of 3–5 nm 3σ is a statistical floor and halving it would cost 4× the dose.
- The track (TEL, SCREEN) runs HMDS, BARC or tri-layer coats, spin coat (t ∝ 1/√rpm), bakes, PEB and 0.26 N TMAH puddle develop with deterministic timing; capillary forces of ~36 atm during rinse limit the resist aspect ratio to ~2.5.
- The scanner follows the light: a line-narrowed ArF excimer laser (60–90 W, 0.3 pm), a programmable off-axis illuminator with polarization, a 4× reticle scanned at 3.2 m/s through a 26 × 8 mm slit, a catadioptric Zeiss lens with ~1–2 mλ aberrations, and twin magnetically levitated stages measuring one wafer while exposing the other; ~12 s per wafer, ~1 nm matched-machine overlay.
- Water immersion beat 157 nm because it raised NA from 0.93 to 1.35 with existing glass, masks and resists, while 157 nm died on CaF₂ birefringence, pellicles and resists.
- Every layer must survive a process window of dose (~8% exposure latitude) and focus (~60–80 nm) errors; the focus-exposure matrix and the Bossung plot are how it is measured, and an honest error budget consumes most of it.
- OPC/ILT, 6% MoSi attenuated phase-shift masks, source-mask optimization and 1D gridded layouts with cut masks are how k1 reaches 0.28; the mask no longer looks like the circuit.
- Below ~80 nm pitch, LELE, SADP and SAQP multiply the pattern; SADP/SAQP set CD by ALD spacer thickness, produce closed loops needing cut masks, and cost 40+ steps and days per layer, which is what justified EUV for the ~15–25 tightest layers.
- ASML holds ~90% of the litho market and all of EUV; DUV remains the majority of layers and the largest part of ASML's system revenue, with China its largest DUV customer; the tracks, lasers, lenses and resists come from a handful of Japanese, German and US suppliers.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The resolution limit of 193 nm lithography is 193 nm."** → With NA 1.35 immersion and k1 ~0.28, single-exposure resolution is ~40 nm half-pitch, and multi-patterning reaches ~30–40 nm pitch in production and 10 nm lines at 20 nm pitch in R&D. The wavelength sets the scale, not the limit.
- **"EUV replaced DUV."** → EUV took ~15–25 of ~80 mask layers at N5–N2. The majority of exposures on a leading-edge wafer, and the overwhelming majority across the industry, are still 193i, KrF and i-line, and in 2025 ASML still shipped 279 DUV systems worth €12.0B, more than its EUV system sales.
- **"Exposure is where the resist pattern is formed."** → For chemically amplified resists, exposure only creates a sparse latent image of acid; the solubility switch happens during the post-exposure bake, which is why PEB temperature and delay are among the tightest-controlled parameters in the fab.
- **"Higher NA is always better."** → DOF scales as 1/NA², so the NA 1.35 lens has a total focus budget of ~60 nm. Every increase in NA has required thinner resists, planarizing underlayers and better wafer leveling; there are layers (thick implant masks, deep-topography layers) that are deliberately printed on low-NA i-line or KrF tools.
- **"Immersion means the wafer is dunked in water."** → Only a millimetre-scale meniscus of water sits between the last lens element and the wafer, held in place by an immersion hood and dragged across the wafer at up to ~800 mm/s; the rest of the wafer is dry.
- **"Multi-patterning just means exposing twice."** → SADP/SAQP define the final CD by ALD spacer thickness, not by lithography, and produce closed loops that need cut masks. It is a deposition-and-etch process with lithography as one input, taking dozens of process steps and days of cycle time per layer.
- **"The mask is a picture of the circuit."** → After OPC, SRAFs and ILT, a critical-layer mask is a distorted, decorated pattern (increasingly curvilinear) whose printed image, not its own shape, matches the design.
- **"A 3 nm node has 3 nm features."** → Node names are marketing labels (Module 00). The tightest pitches at a 3 nm-class node are ~40–50 nm for the lowest metals and ~24 nm half-pitch for the gate, printed with EUV and DUV; nothing on the wafer is 3 nm wide.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Lithography consumes the films deposited in Module 06 (the oxide, nitride, polysilicon, amorphous carbon and metal layers that need patterning), the photoresists, underlayers, developers and neon/fluorine gases covered in Module 04, and the OPC-decorated photomasks written by mask shops from the design's layout file, in the **GDSII** or **OASIS** format, the industry's two standard file formats for chip geometry (Module 04, Module 19). Its output is a patterned resist or hard-mask stack on every one of the ~80 layers of a leading-edge wafer, which is immediately consumed by the plasma etch step of Module 09 (and, for implant layers, by the ion implanter of Module 10). The scanners come from ASML/Nikon, the tracks from TEL/SCREEN, the lenses from Zeiss, the lasers from Cymer/Gigaphoton, and the chemistry from a handful of Japanese companies; the litho cells are the single largest capital line item in a fab (~25–35% of tool spend, an industry estimate that varies by fab type), and their throughput sets the fab's wafer-start capacity. Module 08 continues the story with the layers where even SAQP was not enough: 13.5 nm EUV.
