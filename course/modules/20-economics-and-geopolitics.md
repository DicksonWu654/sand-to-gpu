# Module 20: Economics, Geography and Geopolitics of the Chain

The earlier modules followed how chips are made. This one asks why that physical chain produces high prices, shortages and concentrated economic power. Several critical steps have only one major supplier or an **oligopoly**, a market with only a handful of sellers. Factories cost $20–30 billion, and equipment can **depreciate** over five years: accounting spreads the purchase over its useful life, so a $20 billion fab becomes a ~$3–4 billion-a-year expense whether or not it runs. Geography adds another concentration: 90% of the most advanced output comes from an island 130 km from a country that claims it.

These constraints connect decisions that can initially seem unrelated. The price of a 2 nm wafer, the size of a **hyperscaler**'s **capital expenditure (capex)** budget (hyperscalers are Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta, the operators of the world's largest data centers; capex is money spent on long-lived assets such as buildings and tools), the wording of a **Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS)** rule (the US Commerce Department office that writes export controls), and the **yield** (the fraction of dies on a wafer that work) of the 7 nm process at **SMIC** (Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation, China's largest foundry) are all coupled through the same set of balance sheets, and you cannot understand any one of them without the others.

Two warnings before the numbers. First, this is the module most exposed to time: the industry's revenue, policy and capacity figures moved faster in 2024–2026 than in any period since the 1990s, so every recent figure carries an "as of" date. Second, the industry publishes fewer hard numbers than it appears to. Market shares, wafer prices, and yields are almost all estimates by analysts (TrendForce, SemiAnalysis, IBS, TechInsights) triangulated from company filings; where a number below is an estimate it is marked "~" and you should treat a range as the honest answer.

By the end you should be able to compute a fab's break-even wafer price from its capex and explain why utilization, not list price, sets its margin; explain why the cost of a transistor stopped falling around 28 nm; compute the export-control threshold a GPU is measured against and say why the H20 exists; explain why a 5 nm-class chip is possible on DUV-only lithography but uneconomic; and name the single points of failure that would take longest to replace.

## Before you start

- **Node names** (Modules 00, 11): "7 nm", "N2", "18A" label a process generation, not a measured length. Each new node raises transistor density ~1.5–1.7× and, since ~2012, raises the wafer price by about as much.
- **Wafers, dies, yield and capacity** (Modules 02–03, 13): chips are printed on 300 mm silicon discs (~70,000 mm² usable); a **die** is one chip cut from the wafer; **yield** is the fraction of dies that work; capacity is counted in **wafer starts per month (wspm)**, the blank wafers a fab begins processing each month.
- **Mask layers and patterning** (Modules 07–08): a chip is ~40–80 patterned layers, each needing a lithography exposure plus deposition and etch. **Deep ultraviolet (DUV)** lithography uses 193 nm light and needs 2–4 exposures for the finest layers; **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** uses 13.5 nm light and needs one. EUV access decides both cost per wafer and who can make 5 nm-class chips.
- **Transistor generations** (Module 11): **planar** (flat, to 28 nm), **FinFET** (a fin-shaped channel, 16 nm to 3 nm), and **gate-all-around (GAA)** or **nanosheet** (the gate wraps stacked sheet channels; 2 nm, Intel 18A, Samsung SF3). Export rules and node economics key on these names.
- **Front end and back end** (Modules 05, 16): the **front end** is wafer processing inside the fab; the **back end** is dicing, packaging and test. **Wafer fab equipment (WFE)** is the market for front-end tools.
- **Memory types** (Module 15): **DRAM** is main memory and a commodity; **NAND** is flash storage; **SRAM** is on-chip cache built from ordinary logic transistors; **high bandwidth memory (HBM)** is a stack of DRAM dies wired vertically with **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, copper-filled holes through each die, placed beside a GPU in the same package.
- **Advanced packaging** (Module 17): **CoWoS** (chip-on-wafer-on-substrate) is TSMC's method of placing GPU dies and HBM stacks on a silicon **interposer**, a wiring layer built on its own wafer; it has been the AI supply bottleneck since 2023.
- **Industry roles** (Module 00): **fabless** firms design only; **foundries** make wafers for others; **integrated device manufacturers (IDMs)** design and make; **OSATs** (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test) package and test for others; **EDA** (electronic design automation) is the design software.
- **Four accounting words** defined in the next section and used throughout: capex, depreciation, gross margin, utilization.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## The Economics Toolkit: Five Ideas That Explain the Industry

Almost everything in this module, from why TSMC's margin moves with its loading to why the tool market crashes harder than the chip market, follows from five ideas.

### Idea 1: fixed cost and depreciation

**Capital expenditure (capex)** is money spent on assets that last years: a fab building, a scanner, a wet bench. Accountants **depreciate** it, spreading the cost over the asset's useful life as a yearly expense. **Straight-line** depreciation charges an equal amount each year; a **fully depreciated** asset has had its whole cost charged to past years and sits in the accounts at zero, even if it still runs. TSMC depreciates equipment over 5 years and buildings over ~20. For a $20 billion fab with 80% of the money in tools: $20 B × 0.8 ÷ 5 + $20 B × 0.2 ÷ 20 = $3.2 B + $0.2 B = ~$3.4 billion a year, due whether the fab runs flat out or stands empty. Depreciation is an accounting expense, not a fresh payment to the tool supplier each year. The purchase and its financing determine cash payments; depreciation determines how its cost is allocated to accounting periods. A leading-edge fab's largest cost is fixed before the first wafer starts.

### Idea 2: utilization and operating leverage

**Utilization** is wafers actually started divided by the number the fab could start in the same period. When utilization falls, the fixed depreciation expense is spread over fewer wafers. Each wafer that is made must therefore cover a larger share of that expense. Cost per wafer rises, and profit can swing far more than production volume. That sensitivity of profit to volume is **operating leverage**.

> **Intuition:** A fab is an airline. The aircraft lease and crew are paid whether the plane flies full or half empty, and one more passenger costs a meal and a little fuel, so an airline at 90% load makes money and one at 60% loses money on the same fares, and it sells the last seats cheaply rather than fly them empty. Depreciation and staff are the aircraft; a wafer's chemicals and gases are the meal. Foundries discount old nodes for the same reason, and their margin moves with a few points of loading. The analogy breaks in one place: airlines can park planes in a desert; a fab's tools keep depreciating and its process drifts if it stops.

### Idea 3: gross margin

**Gross margin** is revenue minus the direct cost of making the product, as a share of revenue. At 60%, $0.40 of each sales dollar went to making the chip and $0.60 is left for research, sales, overhead and profit. Two very different fabs can show the same margin: Texas Instruments earns ~60% on analog chips from 20-year-old, fully depreciated fabs (no capital cost left in the wafer), and TSMC earns ~59% on brand-new 3 nm fabs by charging $18,000–20,000 for a wafer whose depreciation is ~$8,000. One has no fixed cost; the other has a price high enough to cover a huge one.

### Idea 4: qualification and switching cost

**Qualification** is the months of test wafers needed to prove that a tool, material, software version or fab site gives in-spec results in a particular process. Changing a qualified supplier means re-running that proof, at a cost in time, wafers and yield risk, so almost every supplier in this chain is a de-facto monopoly for the slot it occupies. Re-qualification is the moat behind EDA vendors, equipment makers and materials suppliers, and it is why margins are high in those layers and low where qualification is cheap, such as commodity packaging.

### Idea 5: the capacity lag and the cycle

A fab decided today starts wafers in 2–3 years, so capacity is sized against a forecast that far out, and forecasts are wrong in both directions: the industry alternates between shortages (2021, the leading edge in 2024–26) and gluts (2009, 2019, 2023). Tool makers feel the swing harder than chipmakers, because tools are bought for expected growth, not current sales.

> **Intuition:** The growth-order trap. A fab's tool orders are new capacity for expected growth plus replacements for worn-out tools. A fab with 100 units of capacity that expects 10% growth and replaces 5% of its tools a year orders 10 units of growth tools plus 5 of replacement: 15. Now the forecast drops from 10% growth to 0%. Chip sales have not fallen at all, but the growth orders vanish: 15 units of orders become 5, a 67% drop. This is why wafer fab equipment is the most cyclical line in the chain, why ASML's and Lam's order books fall by more than their customers' capex cuts, and why the same amplification runs in reverse in a boom.

## The Money: Size and Shape of the Industry

Every other number in this module is a fraction of one line: revenue from selling chips. Equipment, materials, design software and packaging all sell to the chipmakers, so the size of that line sets their size and its swings set their swings.

The **World Semiconductor Trade Statistics (WSTS)** consortium, which aggregates company-reported sales through the **Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA)**, the US trade body, put 2024 global chip sales at ~$631 billion, up ~19% on 2023. The 2025 figure came in at ~$792 billion (+25.6%; the autumn 2025 forecast had been $772 billion), and WSTS's Spring 2026 forecast (May 2026) projected an unprecedented ~$1.51 trillion for 2026 (+90%), driven overwhelmingly by memory prices, with the memory segment alone forecast above $800 billion (up ~250%). The 2026 figure is a forecast, not an actual: it will be revised in the autumn 2026 release, and it is a price-driven high-water mark rather than a structural level, since unit shipments rose far less than dollars and memory has a long habit of giving back price gains. (All 2026 figures as of mid-2026.)

Three definitions before the table. **SEMI** is the equipment-and-materials trade association whose surveys give the tool and materials lines; **wafer fab equipment (WFE)** is the **front-end** subset of equipment, the tools that process wafers inside the fab (Modules 05–13), as opposed to **back-end** packaging and test tools (Modules 14–18); **semiconductor IP** (intellectual property) is pre-designed circuit blocks, such as a CPU core or a memory interface, that designers license rather than build.

> **Intuition:** The chain as nested fractions. Of every $100 of chips sold in 2025, ~$17 bought equipment, ~$9 bought materials, and ~$2–3 licensed design software and IP. These are descriptive ratios for the selected year, not fixed input shares. Equipment purchases, material consumption, inventories and selling prices can move on different schedules, so a chip-revenue forecast alone does not determine supplier revenue. What is not stable is the timing: because tools are bought ahead of demand (Idea 5), a 1% change in expected chip growth can move tool orders by ~5% or more, and when chipmakers cut capex by 20%, ASML's and Lam's order books fall by more than that. EDA, at ~2–3% of chip revenue, is small in dollars and enormous in leverage: no chip at any node is designed without it.

By 2025–26 logic and memory had swollen with AI accelerators and HBM while analog, MCU and discrete lines (automotive, industrial) were flat to down: a leading-edge AI economy growing 30–50% a year sitting on top of a mature-node economy growing a few percent a year, roughly in line with the wider economy.

## The Layers and Who Owns Them

The industry organizes itself into layers that map onto the modules of this course, and in each layer one toolkit idea (fixed cost, qualification, the learning curve) decides whether it ends up with two firms or two hundred.

### EDA and IP

**Electronic design automation (EDA)** is the software that turns a chip design into the geometric patterns on each photomask (Module 19). The design starts as **register-transfer level (RTL)** code, a text description of the chip's logic in a language such as Verilog, which EDA tools turn into transistors, wires and finally a **mask set**, the ~40–80 photomasks that print the chip. Synopsys (~$6–7B revenue in FY2025 before the Ansys acquisition closed in July 2025, which added ~$2.5B of simulation software), Cadence (~$5B), and Siemens EDA (formerly Mentor, ~$2B, expanded with the 2025 Altair acquisition) hold ~85% of the market between them. Their pricing power is qualification (Idea 4): a foundry's **process design kit (PDK)**, the bundle of design rules, transistor models and pre-built cells that lets a layout print and work on that process, is qualified against specific EDA versions, so switching tools mid-node means re-qualifying everything. Arm (Cambridge, UK; majority owned by SoftBank) licenses the CPU architecture inside essentially every smartphone and inside NVIDIA's Grace and Vera CPUs, earning ~$4 billion a year in licenses and royalties. Other IP vendors (Rambus, Alphawave, Synopsys's own interface IP group) sell the blocks designers no longer build themselves: **SerDes** (serializer/deserializer, the very fast circuits that move data over a wire between chips), **DDR PHYs** (the analog interface circuits that talk to DRAM), and **UCIe** (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express, the open standard for die-to-die links inside a package).

### Fabless

**Fabless** companies design chips and buy wafers. NVIDIA (~$130B revenue in FY2025, ~$216B in FY2026, on a January **fiscal year**, a company's 12-month accounting year; NVIDIA's ends in late January, so its FY2026 is mostly calendar 2025), Broadcom (~$64B total revenue in FY2025, ~$38B of it semiconductors, dominated by custom AI **ASICs**, application-specific integrated circuits designed for one customer's one job, here accelerators for Google and Meta, plus networking), Qualcomm (~$38B chip revenue), AMD (~$34B in 2025), MediaTek (~$16–19B), and Apple (which designs but does not sell chips; its ~$25–30B of annual TSMC wafer purchases make it TSMC's largest customer at ~20–25% of revenue, with NVIDIA close behind or ahead by 2025–26). The fabless model, invented in the late 1980s and made viable by TSMC's founding in 1987, converts a fixed cost (a fab, Idea 1) into a variable cost (wafers bought as needed), and it now accounts for ~35–40% of chip revenue and nearly all of the AI accelerator market.

### IDMs

**Integrated device manufacturers (IDMs)** design and fabricate their own chips: Intel (~$53B), Samsung's Device Solutions division (~$70–100B depending on the memory cycle), SK hynix (~$65B in 2025 on the HBM boom), Micron (~$37B FY2025), Texas Instruments (~$16B, analog and embedded, mostly on 300 mm **mature nodes**, older processes such as 28 nm and above, in Texas), Infineon (~€15B, power and automotive), STMicroelectronics (~$12B), NXP, Renesas, and onsemi. Commodity memory remains dominated by IDMs because cell design and the specialized array process are tightly co-optimized. This is an economic and technical organization, not a physical impossibility of outsourcing: an HBM package can combine memory-array dies from the memory maker with a logic base die made at a foundry (Module 15). The analog IDMs run fabs that are decades old and fully depreciated (Idea 1), which is why their gross margins (~60% at TI) rival TSMC's despite no leading-edge exposure. Intel is the hybrid case: a fading IDM trying to become a foundry, discussed below.

### Foundries

The **foundry** layer makes wafers for others. A **pure-play** foundry makes wafers only for others (TSMC, UMC, GlobalFoundries); a **merchant** foundry is a foundry arm inside an IDM (Samsung Foundry, Intel Foundry) that also serves its parent. By revenue in 2025 (TrendForce estimates, as of early 2026): TSMC ~67–70% of the pure-play plus merchant foundry market, Samsung Foundry ~7–8%, SMIC ~5–6%, UMC ~4–5%, GlobalFoundries ~4%, then Hua Hong, PSMC, Vanguard, Tower, and DB HiTek. At the leading edge the concentration is far higher: at 5 nm-class and below, TSMC holds on the order of 90%+ of wafers, with Samsung's SF3/SF2 (its 3 nm- and 2 nm-class nodes) and Intel's 18A (its 1.8 nm-class node; A for ångström, 0.1 nm) the only alternatives and neither at scale. The two shares answer different questions: ~67–70% is a share of foundry dollars, and a much smaller share of wafers, because a leading-edge wafer sells for 5–10× a mature-node wafer; the ~90% counts wafers at ≤5 nm-class only. TSMC's ~$122 billion 2025 revenue (up ~36% in USD terms) exceeds the combined revenue of every other foundry by roughly 3×. The mechanism is a volume flywheel: whoever has the most wafers on a node amortizes its development across the most units and moves fastest down the **learning curve** (the yield improvement that comes from processing more wafers), which lowers cost, wins the next customer, and adds more volume.

### OSATs

**Outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT)** firms package and test chips (Modules 16–18): ASE Technology (Taiwan, including SPIL; ~$20B revenue in 2025 including its **EMS** arm, electronics manufacturing services, the assembly of finished boards and products; ~$12B of it packaging and test; ~30% OSAT share), Amkor (USA; ~$6–7B), JCET (China), Powertech, KYEC (test), and Tongfu. It is the least concentrated and lowest-margin layer (gross margins ~15–20%) because conventional packaging is labor-intensive and needs little capital, so entry is cheap, qualification is short, and customers move volume between vendors; the exception is where TSMC's **CoWoS** (Module 17) has pulled advanced packaging inside the foundry.

### Equipment

Five companies sell roughly three-quarters of all wafer fab equipment: ASML (Netherlands; ~€32 billion 2025 revenue; the only supplier of **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** tools, which print the finest layers with 13.5 nm light in one exposure, Module 08, and ~85–90% of **immersion DUV**, 193 nm lithography with a film of water between lens and wafer to sharpen the image, Module 07), Applied Materials (USA; ~$28B; deposition, etch, **chemical mechanical polishing (CMP)**, the flattening of each layer with a slurry-fed pad, Module 12, implant, inspection), Lam Research (USA; ~$18B; etch and deposition, especially for memory), Tokyo Electron (Japan; ~¥2.4 trillion or ~$16B; ~90% of coater/developer **tracks**, the robots that coat wafers with resist before the scanner and develop them after, plus etch, furnaces, and clean), and KLA (USA; ~$12B; ~55% of process control, the inspection and **metrology** tools that measure what was just built, Module 13). The second tier is Japanese and Dutch: ASM International (**atomic layer deposition (ALD)**, films grown one atomic layer at a time, Module 06; ~€3B), Screen (wet clean, ~¥500B), Advantest (test, ~¥800B), Disco (dicing and grinding, ~¥400B), Besi (**hybrid bonding**, joining two dies copper-to-copper with no solder, Module 17), Lasertec (EUV mask inspection), Kokusai (batch furnaces, which process 25–100 wafers at once), plus Axcelis (implant) and Onto (metrology) in the US. The equipment layer has the highest gross margins in the physical chain (ASML ~52%, KLA ~60%) because each tool is a monopoly or duopoly product that a fab cannot substitute without re-qualifying its process (Idea 4): once a 3 nm etch recipe runs on a Lam chamber, moving it to an Applied chamber costs months and yield risk, so the incumbent prices near the value of the step, not its own cost.

### Materials

Japan dominates: Shin-Etsu (wafers, EUV **resists**, the light-sensitive films that record the mask pattern, and **photomask blanks**, the polished glass-and-multilayer plates a mask is written on), SUMCO (wafers), JSR (resists; taken private by the government-backed Japan Investment Corporation in 2024), Tokyo Ohka Kogyo (TOK, resists), Fujifilm (resists and CMP **slurries**, the abrasive liquids CMP pads use), Hoya and AGC (EUV mask blanks), Ajinomoto (**Ajinomoto build-up film (ABF)**, the epoxy insulating film laminated in layers to make the package **substrate**, the small circuit board a chip is mounted on, Module 16), Resonac (formerly Showa Denko; gases, CMP), Sumitomo Chemical, and Ibiden and Shinko (substrates). Non-Japanese leaders are Entegris (USA; filters, **FOUPs**, front-opening unified pods, the sealed 25-wafer carriers that move wafers between tools, CMP), Merck/EMD (Germany; specialty chemicals), Air Liquide and Linde (gases), DuPont (CMP pads and slurries), and Taiwan's GlobalWafers and Unimicron. The layer is fragmented across hundreds of products but concentrated within each: two firms make ~90% of EUV mask blanks, one firm makes essentially all ABF, and Japan supplies ~90% of photoresist. The mechanism is spec lock-in in tiny markets: a resist is qualified by its exact chemistry, the market for any one grade is tens of millions of dollars, and no second supplier can justify years of development to win a slot the incumbent holds.

## The Geographic Map

Where each layer physically sits is the substance of every geopolitical argument about the industry. Four terms before the table. **High bandwidth memory (HBM)** is a stack of 8–16 DRAM dies wired vertically through **through-silicon vias (TSVs)**, copper-filled holes drilled through each die, and placed beside the GPU in the same package (Module 15); it is why memory makers are now central to the AI story. **1β/1γ** ("one-beta", "one-gamma") are the DRAM makers' names for their newest process generations, roughly 12–13 nm-class, the only lines that can make current HBM. **Back-end** is packaging and test; **ATMP** (assembly, test, marking and packaging) is India's term for a back-end plant. **300 mm capacity** counts wafer starts on today's standard 300 mm wafers. The map has a structure worth stating plainly: design is American, lithography is Dutch and German, materials are Japanese, memory is Korean, leading-edge logic is Taiwanese, and the largest single market is Chinese. No region can make a leading-edge chip alone; the United States, for all its design strength, has no EUV tools of its own making, no mask blanks, no resist to speak of, and ~10% of world fab capacity. This mutual dependence is the basis of both the export-control regime (the US can weaponize the choke points its allies control) and the pushback against it (each ally's firms lose Chinese revenue when it does).

How did the map get this way? By decisions made 40 years ago that the learning curve then locked in. In the 1980s Japan's NEC, Toshiba and Hitachi took the DRAM market from the American firms that invented it, and the materials suppliers that grew up around those fabs (resists, wafers, gases, mask blanks) stayed after Japan's DRAM makers lost to Korea in the 1990s. Korea's position came from two **chaebol** (family-controlled conglomerates), Samsung and what became SK hynix, betting on memory and investing through every bust when rivals stopped, which is what a commodity with 2–3-year capacity lags rewards. Taiwan's research institute **ITRI** spun out TSMC in 1987 under Morris Chang, and the pure-play foundry let American designers give up their fabs, creating the fabless layer and the volume flywheel at once. ASML was spun out of Philips in 1984 and won the EUV race in the 2000s partly because its US rivals had left lithography. The United States kept what did not need a fab: design, EDA, and most tools that do not involve the lens. Every later policy in this module is an attempt to move something on this map against the grain of those decisions.

## Fab Economics: What a Wafer Costs

A fab is the most expensive factory humans build, and almost all of its cost is committed before it makes anything, so the economics of a wafer are the economics of spreading a fixed bill over as many wafers as possible. This section covers what capacity costs to build, what a wafer sells for, why the price of a transistor stopped falling, and what happens to a fab's margin when demand dips.

### Capex per unit of capacity

The unit of fab capacity is **wafer starts per month (wspm)**, sometimes wpm. Capital cost per 1,000 wspm rises steeply with node, and the mechanism is tool-hours: a fab's tool count is (mask layers × minutes per layer) ÷ wafers per month, because every layer needs a lithography pass, deposition, etch, clean and measurement. A 2 nm process with ~80 layers needs roughly twice the tool-hours per wafer of 28 nm with ~40, its finest ~20 layers each pass through a $200–380 million EUV scanner that handles only ~150–200 wafers an hour, and each generation adds metrology steps (Module 13). In the table, **HKMG** is high-k metal gate, the last planar transistor generation; FinFET and GAA are defined in Before you start (Module 11); **NA** is **numerical aperture**, how wide a cone of light the scanner's lens collects, and higher NA prints finer lines (Module 08): a Low-NA NXE:3800E costs ~$200 million and a High-NA EXE:5200 ~$380 million. Approximate all-in capex (building plus tools) per 1,000 wspm of 300 mm capacity, as of ~2025:

| Node class | Capex per 1,000 wspm | Fab of 50,000 wspm | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 28 nm (planar HKMG) | ~$100–130M | ~$5–6B | No EUV; ~40 mask layers |
| 16/12 nm FinFET | ~$150–200M | ~$8–10B | |
| 7 nm (N7, DUV-only or light EUV) | ~$200–250M | ~$10–12B | |
| 5 nm (N5, ~14 EUV layers) | ~$300–350M | ~$15–17B | |
| 3 nm (N3, ~20+ EUV layers) | ~$350–400M | ~$18–20B | |
| 2 nm (N2, GAA) | ~$450–550M | ~$22–28B | Intel/TSMC 2 nm-class fabs quoted at $20–30B |
| Leading-edge DRAM (1β/1γ with EUV) | ~$150–250M | ~$8–12B | HBM adds TSV and stacking lines |
| 3D NAND (200+ layers) | ~$100–150M | ~$5–8B | Etch-heavy rather than litho-heavy |

Tools are ~70–80% of the total. A leading-edge logic fab is in the range of ~$15–20 billion of equipment per 50,000 wspm, of which lithography is ~25–30%, deposition and etch ~40%, and **process control** (inspection and metrology tools, Module 13) ~10–12%.

### Wafer prices by node

Foundries do not publish prices, but the estimates below (compiled from analyst work by SemiAnalysis, IBS, TrendForce, and customer disclosures; as of 2025) are consistent enough to plan on. These are for high-volume customers on a mature-yield process; small customers pay more, and prices were rising 3–10% a year on advanced nodes in 2025–26 as TSMC exercised pricing power. Density is in **million transistors per mm² (MTr/mm²)** for logic cells.

| Node | Approx. price per 300 mm wafer | Transistor density (MTr/mm², logic) | Approx. price per 100M transistors |
|---|---|---|---|
| 28 nm | ~$2,500–3,000 | ~15 | ~$0.28 |
| 16/12 nm | ~$4,000–4,500 | ~30 | ~$0.20 |
| 7 nm (N7) | ~$9,000–10,000 | ~90–100 | ~$0.15 |
| 5 nm (N5) | ~$15,000–17,000 | ~135–140 | ~$0.17 |
| 3 nm (N3E) | ~$18,000–20,000 | ~200–215 | ~$0.13 |
| 2 nm (N2) | ~$30,000 | ~250–280 | ~$0.16 |

The last column is arithmetic you can check. A 300 mm wafer has an area of π × 150² ≈ 70,700 mm², call it 70,000 mm² usable; multiply by density for transistors per wafer, then divide the price by that. For the 7 nm row: ~$10,000 ÷ (95 MTr/mm² × 70,000 mm² ÷ 100 MTr) = $10,000 ÷ 66,500 ≈ $0.15 per 100 million transistors. Real chips lose area at the wafer edge and to defects, so every row is really higher by a similar factor; the shape of the column is the point. Price rises per node for three reasons: more EUV layers, each costing more than the DUV layer it replaces; longer cycle time (each wafer occupies the fab for more of its ~3 months); and TSMC's pricing power at nodes with no second source.

### Cost per transistor has flattened

For four decades the cost of a transistor fell ~30% a year, because each node roughly doubled density while wafer price rose only ~10–20%. That stopped around the 28 nm to 20 nm transition in 2012–2014, when **double patterning** arrived: 193 nm light cannot print lines closer than ~80 nm apart in one pass, so a layer's pattern is split across two exposures, each with its own litho-etch loop (Module 07), and wafer cost rose faster than density improved. It has been roughly flat since, with modest gains at 7 nm and 3 nm and a step backward at 5 nm and again at 2 nm in the table above. Three things drive the flattening:

1. **Wafer price is rising ~1.5–1.8× per node** while logic density improves only ~1.5–1.7× (TSMC N5 to N3E logic-library improvement is reported around 1.6×; its N2 disclosure instead quotes >1.15× chip density versus 3 nm, a different circuit-mix metric). Whether they cancel depends on the node pair and product mix; a 1.5× price increase divided by a 1.15× density gain is a 1.30× increase in cost per transistor before yield changes.
2. **SRAM stopped scaling.** **Static RAM (SRAM)** is the fast on-chip memory used for caches, six transistors per bit, built from the same logic transistors as the rest of the die; a **bit cell** is the area one bit occupies. TSMC's N3E SRAM bit cell is 0.021 µm², essentially identical to N5's, while the [2025 N2 SRAM demonstration](https://research.tsmc.com/page/memory/4.html) uses a 0.021 µm² HD cell and reports a 1.1× macro-density improvement through design co-optimization. Cell area and macro density need separate comparisons. SRAM can occupy a large, product-dependent fraction of a die, so a logic-library gain alone does not determine whole-chip cost.
3. **Analog and I/O do not scale**, because their sizes are set by voltages, currents and wire pitches rather than transistor length, which is one reason AMD, Intel, and NVIDIA moved I/O and cache to **chiplets**, a design split into several dies packaged together (Module 17), on older nodes.

Design cost compounds the problem. IBS estimates of the total cost to design a large **system on chip (SoC)**, one die combining CPU, GPU, memory controllers and I/O (including IP, verification, and masks): ~$50 million at 28 nm, ~$100 million at 16 nm, ~$300 million at 7 nm, ~$540 million at 5 nm, ~$590 million at 3 nm, and ~$700–800 million at 2 nm. A ~$25–30 million mask set (Module 19) is a small part of this; most is **verification** (simulating the design to prove it has no logic bugs, the largest single cost) and **physical design** (placing and wiring the transistors) labor. So only products with revenue in the billions justify the leading edge, which is why the N2 customer list is Apple, NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, MediaTek, Broadcom, and the hyperscalers' ASIC programs, and almost nobody else.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Flat cost per transistor plus non-scaling SRAM and analog is the economic reason chiplets and CoWoS exist (Module 17). If shrinking a cache or an I/O block saves no money, the rational design puts those blocks on a cheaper older node, reserves the new node for the logic that still benefits, and joins the pieces in a package (AMD's EPYC: compute chiplets on the newest node, I/O die on 6 nm). The package, not the transistor, became the place where Moore's law continues.

### Moore's law and Rock's law

**Moore's law** as originally stated (transistor count per chip doubling every ~2 years at minimum cost per transistor) is dead in its cost clause and alive in its count clause. Transistor count per *package* continues to double roughly every 2–3 years, but by adding silicon (reticle-limited dies, chiplets, 3D stacking) rather than by making transistors cheaper. The **reticle limit** is the largest area a scanner can print in one exposure, 26 × 33 mm = ~858 mm²; H100 (814 mm²) sits just under it, which is why Blackwell had to become two dies joined in a CoWoS-L package (Module 17). H100 had 80 billion transistors on one die in 2022, Blackwell 208 billion on two dies in 2024, and Rubin more still across a larger CoWoS-L package. **Rock's law** (Arthur Rock, 1960s) states that the cost of a fab doubles every four years, and it has held uncomfortably well: ~$1 billion in the mid-1990s, ~$3 billion around 2003, ~$5–7 billion around 2010, ~$15 billion for a 7/5 nm fab around 2018, and ~$20–30 billion for a 3/2 nm fab in 2023–2026. Rock's law is why the number of companies at the leading edge fell from ~25 at 130 nm (2001) to ~8 at 28 nm (2011), 4 at 10/7 nm (2017), and 3 at 3/2 nm (2025), with Samsung and Intel both struggling to justify the next step.

### The break-even wafer price

The worked example uses the toolkit ideas with their exact meanings: **straight-line** depreciation (an equal charge each year), **utilization** (wafers actually started ÷ capacity), and **operating leverage** (the sensitivity of profit to volume when most costs are fixed).

> **Intuition:** Think of the fab as a 40,000-room hotel that cost $20 billion and must be paid off in five years. The mortgage is due whether the rooms are full or empty, and cleaning a room costs a fraction of the room rate. At 90% occupancy each guest carries a small slice of the mortgage; at 50% each carries nearly twice as much, and the rate that earned a fat margin barely covers costs. The hotel discounts to fill rooms, but only down to the cleaning cost, which for a fab is the cash cost per wafer computed below.

> **Worked example: break-even wafer price for a 2 nm fab.**
> Take a 40,000 wspm N2-class fab with all-in capex of $20 billion (~$500 million per 1,000 wspm). TSMC depreciates equipment straight-line over 5 years, so annual depreciation is roughly $20B × 0.8 (tool share) / 5 + $20B × 0.2 (building) / 20 = $3.2B + $0.2B = ~$3.4 billion a year.
> At 90% utilization the fab produces 40,000 × 12 × 0.9 = 432,000 wafers a year, so depreciation alone is $3.4B / 432,000 = ~$7,900 per wafer. Variable and semi-fixed cash costs at the leading edge (resists and EUV consumables, gases, wet chemicals, wafers at ~$150, electricity at ~1–1.5 MWh per wafer, maintenance contracts, ~3,000 staff) run on the order of $5,000–6,000 per wafer, giving a full manufacturing cost of ~$13,000–14,000.
> To earn TSMC's ~58% corporate gross margin, price = $13,500 / (1 − 0.58) = ~$32,000, which is where N2 wafer quotes sit. Now drop utilization to 70% during a downturn: output falls to 336,000 wafers, depreciation per wafer rises to ~$10,100, full cost to ~$15,600, and gross margin at the same $32,000 price falls to ~51%. At 50% utilization (240,000 wafers), depreciation per wafer is ~$14,200, cost ~$19,700, and margin ~38%. This operating leverage is the entire explanation for why foundries fight for loading, why they discount old nodes rather than idle them, and why TSMC's margins are so sensitive to a few points of utilization.
> One caveat: the example treats the ~$5,500 cash cost as fully variable, but staff and maintenance contracts are semi-fixed, so real margins at low utilization are slightly worse than shown.

### TSMC: margins, capex, and pricing power

TSMC's 2025 financials, in round numbers: revenue ~$122 billion, gross margin ~59–60%, operating margin ~50%, net income ~$55 billion, capex ~$41 billion, with 2026 capex guided to $52–56 billion (January 2026 guidance). Advanced nodes (7 nm and below) were ~75% of wafer revenue, with 3 nm alone ~25%. Its position lets it do three things no other foundry can: raise prices on its newest node each year (N2 launched at ~$30,000, ~50% above N3, when the density gain was ~15%), charge for advanced packaging as a separate ~$10 billion-a-year business, and require **prepayments**, billions of dollars handed over years ahead to reserve capacity, which shifts the fab's financing risk onto the customer (NVIDIA, Apple, and AMD have all made multi-billion-dollar prepayments). Customers pay 50% more for 15% more density because there is no second source at the node, because their product still needs N2's ~25–30% power reduction and power is the binding limit in phones and data centers alike, and despite a higher estimated wafer price per transistor: at equal usable area and yield, a 1.5× price rise divided by 1.15× density is about 1.30×, or 30% higher. Product economics may still improve through power or performance, but that is a different metric. TSMC's stated policy is to price overseas fabs higher to preserve a corporate gross margin of 53%+ "and higher", and its 2025 disclosures showed Arizona already profitable on N4 wafers priced above Taiwan.

The competitive logic is a flywheel: TSMC's ~90% leading-edge share gives it the volume to amortize each node's ~$20–30 billion development cost across 2–3× as many wafers as Samsung or Intel, which funds the next node earlier, which attracts the next customer. Samsung Foundry's problem in 2022–2025 was a yield gap at SF3 that pushed its anchor customers (Qualcomm, NVIDIA) to TSMC; Intel's problem is that it has been its own only customer.

## Cycles: Utilization, Memory and the AI Supercycle

Capacity is decided years ahead on forecasts (Idea 5), so the industry alternates between gluts, when fabs run at 70% and tool orders collapse, and shortages, when wafers are allocated and prices double. The 2023–26 AI demand shock is the largest swing since the 1990s, and it arrived through the usual mechanism: one line item of customer spending rose faster than any capacity plan assumed.

### Utilization cycles

Foundry utilization swings between ~70% in troughs (2009, 2019, 2023 for mature nodes) and ~100%+ in peaks (2021, and the leading edge in 2024–26). Since equipment is bought ahead of capacity, WFE spending has fallen 20–40% in each of the last four industry downturns. Plug 70% into the worked example above and you have the margin a trough produces.

### The memory boom and bust

Memory is the extreme case. DRAM and NAND are commodities: a Samsung DDR5 module and a Micron one are interchangeable, so price is set by the marginal bit: when every seller's product is identical, price falls to what the most desperate seller with spare capacity will accept, and a 10% oversupply can halve the price. When capacity runs ahead of demand, **contract prices** (quarterly prices negotiated with large buyers, as opposed to the daily **spot** market) can fall 50–70% in 18 months (2018–2019, 2022–2023); when it runs behind, they rise as fast. Samsung's memory division lost on the order of ₩15 trillion (~$11 billion) in 2023 and then earned tens of trillions of won in 2025; SK hynix went from a ₩8 trillion (~$6 billion) loss in 2023 to record profits in 2025 as HBM, which cannot be quickly re-tooled from commodity DRAM lines, sold out through 2026. The 2025–26 upswing was unusual in that the three DRAM makers diverted 1β/1γ capacity to HBM and under-invested in commodity DRAM, so that commodity **DDR5** (today's PC and server memory standard) and **LPDDR5X** (its low-power phone and laptop variant) prices more than doubled during late 2025 and early 2026. HBM consumes ~2–3× the wafer area per bit of standard DRAM because the TSVs take area on every die, the cells around them need larger margins, and stacking 8–16 dies means one bad die can scrap a whole stack. The mechanism is the same as in every memory cycle; only the trigger was new.

> **What can go wrong:** Building for a forecast. In 2021, with contract prices rising and customers double-ordering, the memory makers placed the tool orders that became 2023's capacity; by the time it started, PC and phone demand had fallen and customers were working off hoarded inventory, so contract prices fell 50–70% and Samsung and SK hynix posted their ₩15 trillion and ₩8 trillion losses. Planners detect the turn from two dials: customer **inventory weeks** (weeks of consumption a buyer holds; above ~10–12, orders are about to stop) and TrendForce's contract-price indices. The response is to cut wafer starts (Micron and SK hynix cut ~20–30% in 2023), defer capex and tool deliveries, and slow the move to new nodes, which lowers bit growth a year later and sets up the next shortage. The 2025–26 spike is the mirror image, with the same lag built in.

### The AI capex supercycle

The demand shock of 2023–2026 has one source: hyperscaler capital expenditure. The four largest US cloud companies (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta) spent ~$230 billion of capex in 2024, ~$400–410 billion in 2025, and guided to ~$720–745 billion for 2026 (as of the August 2026 earnings round: Amazon ~$220B, Microsoft ~$175B on a calendar-year basis, Alphabet ~$195–205B, Meta ~$130–145B; the January 2026 guidance had totalled ~$630–690B and was raised twice). Adding Oracle, xAI, OpenAI's **Stargate** partners (the OpenAI, SoftBank and Oracle data-center venture announced in January 2025), CoreWeave and other **neoclouds** (new companies whose business is renting GPUs), and the Gulf sovereign builders takes total AI infrastructure spend toward $1 trillion in 2026. Roughly 40–50% of a data center's capex is the accelerator itself, ~10–15% is memory and storage, and the rest is networking, servers, power, cooling, and building. This is why NVIDIA's data center revenue went from ~$15 billion (FY2023) to ~$115 billion (FY2025) and ~$197 billion (FY2026, on total revenue of ~$216 billion), why SK hynix's HBM revenue overtook its commodity DRAM revenue, and why TSMC's high-performance computing segment became ~60% of its revenue.

The supercycle's fragility is that the capex is financed by four companies' **operating cash flow** (the cash their existing businesses generate, ~$500–600 billion a year combined) plus, increasingly, debt and **circular deals**, in which a chip vendor invests in or lends to a customer that then buys its chips, so the same dollars appear once as investment and again as revenue. The point for the supply chain is that TSMC, SK hynix, and ASML are building 2027–2028 capacity against 2026 orders, exactly the timing mismatch that produced every earlier bust.

### The demand line, in units

The whole 2024–28 build plan of TSMC, SK hynix, Micron and ASML is sized against one line, accelerator units:

- **GPU units**: NVIDIA shipped on the order of 3.7 million data-center GPUs in 2023 (TechInsights), ~5 million **Hopper**-class in 2024, and ~6–8 million **Blackwell**-class in 2025 (Hopper, Blackwell and **Rubin** are NVIDIA's 2022, 2024 and 2026 GPU generations), with 2026 dominated by Blackwell Ultra (**GB300**, the second Blackwell product, paired with a Grace CPU) and the Rubin ramp in the second half. AMD's MI300/MI350 series and the custom ASICs (Google **TPU** v6/v7, its tensor processing unit, and Amazon **Trainium** 2/3, both built via Broadcom and Marvell; Meta **MTIA**; Microsoft **Maia**) add on the order of 3–5 million accelerator-class packages a year. Each package is 800–1,700 mm² of leading-edge silicon plus 8–16 HBM stacks.
- **CoWoS**: TSMC's CoWoS capacity rose from ~35,000 wafers a month at the end of 2024 to ~70,000–75,000 at the end of 2025 and was headed toward ~100,000–130,000 by the end of 2026 (as of early 2026), with NVIDIA taking roughly 60% of it. CoWoS was the binding constraint on accelerator supply in 2023–2025 (Module 17); by 2026 it had been joined by HBM.
- **HBM**: total HBM bit shipments roughly doubled in each of 2024 and 2025; SK hynix's HBM revenue passed its commodity DRAM revenue in 2025, and all three suppliers reported 2026 HBM4 capacity sold out in 2025. HBM's ~2–3× wafer consumption per bit is what pulled commodity DRAM into shortage.
- **Power**: an **NVL72** rack (NVIDIA's 72-GPU liquid-cooled rack) draws ~120–140 kW and a Rubin-generation rack ~200 kW+; a 1 GW campus houses on the order of 500,000–700,000 GPUs. US data centers consumed ~4.4% of US electricity in 2023 and are projected to reach ~6.7–12% by 2028 (**LBNL**, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, author of the Department of Energy's data-center energy reports, December 2024), with **interconnection queues** (the wait to be permitted to draw power from the grid) of 3–7 years in major markets. Power, transformers, and gas turbines, not chips, became the binding constraint on new capacity in 2025–26, which is why hyperscalers began signing nuclear and gas-generation deals.

> **Worked example: from GPUs to wafers, CoWoS and HBM.**
> Take ~6 million Blackwell-class packages a year, each with 2 compute dies of ~800 mm²: ~12 million dies. A 300 mm wafer (~70,000 mm²) fits ~70 rectangular sites of that size inside the circle, and at leading-edge defect density with NVIDIA's practice of shipping dies with a few faulty units disabled, call it ~60 usable dies per wafer. So ~12 M ÷ 60 = ~200,000 N4 wafers a year, or ~17,000 wspm. TSMC made ~15 million wafers in 2025, so the compute dies for the entire Blackwell generation are ~1–2% of its output. This modeled share of aggregate shipments does not establish spare N4 capacity: node, customer allocation, actual sellable-die yield and qualified fab availability must be compared with demand.
> Now the package. Each package needs one CoWoS interposer slot, and a Blackwell interposer is ~3× reticle size, so a CoWoS wafer yields only ~15–20 packages: ~6 M ÷ ~17 = ~350,000 CoWoS wafers a year, ~30,000 wpm, against total capacity of ~70,000–75,000 wpm at the end of 2025. One product line takes a third to a half of world advanced-packaging capacity, consistent with NVIDIA's ~60% share once Hopper and other parts are added.
> Now the memory. ~6 M packages × 8 HBM stacks = ~48 million stacks; at 12 dies per stack that is ~600 million DRAM dies, each on a 1β/1γ line and each using 2–3× the wafer area per bit of a commodity die. CXMT's ~200,000+ wspm is ~10–15% of world DRAM wafer capacity, so world capacity is ~1.5–2 million wspm; ~600 M dies of ~100 mm²-class each, before stacking yield loss and the base die under each stack, is on the order of a tenth of world DRAM wafer starts, all from the most advanced lines. That is the Misconceptions bullet made quantitative: front-end wafers are a rounding error for TSMC, packaging is a third to a half of CoWoS, and the memory is a large enough slice of leading-edge DRAM to move the price of every DRAM chip on earth.

## Taiwan Concentration Risk

Taiwan makes ~90% of the world's most advanced logic chips and ~90% of CoWoS-class packaging inside a 400 km long island on the Pacific Ring of Fire, across a strait from a government that has never renounced the use of force. This matters more than any other concentration in the chain because of time: a fab cannot be replaced in less than 2–3 years, so any interruption longer than a fab's inventory is felt by every electronics product on earth. The risk scenarios divide by mechanism.

**Earthquakes** are the frequent, manageable case. Taiwan's fabs are built on **base isolation** (the building sits on flexible bearings so the ground moves underneath it) and dampers, and tools have automatic shutdown on seismic triggers (the wafer inside a lithography tool during a quake is scrapped, but the tool is protected). On April 3, 2024 a magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck off Hualien; TSMC reported that overall tool recovery exceeded 70% within 10 hours and 80% within a day, with fabs at full production within ~3 days, and a total impact of ~NT$3 billion (~$90 million) in scrapped wafers and lost output, about 0.4–0.5% of a 2024 quarter's revenue. A magnitude 6.4 quake near Chiayi on January 21, 2025 was costlier (~NT$5.3 billion, ~$160 million net of insurance, recognized in the first quarter of 2025) but still well under 1% of a quarter's revenue. Damage depends on local shaking, distance, depth, soil, construction and utility interruption, not a universal magnitude threshold. TSMC identifies natural disasters as a material disruption risk; its spread across Hsinchu, Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung provides partial mitigation.

> **What can go wrong:** Earthquake day inside a fab. Seismic sensors on each tool trip at a set acceleration and the tool halts mid-step: a scanner stops with a wafer half exposed, a furnace holds a batch at temperature, a wet bench pauses with wafers in acid. Every wafer mid-way through a step that cannot be resumed (exposure, some etches and depositions) is scrapped; wafers between steps are held while engineers check each tool's alignment and vacuum. Recovery runs in a fixed sequence: safety and gas systems, then facilities (chilled water, exhaust, ultrapure water), then each tool re-qualified with test wafers, which is why the Hualien numbers were ~70% of tools in 10 hours, ~80% in a day, and full output in ~3 days: the last tools back are the ones whose alignment must be re-verified, chiefly the scanners. The loss is booked as scrapped work-in-progress plus output not produced, charged to the quarter's cost of sales and offset by insurance recognized later. The signature of a well-run fab is that only the wafers mid-exposure are lost, not those mid-flow.

**A blockade** is the serious case. A **PRC** (People's Republic of China) quarantine of shipping would not physically damage fabs but would stop the inflow of chemicals, gases, wafers, and spare parts and the outflow of wafers to packaging (much of it in Taiwan anyway) and to customers. A fab holds days to weeks of most consumables, and the horizon differs by item: specialty gases and some chemicals arrive in cylinders and totes that last days; bulk chemicals and blank wafers last weeks; spare parts vary by tool, and some exist only at the vendor in the Netherlands, Japan or the United States. So within weeks global leading-edge production would fall by ~90%, and within months the fabs would be idle without a shot fired. The published damage estimates differ mainly by scenario: the Rhodium Group (2022) put the economic activity at risk from a blockade at more than ~$2 trillion a year; Bloomberg Economics (2024) estimated a blockade would cost ~5% of world GDP and a full war ~10% (~$10 trillion in the first year), because every server, phone, car, and industrial product waits on a chip.

> **Intuition:** The Silicon Shield. The argument is that mutual dependence deters conflict: China needs Taiwan's chips as much as the West does, a damaged TSMC is worthless to any conqueror, and a fab without its Dutch, Japanese and American suppliers is an expensive shed. As economics it is sound, which is why Beijing's self-sufficiency push (below) can be read as an attempt to lower the cost of the shield to itself. As deterrence it has a hole: shields deter actors who are counting costs, and governments that start wars over territory usually are not. The shield lowers the probability of conflict without changing what conflict would do to the chain, which is why the rest of this module is about moving fabs.

**An invasion** would combine the blockade with physical destruction, the departure of TSMC's ~80,000 employees, and, under the US concept of **denial** (making the fabs useless to an occupier), possible deliberate disabling of tools (ASML has reportedly said that EUV tools can be remotely disabled, and a 2024 Bloomberg report described discussions of this). The only mitigation is geographic diversification, which is why so much of the rest of this module is about fabs outside Taiwan.

## Diversification: Arizona, Kumamoto, Dresden, and the Intel Question

A leading-edge process that exists at one site is a single point of failure for the world. The difficulty in moving it is not money, as the worked example below shows, but three things money buys slowly: a node that has to be copied from Taiwan, a workforce that takes years to train, and customers willing to pay more for wafers from a fab one generation behind.

The one-node lag is structural. TSMC develops each node in its Hsinchu research fab and transfers it to volume fabs by **copy exactly**: every tool, recipe and parameter is matched to the source fab, and the new site runs months of test wafers until its yield and electrical measurements match Hsinchu's. Each additional site is another months-long matching exercise, so a fab an ocean away starts a step behind by construction. At full build-out in the early 2030s, TSMC has said Arizona would hold ~30% of its 2 nm-and-beyond capacity, and it would still lag Taiwan by one node for this reason. Until Amkor's Arizona packaging plant (~2027–28) and TSMC's own Arizona CoWoS lines exist, Arizona wafers are flown back to Taiwan for packaging.

> **Worked example: illustrative US capacity equal to 1% of TSMC’s 2025 wafer shipments.**
> TSMC shipped roughly 15 million 300 mm-equivalent wafers in 2025, so 1% is ~150,000 wafers a year, or ~12,500 wspm. Assume the wafers are N3-class.
> Capex in Taiwan for 12,500 wspm at ~$375 million per 1,000 wspm is ~$4.7 billion. TSMC and its founder have said US construction and installation cost runs ~1.5× Taiwan (Morris Chang put it at 50% higher; some estimates run to 2× for construction labor and permitting), and tools cost the same, so the US figure is roughly $4.7B × (0.8 × 1.0 + 0.2 × 1.75) = ~$5.4 billion for tools plus building before incentives. The 35% investment tax credit on qualifying property (roughly the $3.75 billion of tools and much of the building) returns ~$1.6–1.8 billion, and pro-rata CHIPS grants (~10% of the original $65 billion commitment) another ~$0.5 billion, leaving a net capex of ~$3.1–3.3 billion, below Taiwan's unsubsidized figure.
> Operating cost is the persistent penalty. TSMC has indicated Arizona wafers cost on the order of 5–20% more than Taiwan (labor, construction depreciation, chemicals and gases shipped in, smaller scale); at 10% on a ~$12,000 N3 manufacturing cost that is ~$1,200 per wafer, or ~$180 million a year on 150,000 wafers, ~0.15% of TSMC's revenue. TSMC recovers it by pricing US-made wafers higher to customers who want US origin, under these assumptions, the example shows how incentives can offset construction cost; it does not establish actual profitability, which also depends on utilization, product mix, execution and incentive eligibility (Arizona's ~1,500 Taiwanese expatriates, its multi-year training pipeline, and a day or more of travel for **reticles**, the photomasks themselves, and for engineers). What 1% does not buy is resilience: this hypothetical addition replaces only 1% of the total wafer-shipment benchmark. It cannot establish the share of actual leading-edge output protected, because the benchmark mixes nodes. Moving 20% would cost, by the same arithmetic, ~$110 billion of capex before incentives, the same order as the $165 billion TSMC has actually committed to Arizona (which also covers packaging plants and an R&D center).

### Intel's foundry pivot

Intel's plan under Pat Gelsinger (2021–2024) was to catch TSMC in process technology ("five nodes in four years": Intel 7, 4, 3, 20A, 18A) and open its fabs to external customers as **Intel Foundry**. As of mid-2026: **Intel 18A** (**RibbonFET**, Intel's name for its gate-all-around transistor, plus **PowerVia**, backside power delivery that moves the power wiring under the transistors, Module 11) is in high-volume production at Fab 52 in Chandler, Arizona, shipping the Panther Lake client CPUs launched at CES 2026 and the Clearwater Forest Xeon; 20A was cancelled in 2024 to concentrate resources; and **Intel 14A**, the first node designed for High-NA EUV, has an early PDK (version 0.5) with customers, a 0.9 PDK targeted for October 2026, and Intel stating that prospective external customers would make commitments in the second half of 2026 into the first half of 2027 (PDK versions run from 0.5, an early kit for exploratory design, through 0.9, near-final, to 1.0, production-ready). In July 2025 Intel said it would not build 14A capacity without an external anchor customer; by June 2026 it had committed to completing 14A development and starting capacity expansion regardless, and the 18A-P performance variant entered **risk production** (the phase in which a fab runs paying customers' first wafers before yield is proven, so the customer carries the risk) in June 2026 (as of mid-2026). Reported 18A engagements include Microsoft and Amazon for custom chips, and in 2026 several reports described an Apple engagement for entry-level products (as of mid-2026, not fully confirmed by either company; treat as unverified). Foundry revenue from external customers remained small (well under $1 billion a year) against a foundry segment losing ~$10–13 billion a year on ~$17–18 billion of mostly internal revenue.

In August 2025 the US government converted Intel's undisbursed CHIPS grants ($5.7 billion) and **Secure Enclave** funds ($3.2 billion; a Pentagon program funding a trusted domestic supply of leading-edge chips) into an $8.9 billion equity purchase of 433.3 million shares at $20.47, a ~10% (9.9%) stake, with a **warrant** (a right to buy more shares later at a fixed price) for a further 5% exercisable if Intel's ownership of its foundry business falls below 51% (the stake was diluted somewhat by Intel's ~$20 billion equity offering in August 2026). SoftBank ($2 billion) and NVIDIA ($5 billion, with a joint product agreement) invested the same season. The policy logic is that Intel is the only US-headquartered company with a leading-edge process, and that the government now has a direct financial interest in Intel Foundry surviving. Intel's Ohio fab, originally due in 2025, has been delayed to the 2030s, and its Magdeburg (Germany) and Poland projects were cancelled in July 2025.

### Samsung Taylor

Samsung's Taylor, Texas fab (announced 2021 at $17 billion, since grown past $37 billion with $4.745 billion of CHIPS support) sat idle through 2024 for lack of customers, then in July 2025 secured a reported $16.5 billion contract to make Tesla's AI6 chip there on SF2, with production targeted from 2026–27. Samsung also signed Apple for image sensors in Austin. Taylor is the only leading-edge fab in the US that is not Intel's or TSMC's, and its fill rate is the best proxy for whether a second-source foundry can exist.

> **What can go wrong:** A fab without customers. The recurring failure of industrial policy is not a fab that cannot be built but a fab nobody has ordered wafers from: Samsung Taylor stood complete and empty from 2022 to 2024; Intel Foundry's external revenue has stayed well under $1 billion a year against ~$17–18 billion of internal wafers; JASM 2 slipped a year when the automotive customers it was sized for cut forecasts. It shows up first in utilization, then in the segment accounts: an empty fab still depreciates (Idea 1), so a fully tooled $20 billion fab with no wafer starts is a ~$3 billion-a-year loss before it makes a chip, and Intel Foundry's ~$10–13 billion annual losses are mostly this arithmetic. The fix is not more subsidy, which lowers capex that is already sunk, but an anchor customer whose volume fills the fab (Tesla for Taylor, Apple and NVIDIA for Arizona) or a node advantage that brings customers regardless (TSMC's). A program that funds construction without securing either has built a very expensive building.

## Export Controls in Detail

The US export-control campaign against China's advanced computing capability is the most consequential industrial-policy intervention in the chain's history, and it is legally detailed, so the mechanism matters. The problem is asymmetric: the United States designs the chips but does not make them or the tools that make them, so a rule written in Washington only works if it reaches Taiwanese fabs, Dutch scanners and Korean memory.

BIS controls items through the **Export Administration Regulations (EAR)**, classifying them by **Export Control Classification Number (ECCN)** and requiring licenses for listed destinations and end users; the **Entity List** names specific firms that need a license for almost everything; and the **Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR)** extends US jurisdiction to foreign-made items produced with US technology, tools, or software, which is the clause that reaches a TSMC wafer made on Applied Materials tools with Synopsys software. Four terms used in the table: **Section 232** is a US statute allowing tariffs on national-security grounds; **HBM3** is the third generation of high bandwidth memory (Module 15); **MI308** and **MI325X** are AMD's China-specific and export-class accelerators, the counterparts of NVIDIA's H20 and H200; and the **US persons** rule reaches US citizens and permanent residents wherever they work.

The thresholds in the table are formulas, and the whole regime turns on them, so it is worth computing one.

> **Worked example: computing TPP and performance density.**
> **Total processing performance (TPP)** = peak operations per second, in trillions (TOPS or TFLOPS), × the bit-width of those operations. Performance density = TPP ÷ die area in mm². All performance figures below are datasheet peaks, marked "~".
> H100 (SXM): ~989 TFLOPS of dense 16-bit floating-point math. TPP = ~989 × 16 ≈ 15,800, more than 3× the 4,800 control line. Its die is 814 mm², so density ≈ 15,800 ÷ 814 ≈ 19 TPP/mm², more than 3× the 5.92 line. It fails both tests and is controlled.
> H20: the same 814 mm² die with most of its compute units disabled, ~148 TFLOPS of the same 16-bit math. TPP = ~148 × 16 ≈ 2,370, just under the 2,400 floor of the notification band and far under 4,800; density ≈ 2,370 ÷ 814 ≈ 2.9 TPP/mm², under 5.92. It passes both tests, which is why it was legal in 2024, and the arithmetic says what it is: ~15% of H100's compute (148 ÷ 989) attached to H100's full memory system, which is why it stayed useful for inference, where memory bandwidth matters more than arithmetic.
> The formula also explains the A800/H800 episode: the 2022 rule tested TPP and interconnect bandwidth together, so a full-TPP die with its chip-to-chip links capped at 400 GB/s passed. The 2023 rule dropped the interconnect test, and TPP alone caught them.

The tool controls are the part that binds. Advanced chips can be smuggled (BIS and the Justice Department have prosecuted several cases of H100s routed through Singapore and Malaysia, and the Financial Times reported that on the order of $1 billion of NVIDIA product reached China in the three months after the April 2025 H20 ban), but a fab cannot smuggle an EUV scanner.

> **Intuition:** A GPU is a 2 kg box. It is controlled at the border, and borders leak: it can travel in a suitcase, be re-labelled in Singapore, or be rented by the hour from a data center in Malaysia. An EUV scanner is ~180 tonnes in ~40 shipping containers, needs ASML engineers on site continuously for its whole life, consumes a stream of vendor-only spare parts (collector mirrors, droplet generators, laser components), and runs on vendor software updated remotely. It is controlled not at the border but by its umbilical cord to the vendor: cut the service contract and the tool degrades within months. Immersion DUV scanners and advanced etch and deposition tools are the same on a smaller scale, which is why the controls on tools and on their servicing by US and Dutch persons do the work that the controls on chips cannot.

This is why the Dutch and Japanese alignment matters:

- **Netherlands**: EUV has never been licensed to China (ASML never shipped one; the Dutch government withheld the license in 2019 under US pressure). Since September 2023, Dutch licenses are required for the TWINSCAN NXT:2000i and above (the most capable immersion DUV tools); in September 2024 the Dutch took over from the US the licensing of NXT:1970i and 1980i, and in January 2025 extended controls to metrology and some further immersion tools. ASML may still ship older immersion tools (NXT:1980i-class) to non-Entity-List Chinese fabs, and China was ~41% of ASML's system sales in 2024 (peaking near 49% in individual quarters), ~33% in 2025, and guided to ~20% for 2026 (as of early 2026). US-person servicing restrictions mean ASML's US-citizen engineers cannot support tools at SMIC's or Huawei-linked advanced fabs; Dutch nationals can, subject to Dutch licenses that have been progressively tightened.
- **Japan**: since July 23, 2023, licenses are required for 23 categories of equipment, including EUV-related items, advanced etch and deposition, and cleaning, which affects TEL, Screen, Kokusai, and Nikon. Japan has been slower than the US to restrict servicing.

The effect on ASML, AMAT, Lam, and KLA has been real but bounded: each lost ~5–15% of revenue to the controls, offset by the AI capex boom. China remained a very large market for all of them: ASML's China share of system sales was the 41% (2024) and 33% (2025) quoted above, and the US tool makers' China revenue share was ~25–35% in 2024–25. That revenue comes from mature-node fabs (28 nm and above) that the rules deliberately leave open, and that Chinese fabs have been building at an unprecedented rate.

> **What can go wrong:** Evasion, and how it is caught. The routes are known. **Transshipment**: chips sold legally to a Singapore or Malaysian distributor are re-exported to China; Singapore's share of NVIDIA's billed revenue rising far above its data-center footprint in 2024 was the signature. **Cloud rental**: a Chinese firm rents H100 time outside China, which the chip rules do not reach and the rescinded Diffusion Rule tried to. **Shell affiliates**: a listed entity buys through a subsidiary under another name, which the September 2025 50% rule was written to close. Detection comes from teardowns (the TSMC die found in a Huawei Ascend 910B in October 2024 triggered the January 2025 foundry rule), customs data showing volumes out of proportion to a country's compute demand, and Justice Department cases built on payment records, which have produced guilty pleas for H100 smuggling through Singapore and Malaysia. The scale is real but bounded: the FT's ~$1 billion estimate for the three months after the H20 ban is a few percent of NVIDIA's data-center revenue over the period. Chips leak by the thousand; fabs do not leak at all.

## China's Response

China's answer to the controls is a state-financed effort to replace every layer of the chain, and its results are uneven by layer for the reason the controls were designed as they were: layers that money and time can replace (mature-node capacity, etch and deposition tools) are being replaced, and layers that depend on a single foreign supplier's learning curve (EUV, HBM, resist, EDA) are not.

**Logic**: SMIC's **N+2** process (7 nm-class; N+1, N+2 and N+3 are SMIC's names for its successive DUV-only advanced nodes) runs DUV-only with **quadruple patterning** (one layer's pattern split across four exposures) at critical layers. It appeared in the Huawei Kirin 9000S in the Mate 60 Pro in August 2023, a year after the October 2022 rules, and TechInsights confirmed its dimensions. The follow-on Kirin 9020 (late 2024) was found to be a refined N+2 rather than a true 5 nm node, and SMIC's N+3 "5 nm-class" process, reported in 2025 in some Huawei Ascend and Kirin parts, relies on further DUV multi-patterning at yields analysts put well below 50% (some reports cite ~20–30%), with costs per good die several times TSMC's. Without EUV, each further shrink adds mask layers (Module 07) at rising cost and falling yield, so SMIC can reach 5 nm-class dimensions but not 5 nm economics, and 3 nm-class is not credible on DUV. SMIC's 7 nm-class capacity was estimated at ~30,000–45,000 wspm in 2025, much of it for Huawei.

The mechanism: one EUV exposure prints a fine layer in a single pass. To print it with 193 nm DUV, the pattern is split into four coarser patterns exposed separately, each with its own resist coat, develop, etch, strip and clean, so one EUV layer becomes ~8–10 extra process steps, four masks instead of one, and days of extra cycle time in a flow that already takes ~3 months. Every added step is a chance for a defect, and every added exposure must align to the previous one within a few nanometres. If each extra pass costs ~2–5% of the wafer's dies, then over the ~20 extra passes a 5 nm-class flow needs, (1 − 0.02)<sup>20</sup> ≈ 0.67 and (1 − 0.05)<sup>20</sup> ≈ 0.36: multipatterning alone removes a third to two-thirds of the wafer before any other yield loss. That is the arithmetic behind "well below 50%", and it worsens each node as more layers need splitting.

> **Worked example: cost per good die on DUV-only 5 nm (hedged estimate).**
> Every input below is an estimate marked "~"; the point is the ratio, not the dollars. Take a die with ~100 candidate sites per 300 mm wafer.
> TSMC N5: manufacturing cost is the wafer price less the gross margin, ~$16,000 × (1 − 0.58) ≈ ~$6,700 per wafer, at a mature yield of ~80%: ~$6,700 ÷ (100 × 0.8) ≈ ~$84 per good die. At TSMC's selling price the customer pays ~$16,000 ÷ 80 ≈ ~$200 per good die.
> SMIC N+3: a DUV-only 5 nm-class wafer with quadruple patterning has more steps and more masks than N5, so its cost is at least ~$12,000-class even before the fixed cost of a fab that runs far below capacity; at ~30% yield: ~$12,000 ÷ (100 × 0.3) ≈ ~$400 per good die.
> Ratio: ~$400 ÷ ~$84 ≈ 4–5× TSMC's manufacturing cost per good die, and ~2× what TSMC's customers actually pay after TSMC's margin. Move the yield to ~20% and the ratio at cost is ~7×.
> Why this caps volume: at ~30% yield each good die consumes more than three wafer starts, so SMIC's ~30,000–45,000 wspm of 7 nm-class capacity delivers the good-die output of a ~10,000–15,000 wspm TSMC line, and each of those wafers is one not made on the mature nodes that earn SMIC's actual margin. Only products the state will pay for regardless of cost (Huawei's Kirin phones, the Ascend accelerators) can absorb a ~$400 die, which is why the output is tens of thousands of wafers a month for a short list of customers rather than a merchant business.

**AI accelerators**: Huawei's Ascend 910B (SMIC N+2, ~280–350 TFLOPS **BF16**, a 16-bit number format used for AI arithmetic, HBM2E-class memory) and 910C (two 910B dies in one package, roughly H100-class peak but constrained by HBM supply and interconnect) shipped on the order of a few hundred thousand units in 2024–25, with Huawei's September 2025 roadmap announcing the Ascend 950/960/970 series with in-house HBM through 2028. The binding constraints are HBM (China has no volume HBM producer, and the December 2024 rule cut off Samsung's HBM2E exports; CXMT's HBM3 was in sampling as of 2025), SMIC capacity, and CoWoS-class packaging, which Huawei has sourced from domestic OSATs at lower density.

**Memory**: CXMT (ChangXin Memory) reached DDR5 and LPDDR5X on a ~17–18 nm-class node, with capacity estimated at ~200,000+ wspm and ~10–15% of global DRAM wafer capacity by 2025 (though a smaller bit share), and was preparing an IPO; YMTC shipped 232-layer NAND in 2022 and a ~270-layer generation in 2024 using **string stacking** (building a tall 3D NAND stack as two half-height stacks made in sequence, so each etch is shallower, Module 15) and domestic tools where possible, despite Entity Listing since December 2022. Both operate at lower yields and higher cost than the incumbents but are commercially real and have pushed down prices at the low end of the market.

**Equipment**: Naura (~¥30 billion revenue in 2024; etch, deposition, clean, furnaces), AMEC (etch, including **capacitor etch**, the deep narrow hole etch that forms a DRAM cell's storage capacitor, for DRAM; sold to TSMC before the controls), Piotech (deposition), ACM Research (clean), Hwatsing (CMP), and SiCarrier (a Shenzhen state-backed firm linked to Huawei that unveiled a broad tool line in 2025) cover deposition, etch, clean and CMP at 28 nm and increasingly at 14/7 nm. Domestic tools were ~15–25% of Chinese fab purchases in 2025, up from ~5% in 2019. Lithography is the gap: SMEE's SSA800 **ArF** immersion scanner (ArF is the argon fluoride laser that produces 193 nm light; nominal 28 nm, with ~90 nm-class **overlay** claims, overlay being how accurately a layer lines up on the one below it; a 2 nm process needs ~1–2 nm, so 90 nm-class is 28 nm-era performance) is not in volume production, and reports of a Huawei-led EUV effort using a **laser-induced discharge plasma (LDP)** source (a cheaper and dimmer way of making EUV light than ASML's laser-produced plasma, Module 08; prototype claims in 2025) remain unverified. Metrology, resist (China relies on JSR, TOK, Shin-Etsu for ArF and EUV resist), and EDA (**Empyrean** and **Primarius**, China's EDA startups, are far behind Synopsys/Cadence) are the other soft spots.

**Money**: the National Integrated Circuit Industry Investment Fund ("Big Fund") raised ~¥139 billion (2014), ~¥204 billion (2019), and ¥344 billion (~$47.5 billion, May 2024) for its third phase, on top of provincial funds, subsidized loans, and tax holidays; total state support for the sector is estimated at over $150 billion since 2014. China bought ~$50 billion of semiconductor equipment in 2024 (SEMI, all types), ~42% of the world total and the largest single market for ASML, AMAT, Lam, and TEL; in 2025 it spent a flat ~$49 billion, which fell to ~36% of a larger world total as Taiwan and Korea spending surged. Chinese 300 mm capacity is on track to be ~25% of the world's in 2026 (SEMI) and roughly a third of mature-node capacity by 2027, almost all at 28 nm and above, which is beginning to depress mature-node pricing worldwide.

**Counter-controls**: China restricted gallium and germanium exports (July 2023; ~98% and ~60% of world supply respectively), graphite (Dec 2023), antimony (Aug 2024), banned gallium/germanium/antimony to the US outright (Dec 2024), controlled seven medium and heavy rare earths (April 2025), and on October 9, 2025 announced sweeping rare-earth controls with an extraterritorial clause covering foreign products containing Chinese rare-earth content, then suspended those October measures for one year in November 2025 under the trade truce, issuing general licenses for US end users (as of late 2025). China also opened antitrust probes into NVIDIA (2024–25) and Qualcomm (October 2025) and an anti-dumping probe into US analog chips (Sept 2025). Gallium matters for GaN and GaAs (Module 04), germanium for SiGe and infrared optics; neither stops a silicon fab, but both raise costs for the compound-semiconductor and defense sub-tiers.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The three tool thresholds in the October 2022 rule (logic at ≤16/14 nm with non-planar transistors, DRAM at ≤18 nm half-pitch, NAND at ≥128 layers) map directly onto process steps in Modules 07–12. "Non-planar" means FinFET and GAA (Module 11), so the rule bites at fin and gate patterning, the self-aligned multipatterning of Module 07 and the ALD gate stacks of Module 06; anything SMIC does there must run on tools it already owned, older immersion scanners it may still buy, or Naura and AMEC chambers. For CXMT the 18 nm line falls on the capacitor etch and the finest DUV layers of the DRAM flow (Module 15); for YMTC the 128-layer line falls on the high-aspect-ratio channel etch (Module 09) that Lam and TEL dominate, which is why string stacking, two shallower etches instead of one deep one, is an engineering response to a legal threshold.

**United States, CHIPS and Science Act (Aug 2022)**: $39 billion of manufacturing grants, $11 billion for R&D (the National Semiconductor Technology Center, run by **Natcast**, the non-profit that operates it, and packaging programs), and the Section 48D **advanced manufacturing investment credit**, 25% of qualifying fab and equipment investment, raised to 35% by the July 2025 tax law for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, and available for projects that begin construction before the end of 2026. Final grant awards (Dec 2024 and after) included Intel $7.86 billion (plus $3 billion Secure Enclave; the undisbursed portion became equity in 2025), TSMC $6.6 billion, Micron $6.1 billion (Idaho and New York) plus $275 million (Virginia), Samsung $4.745 billion, Texas Instruments $1.6 billion, GlobalFoundries $1.5 billion, SK hynix $458 million (Indiana HBM packaging), and Amkor $407 million (Arizona). SIA estimates the act catalyzed ~$450 billion of announced private investment and would triple US fab capacity by 2032. The 2025 administration criticized the grants, renegotiated some, and shifted toward the tax credit, tariffs (a ~100% chip tariff threatened in August 2025, with exemptions for companies building in the US), and equity stakes as instruments.

**European Union, Chips Act (Sept 2023)**: a €43 billion headline of mostly national money aimed at doubling the EU's share of world production from ~10% to 20% by 2030, a target nobody in the industry expects to be met. Its main fruits are ESMC Dresden and Infineon's €5 billion Smart Power Fab in Dresden (2026); Intel's Magdeburg fab was cancelled and the STMicro/GlobalFoundries Crolles expansion was shelved, and a "Chips Act 2.0" was under discussion in 2025–26.

**Japan**: the most effective program per yen. METI committed ~¥4 trillion (~$26 billion) in 2021–2025, funding JASM 1 and 2 (¥1.2 trillion combined), Micron's Hiroshima 1γ EUV fab (¥536 billion), Kioxia/Western Digital's Kitakami NAND fab, and **Rapidus**, the 2022 startup backed by Toyota, Sony, NTT, SoftBank, Kioxia, Denso, and NEC that licensed IBM's 2 nm **nanosheet** process (nanosheet is the same thing as gate-all-around, Module 11) and built the IIM-1 fab in Chitose, Hokkaido. Rapidus opened its pilot line in April 2025 with EUV tools installed, reported working 2 nm GAA test devices in mid-2025, and targets mass production in the second half of fiscal 2027 at an initial ~6,000 wspm rising to ~20,000–25,000; cumulative public development support reached ~¥2.35 trillion with the FY2026 allocation (¥630 billion approved June 2026), including ¥100 billion of government equity via the **IPA** (Japan's Information-technology Promotion Agency, the vehicle for the state's shareholding) in a ¥267.6 billion round closed in February 2026 (as of mid-2026). Its unproven elements are yield on a first-generation GAA process, a customer base, and a **single-wafer-processing** cycle-time model (running every step one wafer at a time instead of in 25-wafer batches, to cut the ~3-month cycle time toward weeks) that no volume fab has used at the leading edge.

**South Korea**: the K-Chips Act raised the investment tax credit for large firms to 15% (2023) and to 20% in the 2025 amendment (30% for SMEs), alongside a ₩26 trillion support package and the Yongin mega-cluster, a ₩600+ trillion plan for SK hynix (four fabs from 2027) and Samsung (six fabs) that is the largest fab complex ever planned and is gated by power and water supply.

**India**: the India Semiconductor Mission offers 50% capital subsidies; Tata Electronics and Taiwan's PSMC are building a ~$11 billion, 50,000 wspm fab at Dholera, Gujarat (28–110 nm, first output targeted ~2026–27), Micron opened an assembly and test plant in Sanand, and Tata, CG Power/Renesas, and Kaynes are building OSATs. India's realistic 2030 role is back-end and mature-node.

**Taiwan** itself responded with a 25% R&D tax credit (2023), an **N-1 rule** requiring overseas fabs to run at least one generation behind Taiwan (which TSMC's plan to bring N2 to Arizona by ~2028–29 tests), and export controls on Huawei and SMIC (June 2025).

What the record shows, four years in, is that money buys fabs but not customers or people. The projects producing wafers (Arizona Phase 1, JASM 1) had an anchor customer and an imported workforce before ground was broken; the ones that stalled (Taylor, Ohio, JASM 2, Magdeburg) lacked one or both. Subsidy lowers the capex line in the worked example above and does nothing to the utilization line, which decides whether the fab makes money, or to the training pipeline discussed under Workforce below.

## What If: Single Points of Failure

The chain has a dozen places where one site, one company, or one mine is the only source. A useful exercise is to ask, for each, how long the world could run without it. Terms used in the table: **CZ** is Czochralski crystal pulling (Module 02), where the crucible's inner layer touches the molten silicon, so its purity sets the crystal's; **actinic** means inspected with the same 13.5 nm wavelength the scanner uses, so the inspection sees exactly the defects the scanner would print; **NF3** is nitrogen trifluoride, the gas used to clean deposition chambers, and **WF6** is tungsten hexafluoride, the source of the tungsten in every contact plug; **pm** is a picometre, a thousandth of a nanometre; **e-beam** inspection scans the mask with an electron beam rather than light.

| Single point | What it is | Alternatives | Time to recover if lost |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASML Veldhoven | Sole final assembly of every EUV scanner (~40–50 a year) and most DUV | None; Nikon and Canon make no EUV and little immersion DUV | Years; ~10-year lead to replicate |
| Zeiss SMT, Oberkochen | Sole source of EUV projection optics and illuminators, polished to ~50 pm | None | Years; the optics know-how is irreplaceable on any planning horizon |
| Cymer (San Diego) and Trumpf (Ditzingen) | EUV source and the 30 kW CO2 drive laser | None | Years |
| Spruce Pine, North Carolina | Most of the world's high-purity quartz for CZ crucible inner layers | Chinese synthetic quartz and lower-grade deposits, with yield penalties | Weeks (Hurricane Helene, Sept 2024, closed the mines ~2 weeks; inventories covered it) to months |
| Ajinomoto ABF | ~95%+ of build-up film for every high-end substrate | Sekisui, Taiyo at small scale | Months to years of qualification |
| Hoya, AGC | EUV mask blanks | Each other | Months |
| Lasertec | Sole actinic (EUV-wavelength) blank and mask inspection | KLA's EUV inspection is e-beam and complementary, not a substitute | Years |
| Shin-Etsu, SUMCO | ~55% of 300 mm wafers | GlobalWafers, Siltronic, SK Siltron | Months of shortage |
| TSMC Fab 18, Tainan | The N5/N4/N3 site (~200,000+ wspm) | Fab 20 (Hsinchu), Fab 21 (Arizona), Fab 22 (Kaohsiung) for N2; nothing for the N3 volume | A multi-week outage removes most Apple, NVIDIA, and AMD wafer supply for the period; an extended one takes ~1–2 years to replace |
| Taiwan Strait | Shipping of wafers, chemicals, and gases | None for Taiwan-based capacity | Global leading-edge output down ~90% for the duration plus 1–2 years |
| Neon, helium, tungsten hexafluoride | Ukraine supplied ~50% of semiconductor neon before 2022; NF3 and WF6 are made by a few firms | Chinese and Korean neon (now dominant); Kanto Denka, SK Specialty | Months (2022 neon prices rose ~10× and then normalized) |
| KYEC / ASE test capacity | Test for NVIDIA-class parts | Amkor, in-house | Months |

The recovery times are reasoned from one rule. When a product has a single supplier because the market cannot pay for two, the time to replace that supplier is roughly the time it took to develop the product, because no second firm has walked any of the learning curve: Zeiss's EUV optics took ~15 years of development before the first production scanner, so losing Oberkochen means something like that long to rebuild, and the market (~50 optics sets a year) would still support only one supplier at the end. Where alternatives exist but are unqualified (ABF, mask blanks, wafers), recovery time is qualification time, months to a couple of years. Where the alternative is a commodity with spare capacity elsewhere (neon, quartz), recovery is the time to switch logistics and run down inventories, weeks to months. The pattern across the table is that the most concentrated points are in optics and materials, in Germany, the Netherlands, and Japan rather than in Taiwan, and that they are concentrated not by policy but by economics: the market for EUV optics is ~50 sets a year, which supports exactly one supplier.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Trace a six-month outage at Ajinomoto's ABF plants (Module 16) down the chain. ABF is the insulating film laminated in layers to build the substrate under every high-end CPU, GPU and network chip, and no other film is qualified in the substrate makers' lines at scale. Month one: Ibiden, Unimicron and Shinko run on the few weeks of film in inventory, and finished CoWoS packages keep shipping. Month two: substrate output stops, finished GPU dies and HBM stacks pile up with nothing to mount them on, and NVIDIA's revenue, booked when a board ships, stops with them. Months three to six: Sekisui and Taiyo films are qualified in every substrate line at once, normally the work of a year, while hyperscalers ration what packages exist. The tool makers feel it last and hardest (Idea 5): with wafers piling up unpackaged, foundries delay tool deliveries and ASML's and Lam's order books fall by a multiple of the chip shortfall. A film costing a few dollars per package would have stalled a large part of the ~$1 trillion of 2026 AI infrastructure spending for half a year, which is the argument for keeping second-source films in slow qualification as insurance.

## Workforce and Talent

People gate diversification more than money does. A fab's tools can be bought in two years and its building poured in one, but the several thousand people who run it take 12–18 months each to train, the few who can bring up a new node cannot be hired at all, and every program in this module that fell behind schedule was short of one of those two groups.

A 50,000 wspm leading-edge fab employs roughly ~3,000 people, in an illustrative split:

| Role | Rough headcount (50,000 wspm) | What they do | Time to competence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operators and shift technicians | ~1,000–1,500 | Run the line: move lots, answer tool alarms, load reticles and chemicals | ~3–6 months |
| Equipment technicians | ~600–800 | Keep each tool running: maintenance, part swaps, chamber cleans, recovery after trips | ~6–12 months per tool type |
| Process engineers | ~300–500 | Own one step (an etch, a deposition, a litho layer): recipe, control limits, excursions | ~12–18 months |
| Integration engineers | ~100–200 | Own the whole flow: how a change at one step moves every later step | Years |
| Yield and device engineers | ~50–100 | Find why dies fail: wafer maps, electrical data, failure analysis (Module 13) | Years |
| Facilities, IT, automation, planning | ~300–500 | Ultrapure water, gases, exhaust, lot-routing software | ~6–12 months |

The 12–18 months is not classroom time. A process engineer learns a step by watching it drift and running the experiments that bring it back, which only happens on a running line with real excursions, which is why TSMC trains Arizona hires in Taiwan and then pairs them with expatriates. Node bring-up is scarcer still: a process node is tens of thousands of parameter settings (temperatures, pressures, gas flows, doses, etch times) learned on tens of thousands of test wafers over three or four years, and the reasons behind them live in the heads of the integration engineers who found them, not in documents; copy exactly works because those people fly to the new site and check every deviation. The scarcest single population is the few thousand people worldwide who can bring up a new process node, split among TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK hynix, Micron, and the research ecosystems at **imec** (the Belgian research institute in Leuven where the industry and its tool and material makers co-develop next-generation processes on shared pilot lines) and IBM.

The numbers behind the shortage: TSMC employs ~83,000 people (2024), ~10% non-Taiwanese, and its Taiwan engineers accept working conditions (on-call shifts, ~12-hour days during ramps) that Arizona's did not; the resulting culture clash delayed Phase 1 by about a year, and TSMC ended up sending ~1,500 Taiwanese engineers to Arizona and training ~600 American hires in Taiwan for 12–18 months each. SIA and Oxford Economics projected in 2023 that the US would face ~67,000 unfilled semiconductor jobs by 2030 (of ~115,000 new positions), roughly 40% technicians and 30% engineers; Intel Ohio's delays, Samsung Taylor's idle years, and Micron's slowed New York ramp are partly staffing stories. JASM's starting salaries pulled staff from local Kumamoto firms, and Rapidus sent ~200 engineers to IBM's Albany NanoTech center for two years to learn 2 nm. In Taiwan the constraint is demographic: the island produces on the order of ~10,000 electrical engineering graduates a year against TSMC alone hiring ~6,000–8,000, so every overseas fab staffed with Taiwanese engineers draws on a pool that is not growing.

## How to Read Industry Data

Because so much of this module is estimates, knowing the sources and their biases is a skill in itself. Two terms: a **10-K** is the annual report every US-listed company must file with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and its risk-factor section is where export-control exposure is quantified; a **three-month moving average** replaces each month's figure with the average of the last three, which smooths noise but delays turning points by about a month.

- **WSTS** (via SIA, monthly with a three-month moving average, and semi-annual forecasts): the canonical chip revenue series, built from member company reports; it undercounts Chinese vendors and captive production (Apple, Google), and its forecasts lag turns.
- **SEMI**: equipment billings (monthly, from member reports), the World Fab Forecast (fab-by-fab capacity database, subscription), materials market reports, and the annual silicon wafer shipment series. The best source for capacity by region and node.
- **TrendForce** (Taiwan): quarterly foundry and DRAM/NAND market share rankings, contract price indices, and CoWoS/HBM capacity estimates; its numbers are the ones cited in most news reports, and its price data are the industry standard.
- **SemiAnalysis**: bottom-up cost models, accelerator shipment estimates, and fab and packaging capacity tracking; paid, opinionated, and usually the most detailed public source on the AI supply chain.
- **TechInsights**: teardowns and die analysis; the source of the SMIC 7 nm confirmation, the Huawei die findings, and transistor density measurements.
- **IBS** (International Business Strategies) and **IC Insights** (now part of TechInsights): design cost and wafer cost per node.
- **Company filings**: TSMC's quarterly management report gives revenue by node, platform and region, capex and capacity guidance, and margin bridges; ASML's report gives EUV unit shipments and China share; NVIDIA's 10-K gives customer concentration (two direct customers above 10% of revenue) and inventory purchase commitments (~$50 billion+ in 2025, a leading indicator of CoWoS bookings); Micron's and SK hynix's give HBM revenue trends; Applied's and Lam's give China revenue share. The 10-K risk-factor sections are where export-control exposure is quantified.
- **Government sources**: BIS Federal Register notices (the actual rule text, which is what companies comply with, not press summaries), the Commerce CHIPS Program Office award announcements, METI and the European Commission for their programs, and Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs for local statistics.

> **What can go wrong:** Misreading the data. Three errors account for most wrong conclusions about this industry. First, revenue versus wafers versus bits: TSMC's foundry share is ~67% by revenue and much lower by wafers because its wafers are expensive; CXMT's ~10–15% of DRAM wafer capacity is a smaller share of bits because its node is older; Korea's ~70% of DRAM is by revenue. Ask which unit before comparing. Second, installed versus planned versus announced capacity: "TSMC will have 130,000 wpm of CoWoS" is a year-end target, not a running line, and the $165 billion Arizona commitment includes fabs with no start date; count only capacity with tools installed and qualified, and treat announcements as options the company may never exercise. Third, fiscal-year mismatch: TSMC reports on calendar years, NVIDIA's fiscal year ends in late January (FY2026 is mostly calendar 2025), and Micron's ends in late August, so re-date every figure to calendar quarters before dividing one by another.

## Summary

- Chips were a ~$792 billion business in 2025; equipment (~$135 billion) and materials ($73.2 billion) are fractions of it, so tool makers live on the second derivative of chip demand: orders move with the change in expected growth, not with sales.
- Every layer is concentrated because qualification makes switching suppliers cost months; equipment and EDA earn the highest gross margins, OSATs the lowest.
- The map: design American, lithography Dutch and German, materials Japanese, memory Korean, leading-edge logic Taiwanese, largest market Chinese; no region can build a leading-edge chip alone.
- A 2 nm fab costs ~$20–30 billion, ~80% of it tools depreciated over 5 years; depreciation alone is ~$8,000 per wafer at 90% utilization and ~$14,000 at 50%, which is why utilization, not price, sets foundry margins and why foundries discount old nodes rather than idle them.
- Cost per transistor has been roughly flat since 28 nm because wafer price rises ~1.5–1.8× per node while density rises ~1.5–1.7×, and SRAM, analog and I/O do not shrink at all; Moore's law survives as transistors per package, paid for with chiplets and larger packages.
- Rock's law (fab cost doubling every ~4 years) is why only TSMC, Samsung and Intel remain at the leading edge, and TSMC's volume flywheel keeps widening its lead.
- Memory is a commodity built with 2–3-year capacity lags, so it swings between 50–70% price collapses and doublings; the 2025–26 spike came from HBM's 2–3× wafer intensity diverting 1β/1γ DRAM capacity away from commodity parts.
- AI demand is one line item, hyperscaler capex (~$400 billion in 2025, ~$720–745 billion guided for 2026), and the binding constraints have been CoWoS, then HBM, then power; front-end wafers for an entire GPU generation are ~1–2% of TSMC's output.
- Taiwan makes ~90% of ≤5 nm-class logic and ~90%+ of CoWoS-class packaging; the April 2024 earthquake cost ~0.4–0.5% of a 2024 quarter's revenue; a blockade would idle the fabs within weeks by starving them of consumables without damaging them, and a war would remove ~90% of leading-edge output for years.
- Diversification is real but partial: Arizona N4 in volume, N3 in 2027, N2 ~2028–29, eventually ~30% of TSMC's 2 nm-and-beyond capacity; after the 35% credit and grants a US fab's net capex is below Taiwan's unsubsidized figure, and the lasting penalties are ~5–20% per wafer, talent, and a one-node lag.
- US export controls work through ECCN thresholds (TPP ≥ 4,800, or ≥ 1,600 with density ≥ 5.92 TPP/mm²; H100 ≈ 15,800, H20 ≈ 2,370), the Entity List, the Foreign Direct Product Rule and tool controls coordinated with the Netherlands and Japan; tool controls bind because a scanner cannot be smuggled or serviced covertly.
- China can make 7 nm- and 5 nm-class chips on DUV multipatterning at ~4–5× TSMC's cost per good die and a fraction of its yield; it has no EUV, no volume HBM and no CoWoS-class packaging at scale, but ~25% of world 300 mm capacity at mature nodes.
- The sole-source points that would take longest to replace (Zeiss optics, ASML, Lasertec, Ajinomoto ABF) sit in Germany, the Netherlands and Japan, concentrated by economics, not policy: a market of ~50 optics sets a year cannot support a second supplier.
- People, not money, gate every diversification program: a node bring-up team is a few thousand people worldwide, and a new fab's ~3,000 staff take 12–18 months to train on a running line.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Export controls have failed because Huawei made a 7 nm phone chip."** → The 2022–24 controls targeted AI compute at scale, not the existence of a 7 nm die. SMIC can make 7 nm-class and even 5 nm-class parts on DUV, but at yields and costs that limit volume to tens of thousands of wafers a month, and China still has no EUV, no volume HBM, and no CoWoS-class packaging at scale. The controls slowed China by an estimated several years at the leading edge while accelerating its mature-node and tool self-sufficiency, which is a mixed result, not a null one.
- **"TSMC has 90% market share."** → It has ~67–70% of foundry revenue and ~90%+ of wafers at 5 nm-class and below. The two numbers answer different questions, and confusing them exaggerates TSMC's position in mature nodes (where China and UMC are large) and understates it at the leading edge.
- **"Moving production to the US is uneconomic because US wafers cost 50% more."** → Construction cost is ~1.5× or more, but tools (~80% of capex) cost the same everywhere, and after the 35% tax credit and grants a US fab's net capex is below Taiwan's unsubsidized figure. The persistent operating penalty is ~5–20% per wafer, which customers seeking US origin have been willing to pay. The real barriers are talent, cycle time, and TSMC's preference to develop each node at home.
- **"Cost per transistor is still falling ~30% a year."** → It has been roughly flat since ~28 nm. Density still rises ~1.5–1.7× per node, but wafer price rises nearly as fast, SRAM has stopped scaling, and design costs have reached $500 million or more per chip at 3 nm. Moore's law survives as transistors per package, financed by chiplets and larger packages, not as cheaper transistors.
- **"The AI constraint is GPU die supply from TSMC's fabs."** → Front-end wafer capacity has exceeded accelerator demand throughout; a whole GPU generation's compute dies are ~1–2% of TSMC's wafer output. The bottlenecks have been CoWoS packaging (2023–25), HBM (2025–26), and increasingly data-center power and grid interconnection.
- **"A fab is worth building anywhere with enough subsidy."** → Subsidies reduce capex, but the fab's economics are set by utilization: at 50% loading a leading-edge fab's gross margin roughly halves. Fabs without customers (Samsung Taylor 2022–24, Intel's external foundry, JASM 2's delays) are the recurring failure mode of industrial policy, not construction cost.
- **"China's ~$50 billion a year of tool purchases proves the controls leak."** → Almost all of it is deposition, etch, clean and older lithography tools for 28 nm-and-above fabs, which the rules deliberately allow; the controlled items (EUV, the most capable immersion DUV, advanced-node etch and deposition, HBM) are what China is not buying. The controls redirected China's spending; they did not fail to stop it.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 19 ended with an NVIDIA rack leaving a Foxconn or Quanta line, the physical terminus of the chain that began with quartz in Module 01. This module steps back from the material flow to the money and power that organize it: the ~$790 billion of 2025 chip revenue that funds ~$117 billion of wafer-fab-equipment purchases from ASML, Applied, Lam, TEL, and KLA and $73.2 billion of materials with substantial Japanese supplier participation; the fab capex arithmetic that dictates which three companies can afford the 2 nm node; the geographic concentration in Taiwan, Korea, Japan, and the Netherlands that makes the chain both efficient and fragile; and the export-control and subsidy regimes that governments have built since 2019 to redirect where the next $500 billion of fabs will go. Every upstream module's supplier list (Shin-Etsu's wafers, Zeiss's optics, Ajinomoto's ABF, SK hynix's HBM) reappears here as a single point of failure with a policy attached. Module 21 closes the course with the glossary, the node table, and the full ordered process flow that this module's economics sit on top of.
