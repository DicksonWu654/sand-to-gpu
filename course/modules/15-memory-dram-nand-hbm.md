# Module 15: Memory: DRAM, NAND and HBM Manufacturing

A powerful GPU can still spend time waiting if its data does not arrive fast enough. A Blackwell B200 GPU can move roughly 8 TB/s of data (8 terabytes, 8 × 10¹² bytes, every second). **DRAM (dynamic random-access memory)** is the working memory used while a computer is running. No single DRAM chip on a circuit board can feed that stream. A **DIMM (dual in-line memory module)**, the stick of memory chips that plugs into a server board, delivers ~50–70 GB/s as **DDR5** (the fifth generation of the standard DRAM interface); you would need well over a hundred of them, and the power spent just moving bits across the board would exceed the power of the GPU itself.

To supply that stream, ~180–290 GB of DRAM sits within a few millimetres of the compute die, stacked eight, twelve or sixteen dies high and wired through with thousands of vertical copper **vias** (a via is a hole through a chip filled with copper, so that a signal can travel vertically through the silicon instead of out to the chip's edge). That product, **HBM (High Bandwidth Memory)**, is the single most supply-constrained component of the AI build-out after **CoWoS** packaging (TSMC's process for placing a GPU and its memory stacks side by side on a slab of silicon, Module 17), and it is made by a manufacturing flow that starts as ordinary DRAM and then becomes something closer to advanced packaging than to chipmaking.

This module covers the three memory technologies that matter for the GPU story. **DRAM (Dynamic Random-Access Memory)** is the working memory: one transistor, one capacitor, refreshed every few tens of milliseconds. **NAND flash** is the persistent storage: a charge-trapping transistor, now built as a vertical string hundreds of layers tall. **HBM** is DRAM re-engineered for stacking. In memory the **cell**, the smallest repeating structure that stores one bit (in DRAM one transistor plus one capacitor; in NAND one transistor whose gate holds trapped electrons), is the unit of design, not the transistor; cost per bit, not performance per watt, is the metric; and three companies, Samsung, SK hynix and Micron, make ~90% of the world's DRAM (as of 2025).

By the end you will be able to explain why a DRAM capacitor is ~1.2 µm tall and only ~20–35 nm wide; why 3D NAND is deposited as ~300 sacrificial layers and then carved rather than patterned; how an HBM stack is drilled, thinned to 30 µm and stacked; and why HBM costs 3–5× as much per bit as DDR5 and eats 2–3 wafers for every wafer of DDR5 bits.

## Before you start

- **A transistor is a switch** (Module 11). Current flows from its **source** terminal to its **drain** terminal only when the **gate** terminal is at least the **threshold voltage (Vt)** above the source; below Vt it is off, though never perfectly off.
- **A capacitor** (school physics) is two conductors separated by an insulator. The stored charge is Q = C × V, where C, the capacitance, rises with electrode area and with the insulator's dielectric constant and falls with insulator thickness. 1 fF (femtofarad) = 10⁻¹⁵ farad.
- **Pitch and half-pitch** (Modules 07, 08). Pitch is the centre-to-centre spacing of repeating lines; the half-pitch F is half of it, the line width when line equals space. Multi-patterning (**SAQP**, self-aligned quadruple patterning: print lines, then grow sidewall spacers twice to quadruple the line density) and **EUV** (13.5 nm light) are the two ways to print pitches below ~40 nm.
- **Deposition** (Module 06). **CVD/PECVD** grows films fast from reacting gases (plasma-assisted for PECVD); **ALD (atomic layer deposition)** grows them one atomic layer per gas cycle, so every surface, even the bottom of a deep hole, gets the same thickness.
- **Plasma etch** (Module 09). Ions accelerated straight down cut **anisotropic** (directional) holes. **Aspect ratio** is depth divided by width; **selectivity** is how much faster the target material etches than its neighbours; a **hard mask** is a tough film that carries the pattern into a deep etch that photoresist would not survive.
- **Copper wiring (BEOL)** (Module 12). The **back end of line** is the stack of copper wiring levels above the transistors, made by electroplating copper into holes and trenches and polishing the surface flat with **CMP** (chemical-mechanical polishing: grinding the wafer flat with a pad and a chemical slurry).
- **Yield and the gross-die formula** (Module 13). How many dies fit on a 300 mm wafer and what fraction of them work.
- **Wafer sort and known-good die** (Module 14). Every die is electrically tested before it is packaged; a bad die stacked into a multi-die package scraps the good ones with it.
- **Quantum tunnelling** (school physics reminder, used for NAND). An electron can cross a thin insulator it classically could not, with a probability that rises exponentially with the electric field across the insulator: negligible at ~0.5 V/nm, large at ~1 V/nm.
- **Thermal budget and thermal expansion** (Modules 06, 12). Metals and dopant profiles degrade above certain temperatures, so every step that comes after them is limited in time-at-temperature; and materials expand at different rates when heated (copper ~6× more than silicon per degree).


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## 1. Why memory is a different business from logic

A logic fab (Modules 05–13) builds ~15–18 **metal layers** (the stacked wiring levels above the transistors) on top of a few hundred million transistors per mm², every one of which can be different. A DRAM fab builds a single, ferociously optimized structure, the memory cell, repeated tens of billions of times, and then wraps it in a modest amount of peripheral **CMOS** (the ordinary logic-transistor process). A 16 Gb DDR5 die has ~17.2 billion cells and only 3–4 metal layers. The cell array occupies 55–65% of the die; the rest is the **periphery**, the support circuitry around the array: **sense amplifiers** that detect the tiny stored signal, **decoders** that pick which row and column to access, **I/O** drivers that talk to the outside world, and the **seal ring**, a wall of metal around the die edge that keeps moisture out.

> **Intuition: a stadium, not a city.** Picture a DRAM die as a football stadium: tens of thousands of identical seats in identical rows (the array) with a small press box (the periphery) that decides which seat to look at. A logic die is a city: every building is different, and the streets between them (the 15–18 metal layers) are most of the construction. Everything about a memory fab follows from building seats, not buildings.

The consequences run through everything in this module:

- **Cost per bit is the only score.** DRAM and NAND are commodities with published spot prices. A 10% shrink in cell area that costs 12% more process steps is a loss. This is why memory makers adopted EUV years after TSMC, and why 3D NAND, which grows vertically with the same lithography, replaced planar NAND rather than shrinking it further.
- **The array is a periodic structure.** A **grating**, a pattern of identical parallel lines at one pitch, is what lithography does best: a single repeating pitch can be printed by interference-like patterns and multiplied by spacers, whereas the random shapes of logic cannot. The DRAM cell array is patterned at pitches (~25–30 nm word-line pitch, which is 2F in the cell layout of Section 3) that logic fabs only reach with SAQP or EUV, but with far simpler shapes.
- **The critical tools are different.** A leading-edge DRAM fab is dominated by ALD and **high-aspect-ratio (HAR)** dielectric etch, because the storage capacitor is a hole 40 times deeper than it is wide (a drinking straw 6 mm wide would have to be 24 cm long to match); a 3D NAND fab is dominated by PECVD (for the 300-layer oxide/nitride stack) and HAR etch (for the channel holes). Neither has much use for a 15-layer copper BEOL.
- **Volume dwarfs logic.** Industry DRAM **wafer starts** (wafers entering the line per month) are on the order of 1.6–1.9 million 300 mm wafers per month (as of ~2025), NAND similar; TSMC's total across all nodes is ~1.3–1.4 million.

## 2. The 1T1C cell and the sense amplifier

DRAM is the first of the three technologies, and Sections 2 to 8 walk through it from the cell upward: how a bit is stored and read, the transistor, the capacitor, the periphery, node naming and EUV, the roadmap, and the players.

The problem a DRAM cell solves is storing a bit as cheaply as possible. The static memory inside a processor (**SRAM**) holds a bit in a loop of six transistors, which is fast but large. A compact way to store a bit is to use two distinguishable charge states of a capacitor. One transistor acts as the switch that connects it to the outside world. The familiar picture of a full or empty bucket is useful, but the real cell is read by detecting a voltage difference, not by counting out its electrons. There are two problems to solve. Charge leaks through the switch and the insulator, so the state must be periodically restored. Reading also shares charge with a wire and disturbs the original state; it does not simply empty the capacitor. The sensing circuit must recover the bit and restore it before disconnecting the cell. Almost everything in DRAM design exists to manage those two problems.

A DRAM bit is stored as charge on a capacitor, gated by a single **access transistor**: the **1T1C cell** (one transistor, one capacitor). The transistor's gate is the **word line (WL)**, the wire that opens every switch in one row; its drain is the **bit line (BL)**, the wire that carries the data in and out of one column; its source is the capacitor's bottom electrode (the **storage node**). The capacitor's top electrode is a common **plate** held at **VDD/2**, half the chip's supply voltage **VDD** (~1.1 V for DDR5). Holding the plate at the midpoint means the thin capacitor insulator only ever sees ±VDD/2 rather than the full VDD, halving the electrical stress on it. A 12 fF cell charged to 0.55 V holds Q = C × V ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ coulombs, which is ~40,000 electrons: that is one bit.

To read a cell, the bit line is first **precharged** to VDD/2 and left **floating** (disconnected from every driver, so its voltage is set only by the charge sitting on it). Precharging to the same midpoint as the plate makes a stored 1 and a stored 0 move the bit line by equal and opposite amounts. The word line is then raised to open the transistor. It is raised not to VDD but to a boosted voltage **VPP**, because an n-type transistor conducts only while its gate is at least Vt (~0.5 V) above its source; with the gate at only VDD it could charge the capacitor to no more than VDD − Vt, a loss called the **threshold drop**. Where VPP comes from depends on the interface. DDR4 and DDR5 modules supply it from outside on a dedicated VPP pin (2.5 V for DDR4, 1.8 V for DDR5), and that rail feeds the word-line drivers. The chip also has on-die **charge pumps**, circuits that stack up capacitor charges to make a voltage above the supply (or below ground), which regulate that level and generate the other internal voltages the array needs, such as the slightly negative word-line *low* level (a few tenths of a volt below ground) that holds the access transistors firmly off between accesses; in interfaces with no VPP pin (LPDDR, whose 1.8 V VDD1 rail plays the same role) the pumps do the whole job from the ordinary supply. Either way the word line is driven to roughly the VPP level, ~1.8 V (DDR5) to ~2.5 V (DDR4), comfortably above VDD + Vt ≈ 1.1 + 0.5 = 1.6 V, so the access transistor passes a full VDD.

The storage capacitor and the bit line now share charge. Picture connecting a small vessel to a much larger one: the small vessel can move the shared level only a little. Here the shared “level” is voltage, and capacitance describes how much charge is needed to change that voltage. Unlike water, charge can represent either state relative to an electrical reference.

Call the cell capacitance Cs and the bit-line capacitance Cbl. Cbl includes the long wire and the sense amplifier's input. Vfinal is their common voltage after the access transistor connects them; VDD is the supply voltage. For a stored 1, conservation of charge gives

Cs × VDD + Cbl × VDD/2 = (Cs + Cbl) × Vfinal

The left side counts the initial charge contribution from each capacitance; the right side describes both at their shared final voltage. Solving for ΔV, the change from the bit line's initial VDD/2, gives

ΔV = (VDD/2) × Cs / (Cs + Cbl)

toward VDD if the cell held a "1" or toward 0 if it held a "0". It means: the signal is the cell's share of the total capacitance, and because the bit line is several times bigger than the cell, most of the stored voltage is lost in the sharing.

A **sense amplifier**, a cross-coupled pair of CMOS **inverters** (an inverter is a circuit whose output is the opposite of its input; cross-coupled means each one's output feeds the other's input) connected between this bit line and a **reference bit line**, is then enabled and amplifies the small difference to **full rail** (all the way to VDD on one side and 0 V on the other). The reference is a neighbouring bit line that was also precharged to VDD/2 but whose cells stayed closed, so it sits exactly at the midpoint.

> **Intuition: the sense amplifier as a tug of war.** Two inverters wired output-to-input are two teams, each pulling the other's rope the opposite way. When the amplifier is switched on, whichever side starts a few tens of millivolts higher makes its inverter pull the other side down, which makes that inverter pull the first side up harder, and within a few nanoseconds the higher side is at VDD and the lower side at 0. Positive feedback turns a 140 mV lean into a full-rail answer; it also means the amplifier will confidently amplify noise if the lean is too small.

Because charge sharing destroys the stored value, the sense amplifier's full-rail output is written back through the still-open word line before it is closed. This is the **destructive read** that makes DRAM "dynamic," together with the fact that the capacitor leaks and must be **refreshed** (read and rewritten) within its specified retention window. A conventional DDR example uses **64 ms**, reduced to 32 ms above 85 °C; the exact window depends on memory generation, device and temperature grade, so these values must not be applied to every HBM or DDR5 part. **JEDEC** is the industry body that writes the memory interface standards. Higher refresh activity consumes time and power. Why does the example need a bounded refresh window? Leakage through the off transistor and through the capacitor dielectric drains the ~40,000 electrons; a typical cell holds its charge for seconds, but the refresh interval must cover the leakiest **tail cells** among 17 billion, which is why retention testing at wafer sort (Module 14) is heavier in DRAM than in any logic product.

> **Worked example: the sense-amp signal.** A modern cell has Cs ≈ 12 fF. A bit line is a ~20 nm-wide tungsten line running past ~500–1,000 cells, with Cbl ≈ 30–40 fF including the sense-amp input. With VDD = 1.1 V: ΔV = 0.55 V × 12 / (12 + 35) ≈ 140 mV. Sense amplifiers need a margin above their offset (~20–40 mV from transistor **mismatch**: no two transistors are identical, because random variation in dopant count and dimensions gives each a slightly different Vt, so even a perfectly balanced amplifier has a built-in lean of tens of millivolts) plus noise from adjacent bit lines switching, so this example can adopt a 60–80 mV design margin, corresponding to roughly 6–10 fF with the assumed loading. At 3 fF the signal would be about 43 mV, below that chosen margin. These are illustrative design assumptions; a different bit line, offset distribution or sensing scheme changes the minimum usable capacitance. This is why the capacitor cannot shrink with the cell footprint. Every DRAM node since ~2005 has therefore held Cs roughly constant at 10–20 fF while the footprint shrank from ~0.02 µm² to ~0.001 µm²; the only way to do that is to build upward.

> **What can go wrong.** *Tail-bit retention*: a few cells in a billion leak far faster than the rest, through a defect in the dielectric or a leaky access transistor, and lose their bit before the 64 ms refresh; they are found by retention screens at wafer sort and swapped for spare rows and columns (**redundancy repair**). *Variable retention time (VRT)*: a cell that flips at random between a good and a bad retention state, invisible in a single screen; the defence is on-die **ECC** (an error-correcting code that fixes single-bit errors, standard in DDR5). *Row hammer*: opening one word line many thousands of times within one refresh interval disturbs the charge in the neighbouring rows' cells (its physical origin is in Section 3); the fix is **target row refresh**, where the chip counts activations of each row and refreshes its neighbours early.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The doubling of refresh above 85 °C is the thermal tax HBM pays in Section 22: a stack squeezed against a 1,000 W GPU runs hot, refreshes twice as often, and loses bandwidth and power to its own housekeeping.

## 3. The buried word line and the 6F² cell

Section 2 needs one switch per cell, and the cell has to be as small as lithography allows: a rectangle roughly 25 by 38 nm at today's nodes that must also hold a contact down to the bit line and a contact up to the capacitor. A transistor built on the silicon surface at that size fails twice. Its channel would be too short to switch off cleanly: **short-channel leakage** is what happens when the channel is so short that the gate loses control of it, and current leaks even when the switch is nominally off, draining the capacitor. And its gate would occupy the very silicon that the two contacts (bit line and storage node) need. The 6F² cell only works because the access transistor is buried; this section explains the layout first, then the transistor that makes it possible.

Cell area is expressed in units of **F**, the minimum feature or half-pitch of the process; **F²** is the area of the smallest printable square, so a 6F² cell is a rectangle 2F by 3F. Early DRAM used an **8F²** cell; since the late 2000s (Micron first, at ~78 nm in 2006; Qimonda's buried-word-line 6F² cell followed in 2008 and became the industry template) the industry uses **6F²**: the cell is 2F along the bit line (one word-line pitch: lines F wide with F spaces) by 3F along the word line (one bit-line pitch, wider because a storage-node contact must fit between adjacent bit lines). In a 1β-class process (node names are explained in Section 6) with F ≈ 12–13 nm, a cell is ~25 × 38 nm ≈ 0.0010 µm², and a 16 Gb die packs its 17.2 billion cells into ~35–40 mm² of array on a ~60–70 mm² die.

The **buried word line (bWL)**, also called the **buried-channel array transistor (BCAT)** or **recessed-channel array transistor (RCAT)**, is made by etching a trench ~150–200 nm deep into the silicon along each word line (silicon etchers from Lam and TEL), lining it with gate oxide (~5 nm, thermally grown or by ALD from ASM or TEL) and a **titanium nitride (TiN)** **work-function metal** (the **work function** is the energy needed to pull an electron out of a metal; the gate metal's work function sets the transistor's Vt), filling it with tungsten (Lam or Applied Materials tungsten fill; at newer nodes a lower-resistance metal such as **molybdenum (Mo)** or **ruthenium (Ru)** is under evaluation), recessing the metal below the silicon surface, and capping it with **silicon nitride (SiN)**, a hard insulator. The source and drain are the two patches of silicon surface on either side of the trench (one under the bit-line contact, one under the storage-node contact), so electrons must travel down one trench wall, across the bottom and up the other wall. The channel therefore wraps around the bottom of the trench: a U shape. Two things are gained. The **effective channel length is ~2–3× the lithographic length**, which suppresses short-channel leakage, and the gate is below the surface, which reduces word-line-to-bit-line capacitance and frees the surface for the bit-line and storage-node contacts.

> **Intuition: bricks laid diagonally across two rails.** The **active areas**, the strips of silicon where transistors are made, surrounded by insulating oxide, are laid out as tilted islands (~20–30° off the word-line direction) so that each island crosses two word lines and one bit-line contact sits in the middle with a storage-node contact at each end. Picture bricks laid diagonally across two parallel rails: each brick touches both rails, and each brick's two ends are two cells sharing one bit-line contact in its centre. The tilt is what lets three contacts fit on one island without the island getting longer. Why: the island needs three contacts in a row, storage node, bit line, storage node. Lay it straight along the bit line and both storage-node contacts sit directly under that same bit line, with no room for the landing pads that carry them out to the capacitors above; lay it straight along the word line and it never crosses a second word line, so it holds only one transistor. Tilted, the island's middle still sits under a bit line for the shared contact, each end comes out from under the bit line into the gap between two neighbouring bit lines, where a storage-node contact and its landing pad fit, and the island still crosses two word lines, one per cell.

Above the transistor: the bit line (tungsten with a TiN liner on a **polysilicon** plug, ~20 nm wide, capped with SiN; polysilicon is silicon made of many small crystals, a conductor when heavily doped), then the **storage node contacts (SNC)** and **landing pads** that **fan out** (spread) from the tight SNC pitch to the capacitor bottoms. All of this sits under the capacitor in the architecture every current DRAM uses, **capacitor-over-bitline (COB)**: putting the capacitor above the wiring lets it be as tall as the process can make it without disturbing the transistor and bit line below. The older alternative, a trench capacitor dug down into the silicon, ran out of depth.

> **Worked example: cell area and dies per wafer.** At F = 12.5 nm a 6F² cell is 2F × 3F = 25 × 37.5 nm ≈ 940 nm² ≈ 0.00094 µm². The 17.2 billion cells of a 16 Gb die therefore occupy ~16 mm² of pure cell area; with the local sense amplifiers, word-line drivers and spare rows and columns that live inside the array blocks, the array is ~35–40 mm², and the die with its periphery ~60–70 mm², which is what allows ~900–1,000 gross dies per 300 mm wafer (Section 6). Two conventional ratios make that chain read as normal rather than surprising: **array efficiency** (array area over die area) is ~55–65%, as Section 1 said, and **cell efficiency** (pure cell area over die area) is only ~25–30% in modern DRAM, because inside each array block the sense-amplifier strips (one amplifier per bit line, each serving only ~500–1,000 cells, Section 2), the **sub-word-line drivers** that re-drive each segment of the resistive buried word line, the spare rows and columns for redundancy repair and the gaps between sub-arrays together take more area than the cells themselves. A 4F² cell (Section 7) at the same F would be 2F × 2F = 625 nm²: 33% fewer F² per cell, or 1.5× more bits per mm² of array with no change in lithography.

> **What can go wrong.** *Word-line-to-bit-line shorts*: the SiN cap over the buried gate is only tens of nanometres from the bit-line contact, and a thin or pinholed cap shorts a whole row to a column; found as row and column failures at sort. *Island CD variation* (**critical dimension, CD**: the printed width of a feature): the tilted islands are printed at the tightest pitch on the die (SAQP or EUV), and an island a few nanometres narrow has a weaker, leakier transistor, which shows up as a retention tail. *Adjacent-gate coupling*: the buried gates of neighbouring rows sit tens of nanometres apart in the same silicon, so toggling one word line thousands of times disturbs the charge in the cells beside it. This is the physical origin of row hammer (Section 2).

## 4. The storage capacitor

Section 2 set the requirement: ~10–20 fF from a cell whose footprint has shrunk ~20× since 2005. The only way to keep the electrode area while the footprint shrinks is to grow upward, and the result is the tallest, narrowest structure in any commercial chip. The capacitor is a cylinder or pillar ~1.0–1.5 µm tall and a few tens of nanometres across (~35 nm at the 1x/1y nodes, nearer ~20 nm at 1β; the pitch arithmetic is below), an aspect ratio of 40 or more, and there are ~17 billion of them on a die, each ~10–15 nm from its neighbours.

> **Intuition: a paper cup coated inside and out.** Picture a paper cup 1.2 µm tall and 35 nm across (the 1x/1y-class numbers of the worked example below), with the cup wall itself a 3 nm metal film. The capacitor's insulator is a 6 nm coating painted over the cup inside and out, and the top electrode is a second metal film over that coating, also inside and out. Every square nanometre of the cup's surface, both faces, stores charge; a shorter cup stores proportionally less, which is why height is the one dimension DRAM never gives up.

The dielectric is **ZAZ**: **zirconium dioxide (ZrO2)** / **aluminium oxide (Al2O3)** / ZrO2, deposited by ALD, with a total physical thickness of ~5–7 nm. Its **dielectric constant k** (the factor by which an insulator boosts capacitance over vacuum; silicon dioxide is 3.9) is ~35–40 for **tetragonal** ZrO2, the crystal arrangement of the material that has the high k. The **equivalent oxide thickness (EOT)** is the thickness of plain silicon dioxide that would give the same capacitance: a ZAZ stack with an EOT of ~0.5–0.6 nm "acts like" 0.55 nm of SiO2 while being physically ~6 nm thick, and 0.55 nm of real SiO2 would be about two atoms and would leak like a sieve. That is the whole point of a high-k dielectric. The thin Al2O3 (~0.5 nm, k ≈ 9) interlayer breaks the ZrO2 crystallinity to stop leakage along **grain boundaries**, the seams between crystal grains through which leakage current finds a path, at a small cost in overall k. Both electrodes are TiN deposited by ALD (titanium tetrachloride, TiCl4, plus ammonia, NH3, at ~400–500 °C), ~3–5 nm thick, chosen because it is conformal, conductive, and has a high enough work function (~4.7 eV; an **electron-volt** is the energy of one electron crossing one volt) to suppress electron injection into the ZrO2. The top electrode is thickened with doped **silicon-germanium (SiGe)** or **tungsten (W)** to form the common plate. Research capacitors use higher-k dielectrics (strontium titanate, SrTiO3, and hafnium-zirconium oxide ferroelectric/antiferroelectric stacks) and ruthenium or niobium electrodes; as of ~2025 ZAZ/TiN remains what ships.

**How ~17 billion of them fit.** There is exactly one capacitor per cell, so the average area per capacitor is the cell area of Section 3, whatever the pattern. The capacitors are laid out on a **hexagonal (honeycomb) grid**, the densest packing of circles, and for a hexagonal grid the centre-to-centre spacing a satisfies (√3/2) × a² = cell area, so a ≈ 1.07 × √(cell area). At a 1x node (F ≈ 19 nm, cell ≈ 2,200 nm²) that is ~50 nm; at 1β (F ≈ 12.5 nm, cell ≈ 940 nm²) it is ~33 nm. The gap between neighbouring bottom electrodes has to hold two ZAZ coatings, one on each neighbour (~4–6 nm each), plus the shared top electrode where the two sides merge (~3 nm), so it cannot be less than ~11–15 nm. At ~50 nm spacing a 35 nm cup leaves exactly that; at ~33 nm spacing the bottom electrode can only be ~20 nm across, and a 20 nm cup with two 3 nm walls and two 4–6 nm dielectric coats inside it has no hollow left: it is a solid **pillar** whose outer face alone stores the charge (the industry moved from hollow cylinders to pillars around the 1x–1y nodes for this reason). The area lost that way is bought back with more height, a slightly thinner EOT and a thinner electrode, which is why the aspect ratio keeps rising node after node.

> **Worked example: capacitance from geometry.** Start with 1x/1y-class geometry (~50 nm spacing). A cylinder capacitor ~1.2 µm tall with a 35 nm outer diameter and 3 nm-thick TiN bottom electrode has both inner and outer surfaces active (the mold is removed so the top electrode wraps outside too). Take the mean electrode diameter as ~30 nm: area A ≈ π × 30 nm × 1,200 nm × 2 (inside + outside) ≈ 2.26 × 10⁵ nm² = 0.226 µm². The capacitance is C = ε0 × 3.9 × A / EOT, where ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space, 3.9 is the k of SiO2 (used because EOT is expressed in SiO2-equivalent thickness) and A is the electrode area: C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m × 3.9 × 2.26 × 10⁻¹³ m² / 0.55 × 10⁻⁹ m ≈ 14 fF. That matches the 10–20 fF the sense amplifier needs, and shows why height cannot be given up: at 0.6 µm the same cell would be ~7 fF. Now shrink to 1β (~33 nm spacing): a ~20 nm solid pillar at the same 1.2 µm has only its outside face, A ≈ π × 20 nm × 1,200 nm ≈ 7.5 × 10⁴ nm², or ~5 fF at the same EOT, below the floor. Getting back to ~8 fF takes all three remaining levers at once: ~1.5 µm of height, an EOT nearer ~0.45 nm (which thins each ZAZ coat to ~4 nm and lets the pillar grow to ~22 nm in the same 33 nm) and the thinner electrode: π × 22 nm × 1,500 nm ≈ 1.04 × 10⁵ nm² → C ≈ 3.45 × 10⁻¹¹ × 1.04 × 10⁻¹³ / 0.45 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ 8 fF. These 1β figures are order-of-magnitude: vendors do not publish capacitor dimensions, and TechInsights cross-sections are the only public source.

Making it, step by step:

1. **Mold stack.** A ~1.2–1.6 µm stack of oxide, usually a fast-deposited **borophosphosilicate glass (BPSG)** (silicon dioxide doped with boron and phosphorus, which deposits fast and dissolves fast in HF) or **TEOS oxide** (silicon dioxide made by decomposing **tetraethyl orthosilicate**, Si(OC2H5)4, a liquid that is vaporized into a CVD chamber and breaks apart at ~400–700 °C into SiO2 and volatile fragments; it is cheap because the precursor costs little and the film grows at hundreds of nanometres per minute, against ~0.1 nm per cycle for ALD). Both are chosen for speed and for dissolving quickly in step 4, not for quality, because the mold is temporary. Into it go two or three thin **supporter** layers of SiN (~30–50 nm each) inserted at mid-height and at the top. These are the future scaffolding; the oxide is a temporary **mold** that will be dissolved.
2. **Hard mask and hole etch.** A thick amorphous-carbon or boron-doped carbon hard mask is patterned, with **DUV** (deep-ultraviolet, 193 nm) **immersion** lithography (a film of water between the lens and the wafer sharpens the image, Module 07) plus SAQP, or EUV at the newest nodes, with the hexagonal array of holes described above (~35 nm across at 1x/1y, ~20 nm at 1β). The mold is etched in a **capacitively coupled** fluorocarbon plasma (a plasma driven between two parallel electrodes, which gives the strong vertical ion acceleration a deep hole needs; Module 09; this is the DRAM analogue of the NAND channel-hole etch, at lower depth but tighter pitch). **Bowing** (the hole widens below the mask like a bottle), **twisting** (the hole wanders sideways into its neighbour) and hole-to-hole CD variation are the yield killers; the etch runs at cryogenic or low **chuck** temperatures (the chuck is the cooled plate holding the wafer) with high bias power. The HAR etchers come from Lam (Vantex, Flex) and TEL.
3. **Bottom electrode.** ALD TiN lines the holes; the top is removed by CMP or etch-back so each cylinder is electrically separate.
4. **Supporter open and mold removal.** The top SiN supporter is patterned with openings between cylinders, and the oxide mold is stripped in dilute **HF** (hydrofluoric acid, which dissolves oxide but not nitride or TiN), either wet or as HF vapour to avoid **capillary forces** (the pull of a liquid's surface tension as it dries between two close surfaces). The cylinders are now free-standing 40:1 pillars held only where they pass through the SiN supporter layers. Without supporters, surface tension during drying pulls neighbours together (**leaning** or **stiction**) and shorts them; the supporter concept, introduced around the 40–30 nm nodes, is what allowed capacitor aspect ratio to keep climbing.
5. **Dielectric and top electrode.** ALD ZAZ over every surface, then ALD TiN, then the SiGe/W plate fill. One ALD cycle pulses a metal-carrying molecule, the **precursor**, a molecule built to carry one metal atom into the chamber as a gas: **TEMAZ** (tetrakis(ethylmethylamino)zirconium) or **TDMAZ** (tetrakis(dimethylamino)zirconium), a zirconium atom holding four small nitrogen-carbon arms (**ligands**, the groups attached to the metal atom), for ZrO2; and **TMA** (trimethylaluminium, an aluminium atom with three methyl groups) for Al2O3. The arms make the molecule volatile enough to be carried as a vapour at ~50–100 °C and reactive enough to bond to a hydroxyl-covered surface and then stop, one molecular layer thick, which is the self-limiting behaviour ALD depends on. The cycle lets that layer stick, purges the excess, pulses ozone (O3) to burn the arms off and oxidize the metal, and purges again: ~0.1 nm per cycle at ~250–300 °C, so ~50–70 cycles for the ~5–7 nm ZAZ stack, with every surface getting the same thickness. The TiN electrodes grow much more slowly per cycle and take a few hundred cycles more, so the capacitor's films are several hundred ALD cycles in total. **Conformality** (equal thickness everywhere) inside a 40:1 hole is the reason ALD, not CVD, is the only option; a DRAM fab runs hundreds of ALD chambers (ASM, TEL, Jusung, Wonik, Eugene Technology are the main suppliers, with Samsung and SK hynix also buying from Korean tool makers they part-own).

> **What can go wrong.** *Leaning and stiction*: a supporter opened too wide, or a drying step with too much surface tension, lets neighbouring cylinders touch, shorting two cells; seen as paired-bit failures at sort and confirmed by cross-section **TEM** (transmission electron microscopy, imaging a slice thin enough for electrons to pass through). *Bowing and twisting shorts*: a hole that bulges or wanders into its neighbour shorts two bottom electrodes; screened in-line by **CD-SEM** (an electron microscope that measures hole diameters on the wafer) at the top of the hole, and by TEM at the bottom. *Dielectric leakage tail bits*: a grain boundary or a thin spot in the ZAZ leaks the ~40,000 electrons early; caught only by the retention test, and repaired with spare cells. *Discontinuous bottom electrode*: TiN that thins out at the very bottom of a 40:1 hole leaves a cylinder that reads as a permanently empty cell.

## 5. Periphery, BEOL and the die

The array of Sections 2 to 4 cannot talk to the outside world by itself. Around and between the array blocks sits the **periphery**: the sense amplifiers, the row and column decoders, the charge pumps of Section 2 that make VPP, **delay-locked loops** (timing circuits that align the chip's data to the clock), and the DDR5/LPDDR5X/HBM I/O (**LPDDR5X** is the low-power DRAM interface used in phones and laptops). The problem it poses is that it is ordinary logic, and ordinary logic wants a logic process: fast, small transistors and many wiring levels. A DRAM fab cannot give it that, for one economic reason and one thermal reason, and the compromises are what make a DRAM die look the way it does.

The economic reason is that periphery transistors are not where the bits are, so shrinking them buys little. The periphery is roughly 35–45% of the die (the array is 55–65%, Section 1), and much of it is either analogue (the sense amplifiers, whose two transistors must match to within tens of millivolts, which favours *larger* transistors, because random mismatch falls as the square root of gate area) or high-voltage (the word-line drivers switch VPP, ~1.8–2.5 V, and the pumps run higher still), and neither kind benefits from a leading-edge transistor. Samsung and SK hynix nevertheless moved the periphery to **HKMG (high-k/metal gate)** transistors at the 1α/1a node for DDR5: a gate stack of hafnium-oxide insulator plus a metal gate instead of silicon dioxide plus polysilicon, which lets the gate be electrically thinner without leaking (logic adopted it in 2007), and which they needed to run DDR5's 6.4+ Gbps I/O at acceptable power. Micron, which had first used HKMG in 1z graphics DRAM, extended it across its DRAM line at 1β.

The thermal reason is the order of construction. Periphery transistors are built first, alongside the buried-word-line transistors; the capacitor module of Section 4 comes afterwards, on top of finished, contacted transistors. That module is many hours of cumulative time at 250–500 °C: the mold-oxide deposition, several hundred ALD cycles for the TiN electrodes at ~400–500 °C and for the ZAZ at ~250–300 °C, and the crystallization anneal that gives ZrO2 its high k. This is the periphery's **thermal budget**, the total time-at-temperature a finished structure can tolerate before it changes. An HKMG gate is a stack of thin films (a hafnium-oxide layer, a work-function metal, a fill metal) whose electrical behaviour depends on interfaces a few atoms thick; hours at ~450 °C let atoms diffuse across those interfaces, which shifts the transistor's Vt, and let the dopants under the contacts spread and the silicide contacts (the metal-silicon compound that makes a low-resistance contact) coarsen, raising their resistance. Logic BEOL also runs at ~400 °C over finished transistors, but for minutes per level rather than hours, and logic's gates are tuned to exactly that budget. The DRAM periphery therefore has to use a transistor design that shrugs off the capacitor module: thicker films, larger dimensions, older-generation gate stacks, which is why HKMG reached DRAM 14 years after logic. Conversely, the capacitor's TiN and ZAZ deposition and anneal temperatures are capped by what the periphery can take, which limits which electrode and dielectric materials are practical. The two constraints together are why DRAM periphery lags logic by ~4–5 nodes, and why the 3D NAND makers went to the trouble of bonding a separately made periphery wafer (Section 11).

Interconnect is three to four levels, each with a job (the assignment varies by vendor). The tungsten bit-line level doubles as the periphery's local wiring and gate-contact level. One or two copper levels carry the **main word lines** (metal lines that bring the row address to the sub-word-line drivers of Section 3), the column-select lines, the global data buses and the power grid. An aluminium top layer carries the bond pads and, on an HBM core die, the TSV landing pads and microbump pads (Sections 16 and 17). All of it is built on top of the capacitor, which is the awkward part: the top of the capacitor plate stands ~1.2–1.5 µm above the silicon surface, so the periphery side is first buried in oxide and the wafer is planarized by CMP before the first copper level, and the periphery's contacts then have to reach down through ~1.5 µm of oxide to transistors at the surface, a high-aspect-ratio etch and tungsten fill of their own (order-of-magnitude aspect ratio ~15–30). A 16 Gb DDR5 die at 1β is ~60–70 mm²; a 24 Gb die ~70–80 mm²; 32 Gb DDR5 dies arrived first at Samsung's 1b (12 nm-class) node in 2023–24 and at 1γ/1c elsewhere in 2025–26. The periphery is patterned with ArF immersion scanners (ASML, Nikon), its HKMG stack is deposited by ALD (ASM, TEL) and PVD (Applied Materials), and its contacts and copper levels are filled by the same Lam and Applied tools as the array; there is no separate "periphery tool" vendor, which is the point: the periphery is built with whatever the array flow already has on the floor.

> **Worked example: the die-area budget.** Take the 16 Gb 1β die at ~65 mm². Cells: 17.2 × 10⁹ × 940 nm² ≈ 16 mm² (~25%). Array overhead (sense-amplifier strips, sub-word-line drivers, spare rows and columns, gaps between sub-arrays): ~21 mm² (~32%), taking the array block to ~37 mm² (~57%). Periphery proper (decoders, pumps, DLLs, I/O, pads, seal ring): ~28 mm² (~43%). A cell shrink that cut cell area by 20% but left everything else unchanged would save only ~3 mm², ~5% of the die. This is why a DRAM node is a package of three shrinks pursued together: the cell, the periphery transistors, and the array overhead (fewer, longer bit lines with more cells per sense amplifier), the last of which trades directly against the sense-amp signal of Section 2, because a longer bit line means a larger Cbl.

> **What can go wrong.** *Vt shift after the capacitor module*: periphery transistors that measured on target after gate formation come out of the capacitor module with their Vt shifted; the fix is to pre-compensate the implants and trim the deposition temperatures, and the monitor is a set of test transistors in the **scribe lines** (the strips between dies that the dicing saw later removes) probed after each module. *Periphery contact opens*: the deep contacts from the first copper level down past the capacitor to the periphery transistors are the die's second-hardest etch after the capacitor hole; a contact that stops short is a dead decoder or I/O, found at wafer sort as a whole-block failure rather than as bad bits. *Array-edge effects*: the edge of the capacitor array is a ~1.5 µm cliff; CMP dishes beside it and lithography loses focus across it, so the outermost rows of cells are dummies and the boundary consumes area. *Pump and DLL marginality*: the analogue circuits are the most temperature- and voltage-sensitive part of the die; a DLL that loses lock or a pump that sags under heavy activation shows up as timing failures at speed, not as bad bits, which is why at-speed testing at temperature is part of wafer sort (Section 21).

## 6. Node naming and where EUV is used

DRAM nodes stopped being honest numbers around 2016. The industry names them by Greek letters or Latin letters within the "1x nm" class: Micron uses Greek letters (1α, 1β, 1γ), Samsung and SK hynix use Latin letters (1a, 1b, 1c), and the pairs are roughly contemporaneous and roughly equivalent in half-pitch.

EUV in DRAM is used on a handful of layers where it replaces DUV SAQP: typically the active-area island pattern, the bit-line or storage-node contact layer, and one or two periphery layers. SK hynix was the first to put EUV into mass DRAM production (1a, 2021, a single layer); Samsung uses the most EUV layers (five at 1a); Micron was the last holdout, running 1α and 1β with multi-patterned ArF immersion and only introducing EUV at 1γ in 2025, with EUV tools in Hiroshima (Japan) and Taichung. The economics are simple: one EUV pass (reported estimates of ~$100–200 per **wafer-layer**, one lithography pass on one wafer, falling with tool throughput) replaces four DUV passes plus the associated deposition and etch steps, and only pays when EUV tool throughput is high. That is why Micron waited until 0.33 NA tools reached ~180–220 wafers per hour (**numerical aperture, NA**, is the light-gathering angle of the projection lens, which sets the smallest printable pitch; 0.33 NA is the current production EUV optic).

> **Worked example: cost per bit from wafer cost.** The inputs are wafer cost, gross dies, yield and die capacity. A 1β DRAM wafer costs on the order of $1,500–2,500 to process. **Gross dies**, the number of full die sites on a wafer before yield loss, is ~900–1,000 for a 16 Gb (2 GB) die; at 85–90% yield the wafer holds ~1.7 TB of good DRAM, or ~$1–1.5 per GB at the fab level. Selling prices swing from below cost in gluts (2023) to 3× cost in shortages (2025–26), which is why memory makers' operating margins oscillate between −20% and +50%.

## 7. The roadmap: 4F² VCT and 3D DRAM

This section is a side note on what comes after 6F²; nothing in it ships yet. The 6F² cell runs out of room near F ≈ 10 nm, because the tilted active island and the bit-line contact cannot shrink further without the access transistor leaking. Two successors are in development:

- **4F² vertical channel transistor (VCT) DRAM.** The access transistor is turned vertical, a pillar with the word line wrapped around it and the capacitor stacked directly on top, so the cell footprint is 2F × 2F. Samsung has shown VCT test chips; SK hynix and Micron have parallel programs. It gives a one-time ~30% density gain and is expected around the 1δ–0a timeframe (~2027–2028), with the complication that a vertical transistor has a **floating body** (its silicon is not connected to the substrate, so stray charge can accumulate and leak) and needs careful leakage control; **IGZO** (indium gallium zinc oxide, an oxide semiconductor with extremely low off-current) channels are one candidate.
- **3D DRAM.** The cell is laid on its side and stacked, like 3D NAND: horizontal Si channels formed from an **epitaxial** Si/SiGe **superlattice** (epitaxy is growing crystalline layers atom by atom on the wafer; a superlattice is a stack of alternating thin crystalline Si and SiGe layers, where the SiGe is selectively etched away to leave silicon shelves), with a lateral capacitor per layer. Samsung, SK hynix, Micron and imec (the Belgian research institute) have all published on it; first products are expected no earlier than ~2028–2030. The key difficulty is that a lateral capacitor loses the height advantage, so each layer holds less charge, and the superlattice must be defect-free across hundreds of layers.

## 9. The NAND cell: floating gate to charge trap

NAND flash is the second technology: the storage that holds model weights, datasets and operating systems when the power is off. Sections 9 to 13 cover its cell, its 3D architecture, the gate-replacement flow that builds it, string stacking and the players.

Persistent storage has a different problem from DRAM. It must hold data with the power off for years, at well under $0.10 per GB, and a DRAM capacitor leaks in milliseconds. The answer is to lock the charge behind an insulator thick enough that electrons cannot leak out on their own, and to move them across it only when forced by a very high electric field.

> **Intuition: read the effect of the stored charge.** A **floating gate** is a tiny conductor enclosed by an insulator. Think of a sealed container whose contents affect a nearby sensor: you infer what is inside without opening it. Here stored electrons repel electrons in the transistor channel underneath, so the transistor needs more gate voltage to turn on. That shift in turn-on voltage represents the data. A read asks whether the transistor conducts at a chosen gate voltage. The container picture stops at isolation: programming does not open a lid or push electrons through literal glass. A strong electric field enables tunnelling through the thin insulator, as the next paragraphs explain.

A NAND cell is a **MOSFET** (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor, the ordinary transistor of Module 11, whose gate is separated from the channel by an insulating oxide) whose threshold voltage Vt can be shifted by trapping charge in its **gate stack**, the layers between the gate and the channel. In the classic **floating-gate (FG)** cell, a polysilicon island sits between a ~7–8 nm **tunnel oxide** (the thin insulator over the channel through which electrons are forced) and a thicker **inter-poly dielectric** (the insulator between the floating gate and the **control gate**, the gate the chip actually drives). To program, the control gate is pulsed to ~18–22 V; electrons tunnel from the channel through the tunnel oxide by **Fowler-Nordheim tunnelling** (field ~10 MV/cm), charge the floating gate, and raise Vt by several volts. Erase applies the reverse field (channel/body at ~20 V, gate at 0) for a whole block at once. Reading applies a gate voltage between the programmed and erased Vt and senses whether the string conducts; retention relies on the tunnel oxide leaking negligibly at zero field (datasheets quote up to ~10 years for lightly cycled cells; JEDEC's end-of-life SSD requirement is 1 year at 30 °C for client drives and 3 months at 40 °C for enterprise).

The physics in two sentences: quantum mechanics lets an electron cross a thin insulating barrier it could not classically cross, with a probability that rises exponentially with the electric field, negligible below ~0.5–0.6 V/nm and large above ~1 V/nm. 10 MV/cm is 1 V per nm, so a 7 nm tunnel oxide needs ~7 V across it; and only about half of the control-gate voltage reaches the tunnel oxide, because the floating gate sits in a voltage divider between the inter-poly dielectric above it and the tunnel oxide below it. That fraction is the **coupling ratio**, ~0.5–0.6, and it is why the gate must be pulsed to ~18–22 V, twenty times the 1.1 V logic supply, and why NAND needs high-voltage periphery transistors and charge pumps.

The name comes from the wiring. Cells are connected in series, 32 to ~300 of them in a **string**, like the stacked transistors of a NAND logic gate; one contact per string rather than per cell is what makes the array dense. A **page** is one word line's worth of cells across thousands of strings, read or written together (~16 KB). A **block** is the set of strings sharing one silicon body, and it is erased as a unit because the erase voltage is applied to that shared body. A drive can write pages but erase only blocks, which is why an SSD controller shuffles data around and why NAND is slow to overwrite in place.

> **Worked example: the coupling ratio and counting electrons.** Tunnelling needs ~1 V/nm across a 7 nm tunnel oxide, so ~7 V. At a coupling ratio of ~0.5–0.6 the control gate must supply ~12–14 V to put 7 V on the tunnel oxide; margin for the slowest cells and for reaching the highest programmed states pushes the pulse to ~18–22 V. Now count electrons: a planar ~15 nm cell stores only a few hundred of them. If ~300 electrons span a ~5 V Vt window, each electron is worth ~15 mV of Vt (all of these are order-of-magnitude figures), so losing ten of them shifts Vt by ~0.15 V, which is a large fraction of the ~0.3–0.4 V spacing between the 16 states of a **QLC** cell (quad-level cell: one that stores 4 bits as one of 16 threshold-voltage levels; the multi-level cells are defined two paragraphs below). That is why planar scaling stopped and why every extra bit per cell costs endurance.

Planar FG scaling ended at ~14–16 nm because adjacent floating gates couple capacitively (a neighbour's program shifts your Vt), and because a 15 nm cell stores only a few hundred electrons, so losing ten of them is a bit error. The industry's answer was to go vertical and, in the same move, to switch most designs to the **charge-trap (CT)** cell: the floating gate is replaced by a ~5–7 nm layer of silicon nitride, which stores electrons in discrete, immobile **traps**. A trap is a defect site in the nitride, a broken or dangling bond, that can hold a single electron at a fixed spot, so the charge sits where it was injected rather than spreading through the film as it would in a conductor. Because the charge cannot move laterally, a nitride layer can be shared, unpatterned, along an entire vertical string with no cell-to-cell coupling, which is what makes the 3D process (Section 11) possible: inside a 100 nm hole a floating gate would have to be cut into a separate conducting island at every one of 300 layers, which is nearly impossible, whereas a continuous nitride film can simply line the whole hole. Samsung's V-NAND, SK hynix, Kioxia/SanDisk BiCS and YMTC all use charge trap; Micron switched from a floating-gate 3D cell to **replacement-gate** charge trap (the gate-replacement flow of Section 11) at its 176-layer generation (2020), and Intel/Solidigm's QLC (now under SK hynix) was the last floating-gate holdout.

**Multi-level cells.** Because Vt is analogue, a cell can hold more than one bit by programming it into one of several Vt windows: **SLC** (2 levels, 1 bit), **MLC** (4, 2 bits), **TLC** (8 levels, 3 bits, the mainstream), **QLC** (16 levels, 4 bits, a fast-growing share of data-centre SSD bits as of 2025–26), and **PLC** (32 levels, 5 bits, sampled but not yet in volume as of ~2025). Programming uses **incremental step pulse programming (ISPP)**: a train of gate pulses rising by ~0.2–0.5 V per step, with a verify read after each, until the cell lands in its target window; a QLC page program takes ~1–3 ms versus ~100–200 µs for SLC. Sixteen Vt windows within a ~6–8 V span leaves ~0.3–0.4 V per state, so read voltages are calibrated per block over time and every page carries ~10–15% **LDPC (low-density parity check)** error-correction overhead in the SSD controller. Endurance falls with levels: ~100k program/erase cycles for SLC, ~3k for TLC, ~1k or less for QLC, because each high-field program/erase cycle damages the tunnel oxide.

> **What can go wrong.** *Retention loss*: trapped electrons leak out slowly, faster when hot and faster in worn cells, so the Vt distributions drift down over months. *Read disturb*: reading one page puts a moderate voltage on every other word line in the string, a weak program that, repeated millions of times, nudges neighbours' Vt upward. *Program disturb*: the 18–22 V pulse leaks a little onto cells sharing the word line that were meant to stay erased. *Cycling wear*: every program/erase cycle breaks bonds in the tunnel oxide, creating traps that leak and widen the Vt distributions. The SSD controller handles all four: it tracks the drifting read voltages per block, corrects the residual errors with LDPC, spreads writes evenly across blocks (**wear levelling**) and rewrites pages whose error counts climb before they become unreadable.

## 10. The 3D NAND architecture

Planar NAND stopped at ~15 nm, and the way to keep multiplying bits per mm² without a new lithography generation was to build up: the same ~100 nm hole, patterned with decade-old tools, now threads through hundreds of cells stacked vertically, so density is set by how many layers can be deposited and drilled through rather than by the smallest printable feature.

A 3D NAND **string** is a vertical polysilicon channel, ~100 nm in diameter, running down through a stack of horizontal word lines. Each crossing of channel and word line is one cell, a **gate-all-around** transistor (the gate wraps fully around the channel, the strongest possible control) with the channel inside and the word line wrapped around it. The stack, from top to bottom: a bit line (copper, running perpendicular over the tops of thousands of strings), a drain-select transistor, a few dummy word lines, the N active word lines, more dummies, a source-select transistor, and the common source line at the bottom. The **select transistors** are switches at each end of the string that connect it to the bit line and to the source only when that string is addressed; the **dummy word lines** are sacrificial cells at the ends that absorb the electrical disturbance from the select gates and, in stacked decks, from the deck junction; the **common source line** is the shared ground return for every string in a block. In the current products N is 200–300+.

The word-line stack is deposited, not patterned: alternating layers of SiO2 (~18–22 nm) and SiN (~20–25 nm), the **ON stack** (oxide-nitride), at a vertical pitch of ~40–45 nm (**TechInsights**, a teardown-analysis firm whose cross-sections are the public source for these numbers, measures ~45 nm at 176 layers and ~40 nm at SK hynix's 321-layer generation). The nitride layers are placeholders: Section 11 explains how they are later dissolved and replaced by tungsten word lines. The tier pitch cannot shrink much below ~40 nm because each word line must stay thick enough (~20 nm) to keep its resistance acceptable across a millimetres-long sheet, and each oxide layer thick enough (~20 nm) to keep adjacent word lines from interfering with each other's cells. A 300-layer stack, with dummies and select gates (~345 **tiers** in total; a tier is one oxide-plus-nitride pair, one future word-line level), is ~13–16 µm tall, about a fifth of the width of a human hair. That is ~200 times the height of a logic transistor and comparable to the entire BEOL of an N3 chip.

> **Worked example: strings per die.** A 1 Tb TLC die stores 1.1 × 10¹² bits (2⁴⁰). At 3 bits per cell that is ~3.7 × 10¹¹ cells, and with ~300 active word lines per string, ~1.2 billion strings, each one a channel hole that must be etched, lined and filled without touching its neighbours. A hole defect rate of one in a billion is one bad string per die, which is why hole yield, not lithography, dominates NAND yield, and why every design carries spare strings and blocks.

## 11. The gate-replacement flow step by step

This is the flow used by Samsung, SK hynix, Kioxia/SanDisk, Micron (since 176L) and YMTC, with proprietary differences in every step. The idea that makes it work: the stack is deposited as cheap oxide and nitride, the channel holes are drilled and filled while the stack is still solid, and only at the end is the nitride dissolved and swapped for metal.

1. **Periphery first (or separately).** In Micron's **CMOS-under-Array (CuA)**, SK hynix's **Periphery-under-Cell (PUC, "4D NAND")** and Samsung's **Cell-on-Periphery (COP)**, the **page buffers** (the row of sense circuits that reads or writes one whole page at once) and decoders are built as ordinary CMOS on the wafer first, covered with an oxide, and the array is built on top; this recovers ~20–30% of die area but restricts the array's thermal budget: no step above ~600–700 °C after the periphery, because above that the periphery's tungsten contacts and dopant profiles degrade. YMTC's **Xtacking** and Kioxia/SanDisk's **CBA (CMOS directly Bonded to Array)** instead build the periphery on a separate wafer, on a more advanced logic-like process, and join the two by wafer-to-wafer **hybrid bonding** (Cu-Cu with a **silicon carbonitride (SiCN)** or oxide dielectric at a ~1–3 µm pitch, millions of connections per wafer; the technique is explained in Section 19). This decouples the two thermal budgets and lets the periphery use faster transistors; it is the same bonding technology HBM will eventually use for stacking.
2. **ON stack deposition.** PECVD (Lam Vector, AMAT Producer, TEL) deposits the alternating oxide and nitride at ~400–550 °C. **Film stress** must be balanced layer by layer: a deposited film is either stretched or compressed relative to the silicon beneath it and bends the wafer like a bimetallic strip, and a ~15 µm stack of one-signed stress warps the wafer by hundreds of microns, which cannot be held flat on a lithography chuck or kept in focus. Tensile and compressive layers are therefore alternated to cancel, and thickness uniformity of each layer must be ~1% so that the 300th word line is at the intended depth. For string-stacked devices this is done one **deck** at a time (a deck is a sub-stack of ~100–170 layers deposited and drilled as a unit; Section 12).
3. **Channel-hole etch.** A carbon hard mask (~2–4 µm of boron-doped or ion-implanted amorphous carbon) is patterned with a hexagonal array of ~100–130 nm holes at ~150–180 nm pitch, and a capacitively coupled high-power plasma etches through the full deck in fluorocarbon/hydrofluorocarbon chemistry, as detailed in Module 09.

   > **Intuition: a well 50 m deep drilled with a straight-down drill.** Scale the hole up: 100 nm wide and 5 µm deep is a well 1 m across and 50 m deep, drilled with a bit that can only push straight down and must not wander sideways by more than a hand's width at the bottom. The fluorocarbon polymer that coats the walls during the etch is the well casing: it protects the sides while the bottom keeps going.

   The physics in three sentences. A strong bias voltage accelerates ions vertically into the hole; the fluorocarbon gas deposits a Teflon-like polymer on the walls that protects them while the ion-bombarded bottom keeps etching; deep in the hole fewer ions arrive and the insulating walls charge up and deflect the ones that do, so the profile bows and twists, which is what cryogenic temperatures and pulsed high power fight. The aspect ratio depends on how many decks the stack is split into (Section 12): ~40–60:1 per deck on a 3-deck 321-layer device (4–6 µm through a ~100–130 nm hole), ~60–80:1 on the 2-deck designs that etch ~6–8 µm at once, and approaching ~100:1 only on the deepest single etches. Lam's cryogenic dielectric etch (announced 2024) cut the etch time per deck by ~2.5× (Lam's stated figure; Lam does not publish the chuck temperature, which is well below 0 °C) and reduced bowing. This is the single longest step in the flow, reportedly on the order of an hour per wafer per deck. A 1 Tb TLC die has ~1–1.5 billion channel holes (each etched once per deck); one that twists into its neighbour kills the string.

   > **Worked example: why NAND fabs are etch-bound.** Section 10 counted ~1.2 billion holes per die; on a 3-deck device every one of them is drilled three times. At the reported ~1 hour per wafer per deck, a 3-deck flow spends ~3 chamber-hours per wafer on this one step. A fab running ~100,000 wafer starts per month therefore needs ~300,000 chamber-hours per month; with a chamber productive for ~600 hours a month, that is on the order of ~500 HAR etch chambers for the channel hole alone (an order-of-magnitude estimate). Few other steps in the flow come close, which is why the channel-hole etcher is the tool a NAND fab is sized around, and why a 2.5× faster etch is worth more to a NAND maker than any lithography upgrade.

4. **Gate stack and channel inside the hole.** ALD deposits, from the outside in: a blocking oxide (some makers put an Al2O3 high-k blocking layer here or later, in the gate), the ~6 nm SiN charge-trap layer, the ~5–7 nm tunnel oxide (often an oxide-nitride-oxide **bandgap-engineered** stack, layered so that the energy barrier is thin during erase and thick during retention), then a thin (~5–10 nm) polysilicon channel; the ALD liners come from ASM, TEL and Lam (Striker). The channel is a tube; its hollow core is filled with oxide (the **macaroni** channel), because a thin poly tube with a thin body has better **subthreshold control** (how sharply the transistor turns off) than a solid poly pillar. The bottom of the hole must be punched through the deposited films to reach the source, and the poly is crystallized by a ~600–800 °C anneal to enlarge its grains, since **grain boundaries** in the channel (the seams between the small crystals of polysilicon, which scatter electrons) are the main source of cell-to-cell current variation: a channel with a few large grains conducts more uniformly than one with many small ones.
5. **Staircase.** Every word line needs a contact, so the edge of the stack is carved into a staircase, one step per layer, using the cheapest lithography on the wafer (**KrF** and **ArF** dry scanners, 248 nm and 193 nm lithography without water immersion, from Nikon and Canon; Module 07). A thick resist is exposed and etched one tier, then **trimmed** laterally by an **isotropic** plasma (one that etches equally in all directions, so it shrinks the resist edge sideways by a controlled ~100–200 nm per cycle), etched another tier, and so on; ~10–16 tiers per lithography pass, so a 300-layer device needs ~20–30 trim-etch cycles across several masks. The staircase consumes ~5–8% of die area.
6. **Slit (gate line cut).** Long trenches are etched through the entire stack between rows of channel holes, every ~8–12 holes, exposing the nitride edges of every layer.
7. **Nitride removal.** Hot phosphoric acid (H3PO4 at ~150–170 °C, in **wet benches**, tanks of heated chemistry through which batches of wafers pass, from SCREEN, TEL and SEMES) is pumped through the slits and dissolves every SiN layer laterally, with a selectivity to oxide of > 100:1 (it removes 100 nm of nitride for every 1 nm of oxide), leaving the oxide layers as free-standing shelves supported by the channel pillars. The channel-hole pillars, at 100+ nm diameter and a few hundred per slit segment, are now the only thing holding the stack up. This is why hole placement is a mechanical as well as electrical design.
8. **Word-line fill.** ALD Al2O3 (high-k blocking) and TiN line the horizontal cavities, then tungsten is deposited by ALD/CVD (tungsten hexafluoride, WF6, with a diborane, B2H6, or silane, SiH4, **nucleation** step, a thin seed layer that lets tungsten start growing on the liner, then bulk fill at ~300–400 °C), filling every gap from the slit inward. The W is then etched back inside the slit so the layers are electrically separate, and the slit is filled with oxide (and, in most designs, a tungsten source-line contact down the middle). Fluorine-free tungsten and, at the newest generations, **molybdenum** word lines (lower resistivity in thin films, no fluorine attack on the oxide) are in production or qualification. A word line is a ~20 nm-thick sheet several millimetres long, so its resistance times its capacitance, the **RC delay**, sets how long the voltage takes to reach the far cells; that delay is what limits read latency as the lines get thinner and longer, and it is what pushes the industry toward molybdenum.
9. **Contacts and BEOL.** HAR contacts drop from the surface onto each staircase step; the bit lines (copper, ~40 nm pitch, one per channel-hole column) and two or three more metal levels finish the die. For bonded designs (Xtacking, CBA), the array wafer's top metal is planarized to a bonding surface and joined to the periphery wafer here.

> **What can go wrong.** *Hole twist into a neighbour*: two strings shorted, found as paired string failures at sort. *Incomplete nitride strip*: a pocket of nitride left deep in a cavity leaves a word line open or thin past that point, so a whole row of cells beyond it is dead; found electrically and confirmed by cross-section TEM. *Tungsten seam voids*: a cavity that closes at the slit mouth before it fills from the inside leaves a void down the middle of the word line and a high-resistance, slow row. *Shelf sag or collapse*: when the nitride is gone, oxide shelves spanning too far between pillars droop and touch, shorting two word lines. *Staircase punch-through*: a contact etched too deep on one step reaches the word line below it. *Deck misalignment*: an upper-deck hole that lands off-centre on the lower one makes a bad junction transistor (Section 12). Detection is by electrical string and word-line tests at sort, in-line CD-SEM of the hole array, and TEM cross-sections of the whole 15 µm stack.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The word-line resistance set in step 8 is the largest term in the time to read a page, and the number of pages a drive can read in parallel divided by that read time is roughly the **IOPS** (input/output operations per second) of the SSD. A thinner, longer word line in a taller stack means a slower drive unless the metal improves, which is why molybdenum matters to a data centre.

## 12. String stacking and the layer race

A single-deck etch is limited to ~128–176 layers by hole bowing and etch rate, so higher counts are built as **string-stacked decks**: deposit and etch one ON stack, fill the channel, planarize, deposit a second stack, and etch a second set of holes that must land on the first with an **overlay** (the positional error between one patterned layer and the one beneath it) of ~20–30 nm on a 100 nm hole. The channel is electrically continuous through an inter-deck poly plug, and the boundary layers are dummies (the misaligned junction is a bad transistor).

How the junction is made. After the lower deck's holes are lined and filled (step 4 of Section 11), the polysilicon channel is recessed a little below the top of the deck and the recess is filled with a plug of polysilicon, the **inter-deck plug**, made slightly wider than the hole (it spreads over the top oxide like a rivet head) so that it serves as both a landing pad and an **etch stop** (a material the next etch removes much more slowly, so the etch halts on it). The upper ON stack is deposited over the planarized surface and its holes are etched to land on the plugs, with the etch tuned to stop in the poly rather than punch through into the lower deck. The upper hole is then lined with its own gate stack and channel, and the new poly channel touches down on the plug, so the string is electrically one channel with a thicker, plug-shaped section at the junction. That section is a poor transistor, wider than the rest and with a different grain structure, so the one to three tiers on either side of it are the dummy word lines of Section 10 and store nothing. What the overlay tool sees: the scanner cannot measure the holes themselves, so overlay is measured on **overlay marks**, box-in-box or grating targets printed at both levels in the scribe lines and read by optical overlay tools (KLA Archer, ASML YieldStar) after lithography, then checked on the actual holes by CD-SEM after etch and by TEM cross-sections of sample junctions. Vendors differ in the details, and every plug and junction design is proprietary, but every string-stacked device has this junction, and it is the main reason a 2-deck die is not simply two 1-deck dies stacked.

> **Worked example: the junction's overlay budget.** Two 100 nm circles offset by 25 nm still overlap ~70% of their area (more in practice, because the plug is wider than the hole), which is an acceptable junction. Offset by 50 nm they overlap only ~40%, and the junction conducts through a neck whose resistance the string's read current (on the order of ~100 nA to 1 µA per string) cannot tolerate; a hole that lands beside the plug punches into the lower deck's oxide and the string is dead. That is why the ~20–30 nm overlay figure is a hard specification rather than a nice-to-have, and why the upper-deck hole layer is the one place in the NAND flow where lithography overlay, not etch, is a first-order yield term: the scanner's own overlay is a few nanometres, but the wafer it is printing on has been bent by a 4–8 µm stack of stressed films and its alignment marks are buried under that stack, so the overlay measured on the holes is far worse than the tool's specification.

## 14. HBM and the bandwidth wall

HBM is the third technology and the one this course is really about: DRAM re-engineered so that a GPU can be fed. Sections 14 to 25 cover why it exists, what a stack is, how the core dies are drilled, thinned and bumped, how they are stacked, tested and cooled, what it costs, and who supplies whom.

A GPU's useful throughput on large-language-model inference is set by how fast it can stream weights and the **KV-cache** (the working memory a language model keeps for the text it has already seen) out of memory, not by its **FLOPS** (floating-point arithmetic operations per second). The interface's peak bandwidth is the number of data pins multiplied by the transfer rate on each pin. The pins work in parallel, so their rates add. When those rates are in bits per second, divide by 8 to express the result in bytes per second; this is a transfer-rate calculation, not the time needed to answer one memory request. The conventional way to add bandwidth is to run each pin faster: DDR5 at 6.4–8.8 Gbps (gigabits per second, per pin), **GDDR7** (the fast, narrow memory on gaming cards) at 28–32 Gbps. But pushing a signal across a **PCB** (printed circuit board) **trace** (a copper track on the board, typically centimetres long) at 30 Gbps costs ~6–8 pJ per bit (picojoules, 10⁻¹² J) and needs heavy **equalization** (circuits that undo the smearing a long trace does to a fast signal), and a GDDR7 device has only 32 data pins, so one device delivers 32 × 32 Gbps ÷ 8 ≈ 128 GB/s; feeding 8 TB/s would need ~64 devices on a board with no room for them. Convert the energy figure to watts and the problem is obvious: 8 TB/s is 6.4 × 10¹³ bits per second, and at ~6 pJ per bit that is ~380 W spent purely on moving data, before a single multiplication.

HBM inverts the trade: **wide and slow**. Each HBM3/3E stack presents **1,024 data pins** (HBM4: **2,048**) at a modest 6.4–9.2 Gbps (HBM4: 8–11 Gbps), over traces only a few millimetres long on a silicon **interposer** (a slab of silicon with fine copper wiring on which the GPU and the HBM stacks sit side by side, Module 17), which need no equalization and cost ~3–4 pJ/bit, or ~220 W for the same 8 TB/s at 3.5 pJ/bit. One stack delivers 0.8–1.2 TB/s, and a B200 has eight of them.

> **Worked example: stack bandwidth.** HBM3: 1,024 pins × 6.4 Gbps = 6,554 Gb/s ÷ 8 = 819 GB/s. HBM3E (NVIDIA Blackwell grade): 1,024 × 9.2 Gbps = 1,178 GB/s ≈ 1.2 TB/s; 8 stacks → 9.4 TB/s peak (B200 is specified at ~8 TB/s with pins run at ~8 Gbps). HBM4 at the JEDEC base rate: 2,048 × 8 Gbps = 2.05 TB/s. NVIDIA's Rubin, as announced at CES 2026, specifies 8 stacks of HBM4 at 22 TB/s total; that is ~2.75 TB/s per stack, and 2.75 TB/s × 8 ÷ 2,048 ≈ 10.7 Gbps per pin. Micron's 12-high HBM4 at 11 Gbps reaches 2.8 TB/s per stack and Samsung's runs at 11.7 Gbps. The width doubling is why HBM4 reaches HBM3E-class bandwidth at a *lower* per-pin speed (the JEDEC base rate) and more than doubles it at HBM3E-class pin speeds, which is also why the base die and interposer routing, not the DRAM cell, are the HBM4 design problems.

## 15. Anatomy of an HBM stack

The bandwidth of Section 14 has to come out of a physical object about a centimetre square that sits beside the GPU; this section describes that object, and Sections 16 to 18 build it. An HBM stack, or cube, is 8, 12 or 16 **core DRAM dies** on top of one **base die** (also called the **logic die** or buffer die), all connected by **through-silicon vias (TSVs)** and **microbumps**, in a footprint of ~11 × 11 mm (HBM3/3E; HBM4 grows to a reported ~11 × 13 mm to accommodate the wider interface).

> **Intuition: a hole through the die and a post with a solder tip.** A TSV is a hole etched straight through a DRAM die, lined with insulator and filled with copper, so that a signal passes down through the silicon instead of going out to the die's edge. A microbump is a ~25 µm copper post with a tip of solder, plated onto the face of one die, that joins to a matching pad on the back of the die below it. Why both are needed: a 1,024-wire bus (2,048 for HBM4) cannot be wire-bonded around the edges of twelve dies (**wire bonding**, the conventional way to connect a die, loops a ~20 µm gold or copper wire from each pad on the die's top face out to the package, so it is limited to a few hundred connections around the die's perimeter); the wires have to go through the stack, and each die has to be joined to the next on every one of those wires.

The base die carries the **PHY** (the physical-layer circuits that drive and receive the signals to the GPU), the TSV signal routing, ECC (Section 2) and **RAS** logic (reliability, availability and serviceability features such as repair and error logging), temperature sensors, and the **Direct Access (DA)** test port used to test the stack after assembly. Through HBM3E the base die was made by the memory maker on its own DRAM periphery process; for HBM4 it moves to a logic-foundry process (Section 20). The core dies are DRAM: a 24 Gb HBM3E core die on a 1β process is ~110–120 mm², larger than a same-density DDR5 die because of the TSV area, the wider internal data path (each die contributes to 2–4 of the stack's 16 **channels**, the independent 64- or 128-bit slices of the interface, each with its own commands; HBM3 has 16, HBM4 32), and the keep-out zones around the TSVs.

## 16. The core die: TSV formation

An HBM core die is a DRAM die with an extra module inserted into the flow: **TSV-middle**, done after the transistors and capacitors (the **FEOL**, front end of line, the transistor-and-capacitor part of the flow before wiring) and before or during the first metal levels, so the vias connect to the die's metal from below. (TSV-first, before transistors, is not used because the copper cannot survive the DRAM thermal budget; TSV-last, from the back after BEOL, is used in interposers and image sensors but has worse pitch.) The problem the module solves is a hole ~50 µm deep that must be insulated, filled with copper without a void, and left flush with the surface so that ordinary wiring can be built over it.

1. **Via etch.** Photoresist over a hard mask defines ~5–6 µm diameter holes at ~40–50 µm pitch, in several dense columns down the middle of the die (some designs place TSV blocks under the I/O channels at both ends). A **Bosch process** (Module 09; deep-silicon etchers from SPTS, part of KLA, and Lam) etches them ~50–60 µm deep for a target final die thickness of ~45–50 µm (8-high), or ~35–40 µm deep for the ~30 µm dies used in 12- and 16-high stacks. The Bosch process works in three repeating moves: a **sulfur hexafluoride (SF6)** plasma etches silicon quickly in all directions; a pulse of **octafluorocyclobutane (C4F8)** deposits a Teflon-like film on every surface; the next etch pulse's vertical ions clear that film from the bottom only, so the hole advances downward in scallops. Pulses are ~1–3 s each at ~5–10 µm/min, and the scalloped sidewall must be smoothed (a short isotropic SF6 or oxidation-strip step) so the liner is continuous. Aspect ratio is ~10:1, which is easy compared to the capacitor etch; the difficulty is depth uniformity, because the via must be exposed uniformly during backside reveal.
2. **Liner, barrier, seed.** Sub-atmospheric CVD or ALD oxide (~200–500 nm) is the **liner** that insulates the copper from the silicon. A **PVD** (physical vapour deposition: sputtering metal atoms onto the surface) tantalum/tantalum nitride (Ta/TaN) or ALD TiN **barrier** (~20–50 nm) stops **copper diffusion**, because copper atoms that wander into silicon kill transistors. A PVD copper **seed** (~100–300 nm) follows, because electroplating can only grow copper on a conductive surface; the seed is thinnest at the bottom of the via, which is why the plating chemistry matters. Barrier and seed are deposited in Applied Materials Endura PVD systems.
3. **Copper electroplating.** A **bottom-up fill** in an acid copper sulfate bath (Lam SABRE 3D platers): suppressor, accelerator and leveler additives make copper grow faster at the bottom of the via than at its mouth, so the hole closes from the bottom without sealing a void (Module 12, at ~100× the via size); ~30–60 minutes per wafer for 50 µm vias. An anneal at ~350–400 °C stabilizes the copper's grain structure before BEOL, because copper expands ~6× more than silicon per degree and an un-annealed plug "pumps" out of the surface by ~100 nm on later heating, cracking the metal above it.
4. **CMP** (Applied Materials, Ebara) removes the overburden (the excess copper left on the surface) and the via is buried under the normal BEOL.

> **Worked example: copper pumping.** Copper's thermal expansion coefficient is ~17 × 10⁻⁶ per °C, silicon's ~2.6 × 10⁻⁶. Over a 350 °C anneal the free differential expansion of a 50 µm copper plug is (17 − 2.6) × 10⁻⁶ × 350 × 50 µm ≈ 0.25 µm. The surrounding silicon constrains most of that, but a plug whose copper grains are still rearranging pushes ~100 nm out of the surface and does not fully return. A tenth of a micron of movement under metal lines that are themselves only a fraction of a micron thick is a broken wire, which is why the anneal, which lets the grains settle once and for all, must come before any metal is built on top.

Each core die has **several thousand TSVs**: the 1,024 data bits plus command/address, power, ground and redundancy, with HBM4's 2,048-bit interface roughly doubling the signal count. Power and ground TSVs are the majority. Around each TSV a **keep-out zone** of ~5–10 µm is required because the thermal stress from the copper plug shifts transistor **mobility** (how easily electrons move through the channel; mechanical stress changes it, so a transistor near a TSV would run at a different speed from its twin elsewhere). No cells or sense amplifiers are placed there, and the TSV columns plus keep-out plus wider internal buses are the main reason an HBM die is ~1.5–1.8× the area of a DDR5 die of the same density.

> **What can go wrong.** *Axial plating voids*: a via whose mouth closed before the bottom filled has a void down its axis and a resistance that rises with every thermal cycle. *Pumping cracks*: an under-annealed plug lifts and cracks the metal line above it. *Liner cracks*: the oxide liner, squeezed between expanding copper and rigid silicon, cracks and leaks current into the substrate. *Depth non-uniformity*: vias etched a few microns shallower on one side of the wafer are never exposed by the backside reveal (Section 17), leaving open connections. Detection is by X-ray or CT imaging of sample vias for voids, and electrically by **daisy chains** at wafer sort, hundreds of TSVs wired in series so that one resistance measurement checks all of them.

## 17. Thinning, backside reveal and microbumps

A finished DRAM wafer is 775 µm thick. An HBM core die must end up at ~45–55 µm for 8-high HBM3E stacks, ~30–35 µm for 12-high (SK hynix's and Samsung's 12-high HBM3E dies are ~40% thinner than their 8-high dies, to hold the same 720 µm) and ~25–30 µm for 16-high stacks, thin enough that the copper TSVs poke through the back. For scale, 30 µm is about two sheets of kitchen foil, a third of the width of a hair.

1. **Temporary bonding.** The finished front side is bonded face-down to a glass or silicon carrier wafer with a temporary adhesive (Brewer Science, 3M, TOK) that releases with laser, heat or a slide-off mechanism; the bond and debond tools come from EV Group, SUSS MicroTec and TEL. The **total thickness variation (TTV)** of the adhesive layer (~1–2 µm) directly transfers into thinned-die thickness variation.
2. **Backgrinding.** A diamond grinding wheel (Disco DFG series) removes ~700 µm in a coarse pass, then a fine pass to within ~5 µm of the via bottoms; a stress-relief dry polish or CMP removes the ~1–2 µm grinding damage layer that would otherwise crack during bonding. A 300 mm wafer at 30 µm has no stiffness of its own; every subsequent step is done on the carrier.
3. **TSV reveal.** A selective silicon etch (SF6 dry, or a wet etch in **TMAH** or **KOH**, tetramethylammonium hydroxide or potassium hydroxide, alkaline solutions that dissolve silicon but not the copper's oxide sheath) recesses the silicon a few microns so the copper plugs, still sheathed in their oxide liner, stand ~1–3 µm above the silicon surface ("proud" of it, in engineering usage). A low-temperature (< 200 °C, to protect the temporary adhesive) PECVD SiN/SiO2 **passivation** (a protective insulating film) is deposited over everything and then CMP'd to open the copper tips. A backside **RDL** (redistribution layer, a thin wiring layer that reroutes pad positions) and pads are patterned over the tips.
4. **Microbumps.** On both faces (front-side bumps were plated before carrier bonding), ~20–25 µm-diameter **copper pillars** ~10–15 µm tall are electroplated through a thick photoresist, capped with a nickel (Ni) diffusion barrier and a **SnAg solder** tip (tin-silver, ~5–10 µm), at a pitch of ~25–55 µm depending on generation; HBM3E stacks have on the order of 10,000+ microbumps per interface. The bottom face of the base die carries the JEDEC **ball-out** (the standard map of bump positions) for attachment to the CoWoS interposer: several thousand microbumps at ~55 µm pitch (HBM2 through HBM3E), tighter for HBM4.
5. **Debond, dice, test.** The wafer is transferred to dicing tape, the carrier is released, and dies are singulated (cut apart) by laser **stealth dicing** (a laser focused inside the silicon creates a weak plane that the stretched tape snaps along, with no material lost to a saw cut) or plasma dicing, because blade dicing chips 30 µm silicon. Every die is probed at wafer level before this, and the TSV chain resistance and open/short status are tested, because a single failed TSV that is not covered by redundancy scraps the entire stack it is bonded into.

> **What can go wrong.** *Cracking*: below ~50 µm a die cracks under grinding force, at debond, or at pick-up; the damage-removal polish and a gentle release chemistry are the defence. *Carrier and adhesive TTV*: every micron of thickness variation in the adhesive becomes a micron of die-thickness variation, which becomes a bump-height error at stacking. *Reveal non-uniformity*: silicon etched a micron too little leaves TSV tips buried and open; too much leaves them tall and prone to breaking. *Adhesive residue*: a film left on the front-side bumps after debond stops the solder wetting. *Bump coplanarity*: pillars of unequal height across a die leave the short ones unjoined. Detection: TTV metrology on every wafer, acoustic microscopy for delamination, and automated bump-height inspection before the die is released to stacking.

## 18. Stacking: TC-NCF versus MR-MUF

Here is the problem: join twelve 30 µm dies, each slightly warped, on ~10,000 joints per interface, with no voids, no cracks and no shorts, while leaving a path for heat to get out through the whole pile. Two process families exist and the difference between them decided the HBM3 market.

> **Intuition: ironing sheets one at a time, or baking the pile once.** TC-NCF is ironing: each sheet (die) is pressed onto the pile, one at a time, with a hot iron, through a sheet of glue film, and the glue sets under the iron. MR-MUF is stacking every sheet with dabs of solder between them, baking the whole pile once so every dab melts at the same moment, and then pouring resin into all the gaps. Ironing gives a sure bond on every sheet but takes twelve passes; baking is fast and gentle but the resin has to reach every corner.

The stack is built die-on-wafer: a base-die wafer (or a **reconstituted carrier wafer**, a wafer-shaped plate holding many already-diced base dies in a moulded array) is the substrate, and core dies are placed on it one at a time, with each die's front-side bumps landing on the previous die's revealed backside pads with an alignment of ~1–2 µm. In both families the gap around the bumps is filled with **underfill**, a resin that spreads the mechanical stress across the whole die face so that the solder joints do not crack; the families differ in when and how it gets there.

**Thermo-compression bonding with non-conductive film (TC-NCF).** Samsung's process (and, in a variant, Micron's). A **non-conductive film (NCF)**, an epoxy sheet ~10–20 µm thick pre-laminated onto the wafer before dicing, covers each die's bump face. A **thermo-compression bonder**, the tool for **thermo-compression bonding (TCB)**, the general name for joining two parts with heat and pressure at once (Hanmi, Hanwha, ASMPT, BESI, Shinkawa make them), picks up a die, aligns it, presses it onto the stack at ~250–300 °C and a few tens of newtons for ~5–15 seconds; the solder melts and joins through the softened NCF while the film simultaneously cures into underfill. Advantages: the underfill is in place before the joint forms, so there is no **capillary underfill** step (resin drawn into the gap by surface tension after bonding) and no unfilled voids under the die. Disadvantages: it is serial (12 heated dwells per stack); the heat and force on a 30 µm die over a warped stack create bump **non-wets** (joints where the solder failed to bond to the pad) and cracks; the NCF's thermal conductivity is low (~0.3–0.5 W/m·K, watts per metre-kelvin, the unit of thermal conductivity; that is roughly a plastic, where copper is ~400 W/m·K and silicon ~150); and its cure shrinkage adds warpage that compounds die after die. Samsung's difficulty passing NVIDIA's HBM3/HBM3E qualification through 2024 (thermal and power-related failures reported by Reuters) is widely attributed to the heat and warpage behaviour of a 12-high TC-NCF stack. Samsung reworked the process and finally passed the 12-high HBM3E qualification in September 2025, about 18 months after its first attempt.

**Mass reflow with molded underfill (MR-MUF).** SK hynix's process, introduced for HBM2E and refined for HBM3 (2022) and HBM3E. Dies are placed with a high-speed **flip-chip bonder** (a placement machine that sets each die face-down onto the stack) using **flux** (a chemical that strips oxide from the solder and pad so the solder wets) or fluxless plasma-activated placement, but *not* joined individually; once all 8 or 12 are stacked, the whole batch of stacks passes through a reflow oven (**mass reflow**: heating the whole batch until every solder tip melts and flows at the same moment, peak ~240–260 °C for SnAg) and all the joints form at once. Then a liquid **epoxy molding compound (EMC)**, the resin that normally forms the black body of a packaged chip, formulated with fine silica **filler** (glass particles that lower its expansion and raise its conductivity), is injected under pressure and vacuum to flow into the ~10–15 µm gaps between dies and over the sides, and cured: the **molded underfill (MUF)** is at once the underfill, the sidewall protection and the mold body. Advantages: parallel joint formation (throughput ~2–3× TC-NCF), no per-bond force on thin dies, an EMC whose thermal conductivity SK hynix quotes at ~2× that of NCF, and a filler-matched expansion coefficient that holds the stack flatter. Disadvantage: forcing a viscous, filler-loaded liquid into a 10 µm gap without voids is hard and gets harder as gaps close; a void next to a bump is a thermal and reliability failure. For 12-high HBM3E SK hynix introduced **Advanced MR-MUF** (2023–24): thinner dies (~40% thinner than the 8-high), a new lower-viscosity EMC with finer filler, chip-warpage control during stacking, and a redesigned mold flow, yielding a 12-high stack in the same 720 µm as the old 8-high. NVIDIA's H100 (HBM3, 2022–23) was effectively sole-sourced from SK hynix, and the H200/B200 HBM3E majority remained with SK hynix, largely because MR-MUF stacks passed qualification on thermals and yield first.

> **Worked example: bonder throughput.** A TC bonder dwells ~10–15 s on each of 12 dies, plus pick, align and place, so ~3 minutes per stack per bonder, or ~20 stacks per hour and ~14,000 per month at perfect utilization. A flip-chip placement takes ~2–3 s per die, ~40 s per stack, and the reflow oven and molding press handle hundreds of stacks at a time; the placement step alone is ~4–5× faster, and the shared reflow and molding steps bring the net advantage down to the ~2–3× quoted. A fab building ~1 million stacks a month would need on the order of ~70 TC bonders at perfect utilization and ~100 in practice, versus ~15–20 flip-chip bonders (all of these are order-of-magnitude figures; bonder cycle times are proprietary).

> **What can go wrong.** *Non-wets*: solder that never bonded to its pad, from oxide, flux residue or a short pillar; an open channel. *Bump cracks on warped stacks*: a die pressed onto a stack that is no longer flat cracks the joints at its edges. *NCF voids*: air trapped in the film as it softens, which becomes a hot spot or a leakage path. *MUF voids at bumps*: the filled resin fails to reach the last corner of a 10 µm gap. *Cumulative warpage*: each layer's cure shrinkage bends the pile a little more, so the twelfth die lands on a curved surface. *Die shift in reflow*: a die floating on molten solder drifts a micron or two before the joints freeze. Detection: X-ray for voids and shifts, **C-SAM** (acoustic microscopy, which images delamination through the stack with ultrasound) for gaps, and the DA-port electrical test that exercises every channel.

**Micron** uses a TCB flow with NCF but entered HBM3E in 2024 (8-high for H200, then 12-high) with a core die on its 1β node whose lower power (~30% lower than competitors' HBM3E, per Micron) offset the process's thermal handicap. It went from ~5% HBM share in 2023 to ~21% in 2025.

> **Worked example: the height budget.** JEDEC set the HBM3E package height at ~720 µm and, in the HBM4 standard, relaxed it to **775 µm** for both 12- and 16-high stacks (reportedly heading toward ~900 µm for later generations). An 8-high HBM3E with 50 µm core dies: 8 × 50 = 400 µm of silicon, plus 8 bump/underfill gaps at ~15 µm ≈ 120 µm, plus a ~50–80 µm base die (base dies are thinned too, but less), plus ~30 µm of solder on the bottom → ~600–630 µm. A 12-high in the same 720 µm needs ~30–35 µm dies: 12 × 33 = 400 µm of silicon, 12 gaps at ~12–15 µm ≈ 160 µm, base ~60 µm, solder ~30 µm → ~650–700 µm. The stack fits only because the top die is kept slightly thicker for handling and everything else is squeezed. A 16-high in 775 µm: 16 × 30 µm = 480 µm of silicon, 16 gaps × 12 µm = 190 µm, base ~50 µm, bottom solder ~30 µm → ~750 µm, leaving only ~25 µm of margin, feasible with microbumps *only* if the dies go to ~30 µm and the gap shrinks to ~10–12 µm, which is what Advanced MR-MUF and thinner NCF are for; it is also why the "20 µm too tall lifts the lid" rule of Section 22 bites hardest on 16-high. Removing the gaps entirely (hybrid bonding, Section 19) would free ~190 µm and allow 20-high stacks or 40 µm dies; that is why the standard's height number decides which bonding technology wins.

## 19. Hybrid bonding: the 16-high and HBM4E question

Microbumps set a floor on the gap between dies (~10 µm) and on pitch (~25 µm), and the solder joints are the stack's thermal bottleneck. **Hybrid bonding** eliminates them: two faces, each a flat dielectric with copper pads in it, are pressed together so that dielectric bonds to dielectric and copper to copper, with no solder, no underfill and no gap. The sequence, for **die-to-wafer** bonding (one die at a time onto a wafer, as HBM needs):

1. **Recess CMP.** Each die's face is planarized to a dielectric of SiCN or SiO2 with copper pads at ≤ 10 µm pitch, polished so that the copper sits a few nanometres *below* the dielectric surface, at a roughness of < 0.5 nm.
2. **Plasma activation and rinse.** A brief plasma breaks bonds at the dielectric surface to make it chemically hungry, then a deionized-water rinse leaves it terminated with hydroxyl (OH) groups that will bond to their counterparts on the other face.
3. **Room-temperature placement.** The die is placed with sub-micron accuracy and the two dielectrics stick on contact by **van der Waals forces**, the weak attraction between any two surfaces flat enough to touch at the atomic scale.
4. **Batch anneal at 200–300 °C.** The dielectric surfaces bond **covalently** (true chemical bonds replace the weak attraction), and the copper pads, expanding more than the dielectric around them, close the recess and press into each other to form a direct Cu-Cu bond.

The result: 16 × 35 µm dies stack in ~600 µm with a continuous copper thermal path through the TSVs. The demands are extreme: particle-free surfaces (a 100 nm particle makes a millimetre-scale void), sub-µm die-to-wafer placement, and a different finish on both faces of every core die: instead of copper pillars with solder tips on the front and revealed TSV tips with an RDL on the back, both faces must end as a flat SiCN or oxide surface with copper pads recessed a few nanometres and polished to < 0.5 nm roughness, so the microbump step of Section 17 disappears and the TSV reveal of Section 17 and the last metal level of Section 16 both change. What goes wrong: particle voids; a **wrong-sign recess**, where copper that protrudes above the dielectric holds the two dielectrics apart so nothing bonds; misalignment that leaves pads half-overlapped; and any change in die finish upstream, since the surface is the process. BESI (with Applied Materials) and ASMPT supply die-to-wafer hybrid bonders; EV Group, SUSS MicroTec and TEL supply the wafer-to-wafer bonders used for bonded-periphery NAND (Section 11); all three memory makers have pilot lines. JEDEC's April 2025 decision to allow 16-high HBM4 at 775 µm with microbumps postponed that moment: the industry's expectation as of ~2026 is microbump TCB/MR-MUF for HBM4 12- and 16-high, with hybrid bonding arriving with HBM4E or HBM5 (~2027–2029), sooner if a customer demands 20-high stacks.

## 20. The base die moves to a logic foundry

Section 14 ended with the observation that the base die, not the DRAM cell, is the HBM4 design problem. Here is why. The base die is the stack's only logic chip, and it has four jobs. The **PHY** drives and receives 2,048 data lines plus command, address and clock at 8–11 Gbps; physically, per channel, that is a bank of transmitters, receivers, clock trees and **DLL/PLL** timing circuits that keep each data lane aligned to its clock. The **TSV interface** routes each channel's data up the vertical bus to the right core die and selects among the dies' outputs. The **ECC and RAS logic** does on-die error correction, row and column repair and error logging. And the housekeeping: the DA test port (Section 21), **IEEE 1500** test wrappers (a standard test-access structure around each die that lets a tester reach it through the base die), temperature sensors and voltage regulation. HBM4 adds an optional fifth: customer-specific logic such as a cache, a compression engine or an address-translation block that a **hyperscaler** (one of the handful of companies, Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta, that run data centres of millions of servers and increasingly design their own accelerators) wants close to the memory.

Through HBM3E, the base die was made by the memory maker on its own DRAM periphery process (Section 5), which was fine for 1,024 pins at up to ~9 Gbps: the PHY was small enough and the routing simple enough. It is not fine for HBM4, for three reasons. **Transistor speed and power.** A DRAM periphery transistor is ~4–5 nodes behind logic; a receiver that must resolve an 11 Gbps signal, and a transmitter that must launch one, need the switching speed and low capacitance of a logic transistor, with circuits closer to the high-speed links of a network chip than to a DRAM's I/O, and the interface's energy per bit, which sets much of the ~30–40 W an HBM4 stack draws at speed (Section 22), is largely set by the PHY. **Wiring density.** The 2,048 data lines plus command, address, clock and the many power and ground connections have to be fanned out from the ~55 µm-pitch bump array on the bottom face to the TSV columns in the middle of an 11 × 13 mm die; on 3–4 DRAM metal levels that routing does not close, and a logic process's ~10 or more levels at finer pitch is what makes it tractable. **Logic content.** The ECC, RAS, test and any customer logic are ordinary digital blocks; a DRAM periphery process has only a thin digital design kit, a small standard-cell library and little licensed **IP** (intellectual property: pre-designed circuit blocks such as a PHY that a design team licenses rather than draws from scratch), whereas a foundry node has mature libraries, PHY IP and the design tools that go with them.

For HBM4, SK hynix's base die is built by **TSMC on N12 (12FFC+)**, TSMC's 12 nm-class **FinFET** logic process from ~2018 (a FinFET is the transistor whose channel is a vertical fin that the gate grips on three sides, Module 11), a mature and cheap node, in mass production, with N5/N3 planned for HBM4E and a custom-base-die program; Micron kept its HBM4 base die on its own in-house CMOS process and moves to TSMC-made base dies only with HBM4E (2027); Samsung, which owns a logic foundry, makes its HBM4 base die on its own **4 nm** process. Why N12 is enough now: at 8–11 Gbps a 12 nm FinFET PHY has speed to spare, and the die's ~100+ mm² area is set by the stack footprint and the bump map rather than by transistor count, so on a mature node's cheaper wafer the cost per mm² matters more than density. Why N5/N3 later: HBM4E pushes pins to 13–16 Gbps, customer logic wants density, and every picojoule per bit shaved in the PHY is real power.

> **Worked example: what a picojoule per bit is worth.** A Rubin-class stack runs at ~2.75 TB/s, which is 2.2 × 10¹³ bits per second. Each picojoule per bit of interface energy is therefore 2.2 × 10¹³ × 10⁻¹² ≈ 22 W per stack, ~175 W across eight stacks. If moving the PHY from a DRAM-periphery process to N12 saves ~0.5 pJ/bit and N3 another ~0.5 pJ/bit (order-of-magnitude figures; vendors quote total interface energies of ~3–4 pJ/bit for HBM3E and target lower for HBM4E), each step is worth ~10 W per stack, ~90 W per GPU package, on a package whose cooling is already the limiting design constraint (Section 22). That, more than the base die's own speed, is why the base-die node keeps advancing.

This is a structural change: the memory company now buys a wafer from a foundry (or, for Samsung, from its own foundry arm), and the base die is co-designed by four parties. The memory maker owns the core-die TSV map (which via carries which signal, fixed by the DRAM design) and the stack's power delivery; the foundry provides the transistor process, the design rules and the PHY IP (TSMC and SK hynix have co-developed the HBM4 base die under their April 2024 agreement); the GPU vendor's matching PHY at the other end of the interposer traces sets the signalling protocol and timing; and TSMC's CoWoS team fixes the bump map on the base die's bottom face, because those bumps must land on the interposer's routing (Module 17). The base die's TSV landing pads on its top face must match the core dies' bump positions to ~1 µm, its bottom bump map must match JEDEC's ball-out and the interposer, and the whole thing is taped out at the foundry like any logic chip, on a foundry schedule. It also means an HBM4 stack's cost includes a ~100+ mm² logic die at foundry prices, and that a memory maker without a logic foundry, its own or a partner's, cannot make HBM4 at all.

> **What can go wrong.** *PHY-to-PHY mismatch*: the base-die PHY and the GPU's PHY are designed by different companies to the same JEDEC specification, and an interface that trains and runs at 8 Gbps on the tester can fail to close timing at 11 Gbps across a real interposer; this is found only at CoWoS-level test (Module 18) and is why every HBM4 vendor had to qualify against each GPU separately (Section 24). *Bump and TSV map errors*: a base die whose top-face pads are offset from the core-die bumps, or whose bottom map disagrees with the interposer, is a full mask-set respin at the foundry, months of delay. *Foundry lead time*: the base die is now on a foundry queue, so a memory maker's HBM4 ramp depends on TSMC's N12 capacity and schedule, a dependency it never had before.

## 21. Testing and known-good stacks

A 12-high stack has 13 dies and ~100,000+ microbump joints; if each already-selected die has 99% residual good-die probability and each bonding step has 99% success probability, an independent model gives stack yield 0.99^13 × 0.99^12 ≈ 78%; at 98% for both conditional inputs it is ~60%. Raw wafer-sort pass fraction is not this die-quality input: rejected dies never enter the stack. HBM assembly yield is reported in the 60–80% range for 12-high, versus > 90% for conventional DRAM packaging, so every layer of test is aimed at not stacking a bad die. The test flow has three gates, on three different machines.

1. **Core-die wafer sort**, before thinning (Module 14). Every die on the 1β wafer is contacted by a **probe card** (a plate carrying thousands of fine spring contacts, from FormFactor and Technoprobe) on **automated test equipment (ATE)** from Advantest and Teradyne, at a hot corner (~85–95 °C) and a cold one. The tests: a functional write and read of every bit; **retention screening**, in which every cell is written, left for the refresh interval and longer at the hot corner, and read back, to catch the tail cells of Section 2; at-speed patterns that exercise the sense amplifiers, decoders and DLLs; and the TSV **daisy chains** of Section 16. Failing rows and columns are swapped for spares by programming **antifuses** (one-time electrically programmable links; the same mechanism gives DDR5 and HBM their **post-package repair**, a spare row that can be assigned after the part is sealed). Dies that pass with spare capacity left over are the **known-good dies (KGD)**. Retention screening is the longest part, because a 64 ms retention cannot be tested faster than 64 ms per pass and many passes at several temperatures are needed; it is why memory ATE is built to test hundreds of dies on a wafer in parallel.
2. **Base-die wafer sort**, on the foundry-made logic wafer (Section 20): scan test of the digital logic, a PHY **loopback** test (the transmitter's output is routed back into its own receiver so the link can be exercised without a partner chip), and TSV chains.
3. **Stack test**, after stacking and molding, through the base die's DA port: a few dozen extra bumps on the bottom face that give a tester access to every channel without driving the 1,024- or 2,048-bit bus, which would need a tester channel for every one of several thousand bumps. The tester runs bump and TSV open/short tests through the IEEE 1500 wrappers of each die; at-speed channel tests using the PHY's loopback and the core dies' internal pattern generators; a full functional and retention test of every die in the stack at temperature, repeated because stacking (thermal cycles, TSV stress) can create new tail bits; and **burn-in**, running the stack hot and at elevated voltage (typically ~125 °C, for hours; the recipe is proprietary; Module 18) to force out **infant mortality**, the failures that would otherwise occur in the first weeks of service. A final functional test at room and hot temperature after burn-in confirms the stack, now a **known-good stack (KGS)**.

The memory makers do not publish gate-by-gate fall-out; the 60–80% assembly-yield figure is the aggregate, and most of it is lost at bonding (non-wets, cracks, voids, Section 18) and found at the stack's first electrical test rather than at burn-in, which typically removes a further few percent. The KGS ships to TSMC or the GPU vendor's **OSAT** (an outsourced assembly-and-test company such as ASE or Amkor), where it is placed on the CoWoS interposer; a failure discovered there scraps the interposer, the HBM stacks and potentially the ~$10,000+ GPU die, so stack-level test is exhaustive and expensive, on the order of 5–10% of stack cost, most of it tester time for the hot retention and burn-in steps.

> **What can go wrong.** *Test escapes*: a TSV or bump that measures within limits at room temperature and opens at 85 °C under a running GPU; the defences are testing at temperature and at speed, and the RAS logging on the base die that lets a marginal channel be retired in the field. *Untestable at pitch*: the 2,048-bit interface cannot be probed directly, so the at-speed test through the DA port and loopback is an approximation of the real GPU link, and the first true full-speed test happens after CoWoS assembly, on a package worth more than ten thousand dollars (Module 18). *Repair exhaustion*: a core die that used most of its spare rows at wafer sort has none left for the tail bits that appear after stacking, so KGD criteria include a margin of unused spares. *Burn-in escapes and over-stress*: a burn-in too short ships early-life failures; one too long consumes part of the product's own lifetime. *Test-time cost*: every extra hour of retention or burn-in per stack is tester capacity, and during the 2024–25 HBM ramp tester capacity was at times reported to be as tight as bonder capacity.

## 22. Thermal issues

On a B200 package, each HBM3E stack sits ~1 mm from a GPU die dissipating ~1,000 W (Rubin: reported at ~1,800–2,300 W depending on the source and configuration, Rubin Ultra more). The heat spreader/lid that cools the GPU also touches the tops of the stacks, so the stacks are cooled from above and heated from the side and from below through the interposer. DRAM's **junction temperature** limit (the temperature of the silicon itself, as opposed to the case or the air) is ~95–105 °C; above 85 °C the refresh rate doubles (JEDEC 2× refresh), which steals bandwidth and adds power, and retention failures rise exponentially with temperature. The stack's internal thermal resistance is dominated by the 12 bonding layers (underfill and solder), which is why MR-MUF's higher-conductivity EMC and hybrid bonding's absence of any interface layer matter; the bottom dies, nearest the interposer, run hottest, with a ~10–15 °C gradient across a 12-high.

> **Worked example: the bonding layers as thermal resistors (a simplified model).** The thermal resistance of a slab is R = t / (k × A), where t is its thickness, k its thermal conductivity and A its area. A 15 µm NCF layer at k ≈ 0.4 W/m·K over an 11 × 11 mm die (1.21 × 10⁻⁴ m²) is R ≈ 15 × 10⁻⁶ / (0.4 × 1.21 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 0.3 K/W (kelvin per watt, the unit of thermal resistance: how many degrees hotter the hot side gets for each watt flowing through); twelve of them in series are ~3.7 K/W, so even ~3 W of heat conducted vertically through the stack produces a > 10 °C gradient, which is the ~10–15 °C measured. A 10 µm MUF gap at ~0.8 W/m·K is ~0.1 K/W per layer, a third as much; a hybrid-bonded interface, copper on copper and dielectric on dielectric, is close to zero. The model ignores the copper microbumps conducting in parallel through the resin and the heat leaving sideways, so treat it as the shape of the answer, not the number.

Stack height is fixed by JEDEC precisely so that one flat lid can touch the GPU and all eight stacks: a stack 20 µm too tall lifts the lid off the GPU. HBM4's added power (~30–40 W per stack at speed) and 16-high stacks are among the reasons Rubin-class packages are liquid-cooled at the rack.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Two consequences leave this section. The 85 °C refresh doubling of Section 2 means a stack that runs hot delivers less bandwidth and burns more power on the same workload, so the bonding material chosen in Section 18 shows up in the GPU's performance. And the 20 µm lid-planarity rule is a CoWoS assembly specification (Module 17): the height budget of Section 18 exists so that eight stacks and a GPU can share one lid.

## 23. Economics: price, wafer consumption and the shortage

HBM sells for roughly **3–5× the price per bit of DDR5**: as of ~2025, HBM3E at roughly $13–20 per GB in contract (analyst estimates, not disclosed prices), so ~$500–700 for a 36 GB 12-high stack and ~$2,500–3,500 of HBM on a single 180–192 GB B200, versus ~$3–5 per GB for DDR5 (before the 2025–26 DRAM price spike). The 2025–26 conventional DRAM shortage, caused by the same wafers being diverted to HBM, pushed DDR5 prices up 2–3× and narrowed the premium; TrendForce noted in early 2026 that a wafer of DDR5 for 64 GB **RDIMMs** (registered DIMMs, the server memory module with a buffer chip on it) briefly out-earned a wafer of HBM.

The reason HBM strains the whole DRAM industry is **wafer consumption per bit**. [TrendForce’s June 2, 2026 analysis](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20260602-13074.html) estimated that HBM was ~18% of the top three suppliers' DRAM wafer input at the end of 2025 for ~8% of DRAM bit supply, heading to ~30% of wafers for ~13% of bits by 2027: consistent with a substantial wafer-per-bit penalty. These are dated analyst estimates, including an end-of-year wafer-input snapshot, rather than an audited annual production balance. The following **2.5× wafer-per-bit model** is a separate illustrative comparison. Three factors compound: the larger die (TSVs, keep-out, wider datapath), the lower stack yield, and the fact that every HBM core die is made on the newest node (1β, then 1γ) where wafer output per tool is lowest. At a 2.5× illustrative wafer-per-bit penalty, redirecting 100k existing wafer starts per month from DDR5 to HBM removes the former 100k-wafer DDR5 output and produces HBM bits equivalent to only ~40k DDR5 wafers: a net loss of ~60k DDR5-wafer-equivalents of total bits. Conversely, making as many HBM bits as 100k DDR5 wafers would produce requires ~250k HBM wafer starts. These are different comparisons; adding genuinely new capacity need not displace existing DDR5 output.

> **Worked example: wafers for a million GPUs.** Assume a GPU carrying 8 stacks of 12-high HBM3E (36 GB, 288 GB per GPU), 1 million GPUs per year. Core die: 24 Gb on 1β, ~120 mm² (10 × 12 mm). Gross dies per 300 mm wafer follow the Module 13 formula N ≈ π(d/2)²/A − πd/√(2A), where d = 300 mm is the wafer diameter, A the die area in mm², and the second term removes the partial dies at the edge: π(150)²/120 − π·300/√(2·120) ≈ 589 − 61 ≈ 528. At 80% wafer-sort yield, ~420 good dies per wafer, or 35 stacks' worth of core dies per wafer. Base dies (~120 mm² on TSMC N12, ~90% yield) give ~470 per wafer. Stack assembly yield ~90% means 8 million stacks require ~8.9 million stack attempts: 8.9 M × 12 / 420 ≈ 254,000 core-die wafers plus 8.9 M / 470 ≈ 19,000 base-die wafers, ~273,000 wafers per year, or ~23,000 wafers per month. For scale, a large DRAM fab makes ~100–150k wafers per month, and the industry's ~300k wafers per month of HBM in 2025 corresponds to ~13 million GPU-equivalents of demand, consistent with NVIDIA's plus AMD's, Google's, Amazon's and Broadcom's customers' accelerators plus inventory. Per bit: the HBM wafer yields 35 × 36 GB ≈ 1.26 TB of stacked capacity (before the 10% stack loss), while a DDR5 wafer of 24 Gb dies at ~75 mm² yields ~850 gross × 0.9 × 3 GB ≈ 2.3 TB, a ~1.8× wafer penalty before stacking losses and the base-die wafer, or ~2.2× all-in, which matches the TrendForce ratio. For HBM4 16-high, multiply core-die wafers by 16/12, and the core dies move to 1γ.

## 24. Who supplies whom

- **2022–2023 (H100, HBM3):** SK hynix essentially sole source. Samsung's HBM3 qualified late for a minority of H100 volume; Micron had no HBM3.
- **2024 (H200, B200, HBM3E):** SK hynix ~50–60% of HBM bits, majority of NVIDIA; Micron entered with 8-high (H200, then 12-high for Blackwell); Samsung's 8-high qualified for lower-tier NVIDIA parts and AMD MI300, but the 12-high failed repeated NVIDIA quals.
- **2025:** SK hynix ~57–62% of HBM bit shipments, Micron ~21% (overtaking Samsung in Q2), Samsung ~17–22%, recovering after its 12-high HBM3E qualification in September. HBM3E was in shortage all year; SK hynix reported its 2025 HBM sold out by early 2025, and 2026 allocations were contracted in 2025. The HBM lead is what put SK hynix ahead of Samsung in DRAM revenue for three quarters of the year (Section 8).
- **2026 (Rubin, HBM4):** NVIDIA qualified all three suppliers for Vera Rubin (Samsung and SK hynix received allocations in March 2026; Jensen Huang confirmed all three in production by mid-2026), with SK hynix reported by analysts at anywhere from ~50% to ~70% of NVIDIA's HBM4 (estimates that will shift as the others ramp), Samsung a strong second with its own 4 nm base die, and Micron (in-house base die) supplying Rubin as well as AMD and custom **ASICs** (application-specific integrated circuits: accelerators designed for one customer's workload, such as Google's TPU, designed with Broadcom, rather than sold as a general-purpose GPU). (The Rubin CPX inference part uses GDDR7, not HBM.) Counterpoint's Q2 2026 estimate of overall HBM share: SK hynix ~50%, Samsung ~33%, Micron ~18%; Counterpoint also noted that Samsung was the first to mass-produce HBM4.

## 25. Handoff to CoWoS

The HBM stack leaves the memory maker as a tested, molded cube ~11 × 11 × 0.72 mm (HBM4: reported ~11 × 13 × 0.775 mm) with several thousand solder microbumps at ~55 µm pitch on the bottom of its base die. It goes to TSMC (or Samsung, Intel or an OSAT such as ASE/SPIL and Amkor for non-TSMC packages), where in **CoWoS-S/-L** (Module 17) it is placed, with the GPU dies, on a silicon interposer or an **LSI-bridge organic substrate** (local silicon interconnect: a resin substrate with small silicon bridge chiplets embedded only where the fine wiring is needed) whose fine-pitch RDL (the redistribution wiring of Section 17) carries the 1,024 or 2,048 data lines the ~2–4 mm from the HBM PHY on the base die to the matching PHY on the GPU. The interposer's own TSVs, the microbump joint between HBM and interposer, the warpage of a ~3,000 mm² assembly and the lid that must touch nine dies at once are Module 17's problems; the memory maker's job ends at the known-good stack.

## Summary

- Memory is a commodity business scored on cost per bit: one optimized cell repeated ~17 billion times per die, 3–4 metal layers, and a periphery several nodes behind logic; the fabs are ALD and high-aspect-ratio-etch fabs, not BEOL fabs.
- A DRAM bit is ~40,000 electrons on a ~12 fF capacitor; reading shares that charge with a ~35 fF bit line and yields ~140 mV, which a cross-coupled sense amplifier amplifies to full rail and writes back; leakage sets a device- and temperature-specific refresh window, illustrated here by conventional DDR values of 64 ms and 32 ms at elevated temperature.
- Because the sense amplifier needs ≳6–10 fF, the capacitor has kept its capacitance while its footprint shrank 20× by growing to ~1.2–1.5 µm tall at ~20–35 nm wide on a honeycomb pitch of ~33–50 nm (aspect ratio > 40; a hollow cup at 1x, a solid pillar at 1β), built from ALD ZAZ/TiN films (EOT ~0.55 nm) inside an etched, later dissolved, oxide mold held up by nitride supporters.
- The 6F² cell (2F word-line pitch × 3F bit-line pitch, ~0.001 µm² at F ≈ 12.5 nm) works because the access transistor is buried in a trench, giving a longer effective channel and a free surface for the contacts; its successors are the 4F² vertical channel (~2027–28) and 3D DRAM (~2028–30).
- DRAM nodes 1x to 1γ are ~19–11 nm half-pitch; EUV replaces 4-pass DUV on a few layers and only pays at high tool throughput, which is why Micron waited until 1γ (2025); a 1β wafer holds ~1.7 TB of good DRAM at ~$1–1.5 per GB.
- A NAND cell stores a bit as electrons tunnelled through a ~7 nm oxide at ~1 V/nm (18–22 V on the gate, because of the ~0.5–0.6 coupling ratio) into a nitride charge-trap layer, shifting the threshold voltage; TLC/QLC divide a ~6–8 V window into 8 or 16 states with ISPP and LDPC, trading endurance (100k → ~1k cycles) for density.
- 3D NAND is deposited as ~300 alternating oxide/nitride layers (~13–16 µm), drilled with ~100 nm holes at ~40–60:1 aspect ratio per deck on 3-deck designs and up to ~80–100:1 on deeper single etches (the longest step, ~1 hour per wafer per deck, cryogenic etch), lined with the gate stack and a polysilicon "macaroni" channel, then the nitride is dissolved in hot phosphoric acid through slits and replaced with tungsten or molybdenum word lines.
- Layer counts (SK hynix 321, Samsung ~286, Micron 276, Kioxia/SanDisk 218 → 332) are built as 2–3 stacked decks; density comes from height, bits per cell and periphery placement (CuA/PUC/COP or wafer-bonded Xtacking/CBA), not from lithography.
- HBM is wide and slow: 1,024 pins (HBM4: 2,048) at 6–11 Gbps over millimetre traces on an interposer at ~3–4 pJ/bit give 0.8–2.8 TB/s per stack; eight stacks feed a B200 (~8 TB/s) or Rubin (22 TB/s).
- An HBM core die is a DRAM die with a TSV-middle module (5–6 µm Bosch-etched, copper-plated, annealed vias), thinned on a carrier to 30–50 µm, revealed from the back and bumped with ~25 µm copper pillars; the JEDEC height (720 µm HBM3E, 775 µm HBM4) sets how thin the dies must be.
- Stacking is done either by thermo-compression bonding through a non-conductive film (Samsung, Micron) or by mass reflow plus molded underfill (SK hynix); MR-MUF's parallel joints, lower force and ~2× more conductive fill won SK hynix most of NVIDIA's HBM3/3E business; hybrid bonding (no bumps, no gaps) is expected at HBM4E/HBM5.
- HBM costs 3–5× DDR5 per bit and consumes 2–3 wafers per DDR5 wafer-equivalent (larger dies, 60–80% stack yield, a foundry base die, the newest node), which is why diverting wafers to HBM caused the 2025–26 DRAM shortage; HBM shares as of Q2 2026 (Counterpoint) are SK hynix ~50%, Samsung ~33%, Micron ~18%.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"A 1β DRAM is a 12 nm chip like a 12 nm logic chip."** → 1β refers to a ~12–13 nm half-pitch of one cell feature; the periphery transistors are ~4–5 nodes behind logic, the die has 3–4 metal layers, and the density comes from a capacitor that is 1.2 µm tall, not from the transistor.
- **"HBM is fast memory."** → Per pin, HBM is the slowest DRAM interface in production (6–13 Gbps versus 28–32 Gbps for GDDR7); its bandwidth comes from 1,024–2,048 pins over millimetre-long traces on an interposer. Its latency is no better than DDR5.
- **"HBM costs 3–5× more because the DRAM inside is exotic."** → The core dies are standard 1β/1γ DRAM cells; the premium comes from the TSV module, a die ~1.5–1.8× larger per bit, 60–80% stack yield, a foundry-made base die, a serial stacking process and exhaustive test.
- **"Layer count is a direct measure of 3D NAND density."** → Vendors count dummy and select layers differently, string-stacked decks add junction layers, and density also depends on hole pitch, bits per cell and periphery placement; a 218-layer bonded-periphery die can match a 238-layer conventional one.
- **"EUV is what makes new DRAM denser."** → EUV replaces 4-pass DUV multipatterning on a few layers at lower cost and better overlay; the density gains at 1α–1γ come from cell layout and capacitor engineering. Micron shipped 1β with no EUV at competitive density.
- **"HBM4 uses hybrid bonding."** → As of 2026, HBM4 12- and 16-high stacks ship with microbumps (TC-NCF or MR-MUF); JEDEC's 775 µm height allowance made this possible, and hybrid bonding is expected at HBM4E/HBM5.
- **"The tungsten word lines of 3D NAND are deposited as tungsten."** → They are deposited as silicon nitride, and only after the channel, staircase and slit are formed is the nitride dissolved in hot phosphoric acid and replaced with tungsten (or molybdenum) through the slit.
- **"NAND is slow because the cell is slow."** → A single cell switches in nanoseconds; NAND is slow to write because ISPP programs each page in a loop of pulse-and-verify steps (~1–3 ms for QLC) and slow to read because the current must pass through hundreds of cells in series along a resistive word line, not because of anything in the cell itself.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Memory fabs consume the same inputs as the logic fabs of Modules 05–13: Shin-Etsu/SUMCO polished wafers (Module 03), specialty gases and ALD precursors such as TEMAZ, TMA, TiCl4 and WF6 (Module 04), and the deposition, lithography, etch, implant and metrology tools of Modules 06–13, in a different mix that is heavy on ALD and HAR etch and light on BEOL and EUV. They also consume Module 14's wafer sort more intensively than any logic product, because HBM stacking is only viable with known-good dies. Their outputs go three ways: DDR5/LPDDR5X and NAND ship as packaged components (Module 16 covers the conventional packaging) into servers, phones and SSDs; HBM stacks ship as known-good stacks to TSMC's CoWoS lines, where Module 17 places them beside the GPU dies on an interposer, and where Module 18's system-level test finds the stacks that the memory maker's test did not. Module 19 puts eight of them on a Blackwell or Rubin package, 72 of those in an NVL72 rack, and shows why the HBM supply plan is the first line item in every AI capacity forecast.
