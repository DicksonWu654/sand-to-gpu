# Module 13: Metrology, Inspection and Yield

A leading-edge wafer passes through roughly 1,000 to 1,500 process steps over three to four months. Any one of them can go wrong in a way that is invisible to the naked eye, to an optical microscope, and often to the tool that caused it. A 2 nm-class transistor has a gate length near 12 nm, a **nanosheet** (one of the stacked silicon ribbons that form its channel, Module 11) about 5 nm thick, and **vias** (the vertical metal plugs that join one wiring level to the next, Module 12) around 15 nm in diameter that must land on lines aligned to the layer below within about 2 nm. A single 30 nm particle landing before **metal-1** patterning (the lowest wiring layer) can short two lines and kill the **die** (one chip on the wafer) of a $30,000-class data-center GPU. By the time the wafer reaches electrical test at the end of the line, the mistake is three months old and has been repeated on every wafer that followed.

Think of process control as asking three different questions about a newly built road: is it the right width, is there anything unexpected on it, and what is the suspicious object we found? The fab uses three corresponding classes of tool:

- **Metrology** measures a parameter: how thick is this film, how wide is this line, how far is this layer shifted from the one below. The output is a number with an uncertainty, taken at a handful of predetermined sites (tens to a few hundred per wafer).
- **Inspection** finds defects: is there anything on this wafer that should not be there, or missing that should be? The output is a list of (x, y) coordinates with a rough size and signal strength. Inspection tools cover as much of the wafer as possible, as fast as possible, and do not initially know what they have found.
- **Review** classifies what inspection found: particle, scratch, bridge, missing contact, or a false alarm. A review tool revisits the inspector's coordinates, takes a high-resolution image, and a human or an algorithm labels it.

Behind all three sits **yield engineering**: the statistics that translate defect counts and parameter distributions into the fraction of dies that will work, and the detective work of tracing a loss back to the tool, chamber, and step that caused it. Process control is, by **capex** (capital spending on equipment), about one dollar in eight of a modern fab's tool budget, and it is the difference between a node that makes money and one that does not.

By the end of this module you will be able to explain why an ellipsometer needs a model before it can report a thickness; why a defect inspector never looks for defects but compares a die with its twin; why a 20 nm defect is invisible to light and visible to an electron beam, and why the electron beam is nevertheless too slow to replace light; how a defect density and a die area together fix the yield of a chip before it is designed; and why an NVIDIA GPU with a dozen dead cores is a perfectly good product.

## Before you start

- **Wafer, die, lot, FOUP, process flow** (Module 05). A 300 mm wafer carries hundreds of dies; 25 wafers travel together as a **lot** in a sealed **FOUP** (a plastic carrier box) through 1,000 to 1,500 steps over roughly three months.
- **Layers and pitch** (Modules 07, 12). A chip is a stack of patterned layers. **Pitch** is the repeat distance of a row of lines (one line plus one space); the tightest layers of a 2 nm-class node have pitches of roughly 25 to 45 nm.
- **Lithography vocabulary** (Module 07). **Resist** is the light-sensitive film that takes the pattern; **dose** is the light energy delivered to it, in millijoules per square centimetre (mJ/cm²); **focus** is how sharply the image lands; the **scanner** is the exposure machine; the **reticle** (or mask) carries one layer's master pattern and is printed field by field, each **field** about 26 × 33 mm; **OPC** (optical proximity correction) pre-distorts the mask pattern so the printed result comes out right.
- **EUV stochastics** (Module 08). Each 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV, so a small feature is exposed by only thousands of photons and their random arrival can, by itself, leave a contact hole unopened.
- **Etch, deposition and CMP terms** (Modules 06, 09, 12). **Endpoint** is the signal that an etch has broken through; **over-etch** is the deliberate extra time after it; a **chamber** is the vacuum vessel in which one step runs and a **showerhead** the perforated plate that distributes gas over the wafer; **PVD, CVD and ALD** are the three deposition families; **CMP** (chemical mechanical planarization) polishes the wafer flat, with **dishing** (bowls polished into soft wide areas) and **edge exclusion** (the rim it cannot polish well) as its known weaknesses.
- **The transistor and its knobs** (Module 11). **Gate length** is the length of the channel the gate controls; **threshold voltage** is the gate voltage at which the transistor turns on; **on-current** sets speed and **off-current** (leakage) sets standby power. **FinFET** and **nanosheet** (gate-all-around) are the two modern transistor shapes.
- **Interconnect** (Module 12). Metal levels are numbered M1 upward; vias join them; copper is laid into trenches by **dual damascene** with a thin **barrier** and **liner** film that stop it diffusing; the insulator between wires is a **low-k dielectric**; the **scribe lines** are the streets between dies that are cut away at dicing. The **front end of line (FEOL)** is the transistor-making half of the flow, the **middle of line (MOL)** the contacts, and the **back end of line (BEOL)** the wiring.
- **Light as a wave** (school physics). Light has a **wavelength**; a material's **refractive index** says how much it slows light; **polarization** is the orientation in which the wave oscillates; **interference** is waves adding when in step and cancelling when out of step; **diffraction** is waves spreading out from edges and gratings.
- **Statistics**. The **mean** is the average; the **standard deviation σ** is the typical spread around it; the **3σ** convention quotes three standard deviations, within which 99.7% of a normal (bell-curve) distribution lies. The **Poisson distribution** describes counts of rare, independent random events; this module re-derives what it needs.
- **Fab software** (Module 05). The **MES** (manufacturing execution system) records every wafer's route through every tool and chamber; tools report to it over the **SECS/GEM** protocol; **monitor wafers** are blank wafers run through a tool purely to be measured.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. The accompanying review identifies remaining evidence gaps.

## 1. Film Metrology: Measuring Thickness Without Touching

Start with why a fab cares about film thickness to a hundredth of a nanometre. The gate of a 2 nm-class transistor is a stack of films: about 1 nm of silicon oxide that forms on the silicon, then about 2 nm of hafnium oxide (the **high-k** gate insulator, a material that stores more charge per volt than silicon oxide, Module 11), then metal. The threshold voltage of the transistor depends on the thickness of those films, and its gate leakage depends on them exponentially: a 0.1 nm error in the oxide, a third of one atomic layer, shifts the threshold voltage measurably and can change leakage by tens of percent. Elsewhere in the flow, a low-k dielectric a few percent too thin raises wire capacitance, a barrier film too thin lets copper escape, and a resist film too thick shifts the printed line width.

There are hundreds of deposited films on a finished wafer, and each must be checked within seconds, without touching the wafer (anything that touches it adds particles or scratches), on a spot small enough to fit in the scribe line. That combination of tolerance, speed and non-contact is why the film-metrology workhorse is an optical instrument, and why its physics is worth understanding.

### Spectroscopic ellipsometry

> **Intuition: soap-film colours.** A soap bubble or an oil slick on a wet road shows bands of colour because light reflecting from the top surface of the film interferes with light reflecting from the bottom surface. Whether the two reflections add or cancel depends on the film's thickness compared with the wavelength, so each thickness has its own colour. Your eye reads that colour to perhaps 50 nm of thickness. An ellipsometer reads the same interference far more precisely, at many wavelengths at once, and adds a second handle that the eye lacks: it tracks how the reflection changes the light's polarization.

Some definitions first. **Polarization** is the orientation in which a light wave's electric field oscillates. When light hits a surface at a slant, the **plane of incidence** is the plane that contains the incoming and the reflected rays. Light polarized in that plane is called **p-polarized**; light polarized perpendicular to it is **s-polarized**. A surface reflects the two differently, and the difference is largest near the **Brewster angle** (about 75° from vertical for silicon), the angle at which p-polarized light is barely reflected at all. The **phase** of a wave is where in its cycle it is when it arrives; two waves of the same wavelength that arrive a fraction of a cycle apart are "out of phase," and their phase difference decides whether they add or cancel.

The workhorse for transparent and semi-transparent films is **spectroscopic ellipsometry (SE)**. Light reflecting off a surface at an oblique angle (65 to 75°, near silicon's Brewster angle) reflects with a different strength and a different phase for p and s. Each polarization has a **reflection coefficient**, r<sub>p</sub> and r<sub>s</sub>, that records both how much is reflected and how much its phase shifts. The instrument is comparing two versions of the same reflected light. One number describes their relative strengths; another describes how far their oscillations are out of step. You do not need complex-number algebra to follow the method: those two measured changes will be compared with predictions for a candidate film stack. The ratio is written compactly as:

ρ = r<sub>p</sub> / r<sub>s</sub> = tan Ψ · e<sup>iΔ</sup>

Here ρ (rho) is the ratio of the two reflection coefficients; **Ψ** (psi) is an angle whose tangent is the ratio of the reflected p and s amplitudes, so it records how much more the p-wave is dimmed than the s-wave; **Δ** (delta) is the difference between the phase shifts the two polarizations suffer, so it records how much their timing differs; and e<sup>iΔ</sup> is the mathematician's compact way of writing "a phase shift of Δ" (i is the imaginary unit, and the reader loses nothing by reading e<sup>iΔ</sup> simply as "shifted in phase by Δ"). What it means: the instrument reports two numbers, an amplitude ratio and a phase difference, at each wavelength, and both are changed by whatever films lie on the surface. For a thin oxide on silicon, adding ~1 nm of thickness changes Δ by roughly a few tenths of a degree (an order-of-magnitude figure; the exact sensitivity depends on wavelength and angle), and the tool resolves Δ to hundredths of a degree, which is where the sub-atomic repeatability below comes from.

A film stack changes Ψ and Δ because light reflecting from the top surface interferes with light from each buried interface, and the interference depends on thickness, wavelength, and two optical constants of each material: the **refractive index n**, which says how much the material slows light (1.46 for silicon oxide, about 3.9 for silicon at red light), and the **extinction coefficient k**, which says how strongly it absorbs (zero for glass, large for metals). "Spectroscopic" means the measurement is repeated across roughly 190 to 1,000 nm on production tools, with some extending into the vacuum ultraviolet below 150 nm (for thin high-k films) or the infrared to 2,500 nm (for thick or rough films). More wavelengths mean more independent equations to pin down more unknowns.

> **Intuition: precision, accuracy and TMU on a dartboard.** Throw ten darts. **Precision** (also called **repeatability**) is how tightly the ten cluster together, whatever they cluster around; the industry quotes it as 3σ, three standard deviations of repeated readings, so "0.01 nm 3σ" means 99.7% of repeat measurements of the same site fall within ±0.01 nm of their own average. **Accuracy** is how close the centre of the cluster is to the bullseye, the true value; it is set by the model and by reference standards, not by how steady the thrower's hand is. A tight cluster in the wrong place is precise and inaccurate, which is exactly what a wrong optical model produces. **Total measurement uncertainty (TMU)** combines both, plus **tool-to-tool matching** (whether a second tool of the same type, or the same tool next month, would put its cluster in the same place). Every metrology number in this module is quoted in this vocabulary.

The instrument is a broadband source (a xenon arc lamp or a laser-driven plasma), a polarizer that sets a known input polarization, the sample, a **rotating compensator** (a spinning crystal plate that cycles the reflected light through every polarization state tens of times a second, so the detector sees a wobbling signal whose shape encodes Ψ and Δ), an analyzer (a second polarizer), and a spectrograph that splits the light by wavelength onto a detector array. The tool extracts Ψ and Δ at every wavelength from the shape of that wobble in a fraction of a second. The spot is 25 to 50 µm across, larger than any device feature, which is why every film metrology site on a product wafer is a dedicated blank pad in the **scribe line**, the 60 to 80 µm streets between dies, cut away when the wafer is diced, where test pads and metrology targets live.

The key subtlety: the ellipsometer does not measure thickness. It measures Ψ and Δ, and thickness comes from **model fitting**. The engineer builds an optical model of the stack (substrate, interfacial oxide, film, roughness layer, ambient) and gives each layer a formula for how its n and k vary with wavelength, a **dispersion model** (there are standard ones for glassy insulators, for absorbing films, and for rough or porous layers that are treated as a mixture of material and air). The software then applies the **Fresnel equations**, the textbook formulas for how much light of each polarization reflects at each interface, to compute the Ψ and Δ spectra the model would produce, compares them with the measured spectra, and adjusts the free parameters (thicknesses, sometimes n and k) until the difference is as small as it can get. Thickness is the value of a fitted parameter, not a reading on a scale. Metrology recipes take weeks to develop and are validated against cross-section **TEM** (transmission electron microscopy, a microscope that images a sliced sample atom by atom; section 2).

> **What can go wrong.** *The model is wrong and the tool does not know it.* Suppose the recipe assumes a 0.8 nm interfacial silicon-oxide layer under the hafnium oxide but a process change has grown it to 1.1 nm. The fit still converges, the repeatability is still a beautiful 0.01 nm, and the reported hafnium-oxide thickness is simply wrong by a few tenths of a nanometre, because the software has assigned the extra optical path to the wrong layer. Nothing on the tool flags this. It is caught two ways: by the periodic TEM cross-check that every critical recipe carries, and, later and more expensively, by drift in the electrical test data of section 7, where threshold voltages move while the film metrology insists nothing has changed. The fix is a recipe revision with the interfacial layer freed or re-fixed at its new value, and every affected lot's thickness re-computed from its stored spectra.

The performance is extraordinary: for a thin thermal oxide, repeatability is on the order of 0.01 nm 3σ, far smaller than an atom; it is a statistical fit over many photons, not a physical resolution. Accuracy is limited by the model and reference standards to a few tenths of a nanometre. Tools: KLA's SpectraFilm and Aleris, Onto Innovation's Atlas, Nova's PRISM and i-series (Nova began with metrology integrated inside CMP tools). J.A. Woollam supplies research-grade instruments and much of the modelling expertise.

### XRR, XRF, four-point probe, FTIR

These four techniques are side notes, treated at lower depth: each fills a gap ellipsometry leaves, which struggles with metals (too absorbing for light to return from the bottom interface) and cannot separate thickness from density.

**X-ray reflectometry (XRR)** works because X-rays have a refractive index just below 1 inside matter, the opposite of light in glass. Because n is slightly less than 1, going from air into the film is for an X-ray what going from glass into air is for visible light: below a grazing critical angle the wave cannot enter the denser-looking medium and is totally reflected, the same total internal reflection you know from a prism, with the roles of the two media swapped. So at very shallow angles, below a **critical angle** of 0.2 to 0.4° from the surface, the beam reflects totally, like a stone skipping off a lake; above it, the X-rays enter the film and reflect from every interface, and those reflections interfere exactly as in ellipsometry but at an X-ray wavelength of 0.154 nm (the **copper K-alpha line**, the characteristic X-ray a copper target emits when bombarded with electrons). Sweeping the angle from 0 to about 5° produces a fringe pattern (**Kiessig fringes**) whose period gives thickness and whose decay gives roughness. Because the critical angle is proportional to the square root of the **electron density** (the number of electrons per unit volume, which is what X-rays actually scatter from and which rises and falls with the mass density), XRR reads density directly and so separates it from thickness.

> **Worked example: thickness from an XRR fringe period.** For a single film the fringes repeat in angle by about Δθ ≈ λ/(2t), so t ≈ λ/(2Δθ), with λ the X-ray wavelength and t the film thickness. Suppose the fringes are 0.20° apart (illustrative). Converting to radians, 0.20° = 0.20 × π/180 ≈ 0.0035 rad, so t ≈ 0.154 nm / (2 × 0.0035) ≈ 22 nm. A thicker film gives closer fringes; a 2 nm barrier gives fringes ~2° apart, which is why XRR handles very thin metals well.

XRR works on metals, high-k stacks, the ~2 nm tantalum nitride/tantalum **barrier** and cobalt **liner** that stop copper diffusing out of an interconnect line (Module 12), and the ~1 nm interfacial silicon-oxide layer that forms beneath a hafnium oxide gate (Module 11). It is slow (seconds to minutes per site), so it is used for qualification and sampling. **X-ray fluorescence (XRF)** uses a second effect: an atom hit by X-rays re-emits X-rays at energies unique to its element (its **characteristic lines**), so counting them gives composition (the germanium fraction in a silicon-germanium source/drain) and mass per unit area. Bruker (the former Jordan Valley line), Rigaku, and Malvern Panalytical supply combined XRR/XRF/XRD tools (XRD, X-ray diffraction, reads crystal structure).

The **four-point probe** is the oldest tool in the fab. It measures **sheet resistance**, the resistance of a square of a thin film measured between two opposite edges. The size of the square does not matter (a 1 cm square and a 1 mm square of the same film read the same, because a bigger square is both longer and wider by the same factor), so sheet resistance characterizes the film rather than the geometry; its unit is written "ohms per square". Four collinear tungsten-carbide tips at about 1 mm spacing press onto the surface; current flows through the outer pair, and voltage is read across the inner pair. Because the voltage tips carry almost no current, the resistance where the tips touch the wafer adds nothing to the reading, which is the whole point of using four tips instead of two. For a large thin sheet the geometry gives R<sub>s</sub> = (π / ln 2) · (V / I) = 4.532 · V / I ohms per square. It is a contact method, so it runs on **monitor wafers** (blank wafers run through a tool purely to be measured): the dose of an ion **implant** after the **anneal** that activates it, metal deposition, and **silicide** formation (the metal-silicon compound that makes a low-resistance contact; all three in Module 10). KLA's RS series (from Tencor) leads; non-contact **eddy-current** versions (which induce a circulating current in the film with a coil and sense its resistance without touching) exist for product wafers.

**Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)** measures mid-infrared absorption from the vibrations of chemical bonds; each bond absorbs at its own frequency, quoted as a **wavenumber** in cm⁻¹ (waves per centimetre; 2,150 cm⁻¹ is light of wavelength ~4.65 µm). It measures hydrogen in **PECVD** silicon nitride (plasma-deposited nitride, Module 06; Si-H at 2,150 cm⁻¹ and N-H at 3,350 cm⁻¹, which control the film's stress and its tendency to trap charge), boron and phosphorus in **BPSG** (boron- and phosphorus-doped glass used as a filler), **interstitial oxygen** in Czochralski silicon (oxygen atoms trapped between silicon atoms during crystal growth, Module 02; 1,107 cm⁻¹), and porosity in low-k dielectrics (Module 12). It is fast and needs only a calibration curve.

> **Why it matters downstream.** A 0.1 nm error in the gate oxide, invisible to any inspection tool, shifts threshold voltage; a threshold-voltage shift changes the on-current of every transistor on the wafer; that changes the frequency of the ring oscillators measured in section 7; and that frequency decides which **speed bin** (the speed grade a die is sold as, decided by sorting tested dies by their maximum clock frequency; section 10) the finished dies fall into at test (Module 14) and therefore what they sell for. The straight line from a hundredth-of-a-nanometre film measurement to the price of a chip is the reason a fab spends millions of dollars on ellipsometers.

## 2. CD Metrology: Measuring the Width of a Line

**Critical dimension (CD)** is the width of a patterned feature: gate, fin, metal line, contact hole. It matters because the gate length sets the transistor's speed and its leakage: a gate 1 nm shorter switches faster but leaks more, and a gate 1 nm longer does the opposite, so a 1 nm drift in CD across a lot moves the whole lot from one speed bin to another (section 10). Holding CD to a few percent of a 20 nm feature means measuring it to a fraction of a nanometre, and doing so thousands of times a day on features that are smaller than the wavelength of any light that can be focused. Three technologies compete.

### CD-SEM

The **critical-dimension scanning electron microscope (CD-SEM)** is the reference tool. A **field-emission gun** (a sharp tungsten tip that emits electrons when a strong electric field is applied to it) produces a beam focused to about 1 to 2 nm and **rastered** (swept line by line, like an old television) across the feature. The **landing energy**, the energy with which each electron hits the wafer, is measured in **electron-volts (eV)**, the energy an electron gains crossing one volt, so a 500 eV beam is one accelerated through 500 V. It is deliberately low, 300 to 800 eV, so the beam neither penetrates far nor damages the resist. Primary electrons scattering in the top few nanometres eject **secondary electrons** (slow electrons, below 50 eV, knocked out of the sample's atoms) that are collected above the sample.

> **Intuition: why edges glow.** The beam does not stop at the surface; it excites a teardrop-shaped volume of material a few nanometres across, the **interaction volume**. On a flat top surface most of the secondary electrons generated in that teardrop are buried and never escape. On a sloped sidewall the surface cuts through the teardrop, so far more of the electrons made inside it are close enough to the surface to get out, exactly as a hillside catches more sun than a valley floor. The result is that a line appears as a dark plateau bordered by two bright edge peaks, and the CD is the distance between those peaks.

Turning that image into a number is the **edge-detection algorithm**, and its choices determine the answer. The tool averages the signal along the line into a profile, then applies a criterion: a threshold (edge where the signal crosses, say, 50 percent between baseline and peak), maximum slope, or a model-based library that compares the profile against **Monte Carlo** simulations (computer simulations that follow thousands of individual electrons through random scattering events) of lines of known geometry. Threshold is fast and robust but depends on the setting and the sidewall angle; model-based methods are more accurate but need a calibrated physics model. Either way, CD-SEM results are offset-calibrated against cross-section TEM.

Three physical problems plague CD-SEM at advanced nodes. **Charging**: on resist and oxide, which are insulators, the beam deposits charge that deflects subsequent electrons and shifts the apparent edge; tools mitigate with **pre-charge scans** (a fast, low-dose pass over the site that brings the surface to a steady charge state before the measuring scan, so the measurement is not taken while the charge is still building), **energy filtering** (accepting only secondary electrons within a narrow energy band, so that a shift in the surface potential, which shifts the whole energy distribution of the emitted electrons, does not change the collected signal), and landing energies near the point where secondary emission balances incoming current (below about 1 keV each incoming electron knocks out roughly one secondary, so the net charge left behind is close to zero). **Resist shrinkage**: **chemically amplified resists** (the standard resists for ArF, the 193 nm argon-fluoride laser of **deep-ultraviolet (DUV)** lithography, and for EUV, in which one photon triggers a chain of chemical reactions, Module 07) lose volume under the beam because the beam breaks polymer chains and the small fragments evaporate, so a 30 nm resist line may shrink 1 to 3 nm during the first measurement; fabs characterize the shrinkage curve and extrapolate to zero dose, or measure after etch. And the **precision-throughput trade**: more averaged frames means better precision but more shrinkage, more charging, and fewer sites per hour.

> **What can go wrong.** *Shrinkage read as a process drift.* A new resist batch shrinks faster under the beam; every measurement comes in 0.5 nm narrow, the control chart (section 8) alarms, and the lithography engineer trims dose to widen lines that were never narrow. *Charging that moves an edge.* On a thick oxide, charge builds during the scan and the second edge is imaged in a slightly different field from the first, so the line reads wide by a few tenths of a nanometre in one direction only. Both are caught by the same discipline: a dose-extrapolation check (measure the same site at two or three beam doses and extrapolate to zero), a post-etch measurement of the same feature (etched silicon does not shrink), and the standing TEM-derived offset for the recipe. The fix is a recipe change (lower dose, fewer frames, a different landing energy) and a re-characterized shrinkage curve.

Production CD-SEMs reach about 0.2 nm 3σ precision on an etched feature and measure on the order of a thousand sites per hour: vendors quote ~50 to ~100 wafers per hour when each wafer is measured at a handful of sites, and proportionally fewer wafers when more sites are needed. Hitachi High-Tech dominates (the CG series, currently the CG7300 generation introduced in late 2019, as of 2025) with well over half the market; Applied Materials' VeritySEM is the main alternative (KLA's eCD line was discontinued in the 2000s). Because the image is 2D top-down, a CD-SEM cannot easily see sidewall angle, **footing** (a flare at the base of a line), or a buried profile.

> **Worked example: is 0.2 nm precision good enough?** The conventional gauge rule is that a measurement tool's 6σ spread should consume no more than about 10% of the specification window (the **precision-to-tolerance ratio**). A CD-SEM with 0.2 nm 3σ precision has σ = 0.2/3 ≈ 0.067 nm, so 6σ ≈ 0.4 nm. Against a 20 nm gate with a ±2 nm specification (a 4 nm window) that is 0.4/4 = 10%, right at the ceiling: the tool is just good enough. A tool with 0.5 nm 3σ would have 6σ = 1.0 nm and eat 25% of the window, so a quarter of the tolerance would be spent on measurement noise before the process had varied at all. This is why CD metrology precision has had to fall faster than feature sizes, and why the specification for the tool is written in the same units as the specification for the transistor.

### Scatterometry / OCD

> **Intuition: guess the comb from its reflection.** Hold a fine comb under a lamp and the light it throws back depends on how tall, how wide and how sloped its teeth are. You cannot see the individual teeth, but if you know the physics of light well enough to compute the reflection for any guessed tooth shape, you can keep guessing shapes until the computed reflection matches what you see, and the shape that matches is your measurement. That is scatterometry.

**Optical critical dimension (OCD) metrology**, or **scatterometry**, does not image the feature at all. It measures the **specular** reflection (the mirror-direction reflection) from a periodic grating of the feature (a 30 × 30 to 50 × 50 µm target in the scribe line, or increasingly an in-die periodic region) as a function of wavelength and polarization, exactly like an ellipsometer, and solves an **inverse problem**: working backwards from the effect (the spectrum) to the cause (the shape). The forward model is **rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA)**, which is simply the standard computer method for calculating exactly how a periodic structure reflects light of a given wavelength and polarization, for a given geometry: line width, height, sidewall angle, corner rounding, footing, underlayer thicknesses. A library of simulated spectra, or a real-time regression, finds the geometry whose spectrum matches.

Why a plain mirror reflection carries shape information needs a word of explanation. A grating throws light into a few sharp directions (the diffraction orders, treated properly in section 3), but for a grating whose pitch is smaller than the wavelength only the mirror-direction beam (the zeroth order) escapes at all; the sideways orders cannot propagate and die out within a wavelength of the surface. That one returning beam is not a simple mirror image, though: its strength and polarization at each wavelength are set by how the light rattled around inside the grating's teeth before leaving, so its spectrum depends on the teeth's width, height, slope and what lies beneath them. "Diffracted spectrum" below means that shape-dependent spectrum of the single returning beam.

The diffracted spectrum encodes the full 3D profile. One measurement returns fin height, fin width at three heights, and the recess depth of the oxide between the fins (after the fins are cut, the gaps between them are filled with oxide and the oxide is then etched back, or **recessed**, to expose the upper part of each fin; how far it is etched sets the fin's active height, so that depth is a first-order transistor parameter), with sub-nanometre precision, in under a second per site, with no charging, shrinkage, or vacuum. On a nanosheet transistor, built by growing alternating layers of silicon and silicon-germanium and later removing the silicon-germanium to leave three suspended silicon ribbons (Module 11), OCD is the only in-line tool that can report the thickness of each of the three stacked silicon channels and the SiGe layers between them. The catches: it needs a periodic target, and the inverse problem can be **ill-posed**, meaning several different causes give nearly the same effect, so the answer is ambiguous.

> **What can go wrong: parameter correlation.** A thicker underlying film and a slightly narrower line can produce almost the same reflection spectrum, so the fit can trade one against the other and report a line 0.5 nm narrower while the film has actually grown 0.5 nm thicker. The recipe developer detects this at recipe build (the fit's own statistics show the two parameters moving together) and cures it by fixing some parameters, adding physical constraints, or bringing in the value of one parameter from another tool: the film thickness from XRR, or the top CD from a CD-SEM, fed into the OCD model as a fixed or tightly constrained input. That is **hybrid metrology**: combining data from several tools in one fit. A nanosheet stack has too many parameters (three channel thicknesses, three **inner spacers**, the insulating pads between the sheets that separate each channel's gate from its source and drain, widths, sidewall angles, underlayers) for OCD alone to resolve unambiguously, and constraining even one of them from an independent measurement breaks the correlations and improves every remaining parameter. The value moves to the software platform; KLA, Nova, and Onto all sell hybrid frameworks.

Machine-learning models trained on measured spectra against reference data now handle the hardest structures. Nova (the OCD pioneer), KLA (SpectraShape), and Onto (Atlas) are the suppliers; Nova leads stand-alone OCD, KLA leads overall.

### CD-AFM and TEM

As a calibration side tool, **CD-AFM** (atomic force microscopy with a flared "boot" tip that reaches under overhanging sidewalls; Bruker's Dimension X3D) provides non-destructive sidewall measurements with **traceable** calibration, meaning its calibration runs in an unbroken chain back to a national standards laboratory, so two fabs' numbers agree. It is used mainly to calibrate CD-SEM and OCD. Scanning one surface with a physical tip is slower than collecting a whole optical image. Tip wear changes the apparent sidewall shape and creates a measurement bias, so a reference structure is checked before unknown samples and the tip is replaced or recalibrated when that reference drifts. A universal precision or wafers-per-hour figure would be misleading without the tip, structure and scan recipe.

**TEM and STEM** (transmission and scanning transmission electron microscopy, which pass a high-energy electron beam through a very thin sample) are the ground truth. A **focused ion beam (FIB)** tool (gallium or xenon plasma sources; Thermo Fisher Helios and Hitachi lead) carves a slice 50 to 100 nm thin from a chosen location, a **lamella**, thin enough that electrons pass straight through it; a needle-fine **micromanipulator** lifts it out and welds it to a support grid. A TEM (Thermo Fisher Spectra, Hitachi HF5000, JEOL) images it at atomic resolution, with **energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX or EDS)** mapping composition: the beam's electrons knock X-rays out of the sample's atoms, and each element emits X-rays at its own energy, so the spectrum says which elements are where. This is how the industry knows a 3 nm nanosheet is really 3.1 nm with a 0.6 nm interfacial oxide. It is destructive, takes hours per sample, and costs hundreds to thousands of dollars per cross-section, so it calibrates the in-line tools, debugs excursions, and supports **failure analysis (FA)** on dies that failed test. A large fab runs dozens of FIB/TEM systems around the clock.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Every CD-SEM and OCD recipe in the fab carries an offset derived from a handful of TEM cross-sections. If those lamellae were cut from an unrepresentative site (the wafer edge, a lot with an unusual film stack), the offset is wrong and every in-line CD number on the node inherits the error; the process will be centred on a false target and the transistors will be systematically fast or slow. That is why reference lamellae are cut from several sites on several lots, why the offset is re-verified after any process change, and why the metrology group treats its TEM samples like calibration weights.

## 3. Overlay: Aligning One Layer to the Next

**Overlay** is the positional error between a patterned layer and the layer beneath it. If metal-2 is shifted 3 nm relative to via-1, the vias partially miss the lines, resistance rises, and eventually the connection opens. It is worth separating two things a reader will otherwise conflate. **Alignment** happens during exposure: before printing each field the scanner looks at alignment marks printed in an earlier layer and positions the wafer so the new pattern lands on them (Module 07). **Overlay metrology** happens afterwards: it checks how well that alignment, plus everything alignment could not correct, actually worked. The "everything else" is long: distortion in the scanner's projection lens, drift of its wafer **stage** (the moving table that carries the wafer under the lens), errors in the **reticle** (the mask, the master pattern for one layer), warping of the wafer by **stressed films** (deposited layers that pull or push on the silicon, bending it by microns), and how the wafer was held flat on the **chuck** (the vacuum or electrostatic plate that grips it during exposure).

The **overlay budget** at a leading-edge node is roughly 20 to 25 percent of the minimum pitch of the most critical layers; for TSMC N2 and Intel 18A the total on-product tolerance for the tightest layer pairs is on the order of 2 nm 3σ, spread across the scanner's stage and lens, the reticle, wafer distortion from stressed films and chucking, and the metrology itself. The rule of thumb is that metrology uncertainty should consume no more than about 10 percent of the budget, so overlay tools need a **total measurement uncertainty (TMU)**, the dartboard combination of precision, accuracy and tool-to-tool matching from section 1, near 0.1 to 0.2 nm.

> **Worked example: an overlay budget as a root-sum-square.** Independent error sources do not add directly; they add as the square root of the sum of their squares, because errors of random sign partly cancel. Take an illustrative budget for one critical layer pair: scanner (stage plus lens) ~1.2 nm, wafer distortion ~1.0 nm, reticle ~0.6 nm, metrology ~0.3 nm, all 3σ. Total ≈ √(1.2² + 1.0² + 0.6² + 0.3²) = √(1.44 + 1.00 + 0.36 + 0.09) = √2.89 ≈ 1.7 nm, inside a 2 nm budget. Two lessons. Each contributor gets its own line and its own owner (the scanner vendor, the film group, the mask shop, the metrology group). And the metrology term, 0.3 nm, adds only 0.03 nm to the total because it enters squared; had it been 1.0 nm it would have pushed the total to 2.0 nm and used up the whole budget on its own. That is why metrology must be far smaller than the errors it measures, and why 0.1 to 0.2 nm TMU is a requirement rather than a luxury.

### Targets: box-in-box, AIM, and diffraction gratings

The classic **box-in-box** target is a large square (about 20 µm) in the previous layer and a smaller square in the current layer, imaged optically; the overlay error is the offset between centres. It is sensitive to asymmetry in how each box's edges image. A slightly asymmetric lens shifts every image a little (the **tool-induced shift**); if the two layers' marks have the same pitch and are imaged the same way, the shift is the same for both and cancels in the difference. KLA's **AIM (Advanced Imaging Metrology)** target exploits that by replacing the boxes with periodic gratings in the two layers arranged so that each is measured with the same optical response, allowing sub-nanometre precision. These **image-based overlay (IBO)** tools, principally KLA's Archer series (Archer 750 and 800 generations), use broadband visible/near-infrared light at multiple wavelengths, because some films (amorphous carbon hard masks, thick nitrides) are opaque to visible light but transparent in the near-infrared, so the tool can pick a wavelength that reaches the buried mark of the earlier layer.

**Diffraction-based overlay (DBO)** uses a different physical handle. A grating does not reflect light in one direction; it throws it into a few sharp directions called **diffraction orders**, numbered 0 (straight back), +1 and −1 (the first to each side), and so on. DBO stacks two gratings, one per layer, and reads the +1 and −1 orders.

> **Intuition: symmetry.** When the top grating sits exactly over the bottom one, the stack looks the same viewed from the left as from the right, so the +1 and −1 beams are equal. Any shift breaks that symmetry, one order gets brighter and the other dimmer, and for small shifts the imbalance grows in proportion to the shift. The sign of the imbalance tells you which way the layer moved, and the size tells you how far.

The catch is that the proportionality constant depends on the film stack and the wavelength and is not known in advance. DBO removes it with two targets side by side, printed with a deliberate programmed shift of +d in one and −d in its neighbour, so the constant can be cancelled algebraically. The measurement takes tens of milliseconds, the targets are 10 to 16 µm, and averaging over the whole grating gives repeatability near 0.05 to 0.1 nm.

> **Worked example: the two-bias trick.** Call the measured asymmetry (the +1 minus −1 intensity, divided by their sum) A, the unknown overlay ov, and the unknown sensitivity K. The target with the +d bias reads A₊ = K · (ov + d); the target with the −d bias reads A₋ = K · (ov − d). Adding and subtracting: A₊ + A₋ = 2K · ov and A₊ − A₋ = 2K · d, so ov = d · (A₊ + A₋) / (A₊ − A₋), and K has vanished. With d = 20 nm, A₊ = +0.030 and A₋ = −0.020 (illustrative numbers; note that A₋ is negative, so the sum in the numerator and the difference in the denominator below look inverted but are not): ov = 20 × (0.030 + (−0.020)) / (0.030 − (−0.020)) = 20 × 0.010 / 0.050 = 4 nm. The stack's optical response never had to be known, which is why DBO recipes are quick to set up and robust to film changes.

ASML's YieldStar family (380/385, and the newer 1375/1385) leads DBO, with KLA's Archer offering a DBO mode. ASML's advantage is integration: YieldStar data feeds directly back to ASML's NXT (DUV) and NXE (EUV) scanners.

> **Intuition: image-based overlay.** Imagine two combs, one formed in each layer. The tool locates each repeated pattern from many edges rather than trusting one edge of one box, then subtracts their positions. Averaging suppresses random image noise, while a matched optical response can cancel a common image shift. It cannot cancel a deformation that changes only the lower-layer mark; that is why a repeatable target measurement still needs correlation to the device.

### On-product overlay and the metrology-to-device offset

The targets are not the devices. They sit in the scribe line, are far larger than device features, and print under different lithographic and etch conditions than a 20 nm via. So the overlay the target reports can differ from the overlay the transistors experience by a nanometre or more, a **metrology-to-device (MTD) offset** that is often the largest single term in the budget.

> **What can go wrong: the target lies.** Three mechanisms make a target report an overlay the devices do not have. *Asymmetric etch of the target grating*: if the etch leans the grating's sidewalls one way, its apparent centre moves. *Asymmetric films on top*: a deposited film that is thicker on one side of each grating line shifts the reflected light's apparent centre. *Lens aberrations that act differently on coarse and fine pitches*: light from a 500 nm-pitch target and from 40 nm-pitch device lines passes through different parts of the projection lens, so the same lens error displaces them by different amounts. The offset is detected by measuring the devices themselves: an e-beam overlay tool or CD-SEM images real vias sitting on real lines after etch, and the difference between that and the optical target's answer is the MTD offset. The fixes are shrinking targets and moving them in-die so they print more like devices; measuring at several wavelengths and choosing the one least sensitive to asymmetry; and calibrating each optical recipe against the e-beam reference.

The other half of the answer is **on-product overlay (OPO)** control. The scanner can bend the placement of every field with a smooth **correction map**: a shift, a magnification change, a rotation, and higher-order warps, each described by a coefficient; ASML's correction-per-exposure adjusts dozens of such terms per field. Fitting a map with many terms needs many measured points, so OPO relies on dense sampling of several hundred sites on a few wafers per lot, which is exactly why the tens-of-milliseconds speed of DBO matters.

> **Why it matters downstream.** A 15 nm via that lands 3 nm off its line loses roughly a fifth of its contact area (3 nm is a fifth of its diameter; the exact fraction depends on the shapes of via and line). Its resistance rises by a similar fraction, the current through the remaining area is denser, and denser current is what drives **electromigration**, the slow drift of metal atoms under current flow that eventually voids the via (Module 12). The part passes test on day one and fails in year three. Overlay is therefore a reliability specification as much as a yield one, which is why the via layers carry the tightest overlay budgets on the wafer.

## 4. Defect Inspection: Finding Something You Do Not Know Is There

Metrology knows what it is looking for. Inspection does not: a defect can be a particle, a scratch, a missing hole, a bridge, a blister, a stain, and the tool has no description of it in advance. That is the central problem of inspection, and it has one elegant solution.

> **Intuition: spot the difference.** Two pictures side by side, identical except for one detail; you find the detail in seconds without anyone telling you what it is, because you are not looking for a thing, you are looking for a difference. A wafer carries hundreds of dies that are supposed to be identical, so every die has a twin next to it. Subtract the twin from the die and a perfect wafer gives a blank image; anything left over is a candidate defect. No description of the defect is needed, only a twin.

### Bright-field optical inspection

**Bright-field (BF) inspection** images the wafer under near-normal illumination and compares each location to a reference: the same location in the adjacent die (**die-to-die**), the equivalent cell in a memory array (**cell-to-cell**, which allows higher sensitivity because the pattern is perfectly periodic: the reference cell is a few hundred nanometres away instead of a whole die away, so the two images share the same film thickness and the same focus, and the pattern noise described in the next paragraph nearly vanishes from the subtraction), or a rendering of the design (**die-to-database**, used for masks). The difference image is thresholded; anything surviving is a defect candidate with location, pixel size, and signal strength.

The difference image is never truly blank, and the reasons set the sensitivity. **Shot noise**: photons arrive at random, so a pixel that collects N photons fluctuates by about √N from frame to frame; a brighter source raises N and the signal-to-noise ratio grows as √N, which is why the light source matters so much. **Pattern noise**: the two dies are never perfectly identical; their line edges are rough (**line-edge roughness**, the nanometre-scale wobble of every printed edge) and their films differ slightly in thickness, which shifts the interference colour, so the subtraction leaves a faint texture that a small defect must stand out from.

The flagship tools are KLA's 29xx/39xx series (the 2965 and 3935 are the current foundry/logic flagships, successors to the 2935 and 2950/3900 generations of 2017 to 2020, as of 2025). Their source is a **laser-sustained broadband plasma**: a continuous-wave laser focused into a xenon bulb sustains a plasma of tens of thousands of kelvin emitting from about 190 nm into the visible, many times brighter than an arc lamp. The tools offer many optical modes (wavelength band, polarization, **aperture**, the opening that selects which angles of light are used, and focus offset), and the recipe engineer picks the mode that maximizes defect signal against background for each layer. **Time-delay-integration (TDI)** sensors, cameras that shift their accumulated charge along in step with the moving wafer so they can integrate light without blurring, read out hundreds of megapixels per second as the stage scans continuously.

Sensitivity is around 20 nm on a smooth layer and worse on noisy layers. A microscope cannot resolve features much smaller than about half the wavelength of its light (~100 nm at 200 nm light; the **diffraction limit**), so a 20 nm particle is not seen as a shape but as a slight dimming or brightening of a whole pixel; the tool detects the perturbation the defect makes to the reflected field, which is why sensitivity depends so heavily on the surrounding pattern. Pixel sizes are 30 to 100 nm; smaller pixels mean higher sensitivity and lower throughput, roughly as the inverse square: halving the pixel from 100 to 50 nm quadruples the number of pixels and the scan time, from a few minutes per wafer to ten or more, which is the trade the recipe engineer makes between sensitivity and wafers per hour. At highest sensitivity a 300 mm wafer takes tens of minutes; at a relaxed pixel, a few.

Much of what survives the threshold is **nuisance**: a real signal that is harmless (a colour variation, a rough patch, a slight film-thickness step), as opposed to a **false** count from pure noise; section 5 deals with sorting them out. Design-aware inspection (KLA's NanoPoint) uses the layout to define **care areas** where a defect would matter, raising sensitivity there and lowering it elsewhere to cut the nuisance rate.

### Dark-field laser scattering

> **Intuition: dust in a sunbeam.** In a dark room a shaft of sunlight shows every floating speck, although the same specks are invisible in a lit room. You see them because they scatter light sideways into your eye while the clean air around them sends nothing your way. Dark-field inspection lights the wafer from the side and looks only for light scattered off its path.

**Dark-field (DF) inspection** illuminates the wafer with an oblique laser and collects only light scattered away from the specular (mirror) direction. A perfect surface scatters almost nothing, so a particle, pit, or scratch is a bright point on a dark background. For a particle much smaller than the wavelength the scattered intensity follows Rayleigh scattering:

I ∝ d⁶ / λ⁴

with d the particle diameter and λ the wavelength. Halving the particle diameter cuts the signal 64-fold; halving the wavelength raises it 16-fold. Both facts follow from the exponents, and together they explain why the tools use deep-ultraviolet lasers (around 266 nm) and why sensitivity falls off a cliff below a certain particle size.

**KLA Surfscan** (SP7 and SP7XP) is the standard for **bare and blanket wafers** (unpatterned wafers, or wafers covered by a single uniform film): incoming wafer qualification, monitor wafers run through a chamber to check its particle performance, post-CMP and post-clean surfaces. Sensitivity is below 20 nm **PSL-equivalent** at above 100 wafers per hour; calibrated polystyrene-latex spheres of known diameter are the reference, so "20 nm PSL" means "scatters as much light as a 20 nm polystyrene sphere," not that the tool measured a size. Every process chamber is qualified by counting the **adders**, the particles it added, measured by scanning a bare wafer before and after a pass through it; a chamber exceeding its spec (a typical spec allows only a single-digit number of added particles larger than 30 nm per pass) is taken down.

For **patterned wafers**, the pattern itself scatters. A regular pattern scatters like a grating, into a few sharp directions; a random defect scatters everywhere. KLA's Puma series (9850, 9980) combines oblique and normal illumination with multiple collection channels and a **Fourier filter**, a mask placed where the scattered light comes to a focus, with opaque spots exactly at the pattern's few directions: it blocks the pattern's scatter and passes the defect's. Patterned DF is faster and cheaper than BF and is used where the killer defects are particles rather than pattern defects. Hitachi High-Tech competes in both segments.

> **What can go wrong: patterned dark-field.** The Fourier filter can suppress a defect if its scattering overlaps the directions blocked to remove the regular pattern. Changing pattern pitch or orientation can also let background light through and flood the defect list. A fab qualifies the inspection recipe using known defect types and review images, not a universal minimum defect size. Particle-rich process layers may suit this fast screen, while small pattern bridges may need bright-field or e-beam contrast.

### E-beam inspection

Optical tools cannot see the defects that matter most at the front end of a 2 nm-class process: a missing 15 nm contact, a 5 nm bridge between fins, a void inside a via. **E-beam inspection (EBI)** sees them because a 2 nm probe resolves them directly, and because the secondary-electron signal is sensitive to charging.

> **Intuition: voltage contrast.** Rub a balloon on your hair and it holds its charge because nothing conducts it away. A contact that is properly connected to the substrate drains the charge the beam deposits; a contact that is open, floating with no path to ground, charges up like the balloon. A charged surface repels or attracts the secondary electrons leaving it, so it images brighter or darker than its neighbours. The **voltage-contrast** signal therefore reveals an electrical open that is buried and invisible from the surface, before any wire has been built on top of it.

EBI can therefore find buried electrical opens and shorts before the interconnect is built.

The machine is a CD-SEM column (section 2) re-tuned for coverage rather than for the finest possible probe. The landing energy is chosen per layer: a few hundred eV on resist after develop, where the beam must not shrink or damage the pattern, and ~1 to 3 keV after etch, where deliberate charging is what builds the voltage contrast (a higher energy deposits charge faster and drives floating features further from their grounded neighbours). The beam current is in the nanoampere range, orders of magnitude above the picoamperes a CD-SEM uses, because the task is different: a CD-SEM needs a 1 to 2 nm probe to place an edge to 0.2 nm, while an inspector needs enough secondary electrons per pixel to decide "same or different" at a coarser pixel of ~5 to 30 nm, set by the size of the defect being hunted. The wafer moves continuously under the column while the beam deflectors track it, the electron-beam equivalent of the TDI sensor in bright-field, and the image is subtracted die-to-die or cell-to-cell exactly as in the optical case.

What limits the pixel rate is electron counting. At 10 nA and 400 million pixels per second each pixel gets 2.5 ns of beam, so 10⁻⁸ C/s × 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ s ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁷ C, about 156 electrons (illustrative). With roughly one secondary electron emitted per incoming electron and only a fraction of those collected, perhaps ~100 are detected, and their shot noise, √100 = 10, is a 10 percent fluctuation per pixel, about the most a subtraction can tolerate before the difference image fills with false counts. Raising the current to buy more electrons per pixel does not work for free: the electrons in the beam repel one another as they travel down the column, and above some current the probe blurs. That current-against-resolution trade is the hard limit on a single column, and it is why multi-beam tools split the current across many separate probes instead of pushing one harder. The problem, in short, is speed.

> **Worked example: why single-beam EBI cannot cover a wafer.** A 300 mm wafer has an area of π × 150² mm² ≈ 7.1 × 10⁴ mm² = 7.1 × 10¹⁶ nm². At a 5 nm pixel (25 nm² per pixel) that is 7.1 × 10¹⁶ / 25 ≈ 2.8 × 10¹⁵ pixels. Even at a 400 MHz pixel rate with no overhead, 2.8 × 10¹⁵ / (4 × 10⁸ per second) ≈ 7.1 × 10⁶ seconds, about 81 days for one wafer. The beam current needed for adequate signal-to-noise limits real pixel rates to a fraction of that, so full-wafer single-beam inspection is off by a factor of a thousand or more from anything useful, and single-beam tools inspect well under 1% of the wafer.

Single-beam tools (KLA eSL10, Applied PROVision, ASML HMI eP5) inspect a fraction of a percent of the wafer on selected care areas, for **hot spots** (layout locations known from simulation or experience to print marginally) and characterization rather than lot monitoring.

**Multi-beam inspection** attacks the serialization directly. ASML's HMI eScan 1000, shipped in 2020, splits one source into a 3 × 3 array of nine beams with separate detector channels, for up to about 6× single-beam throughput. The eScan 1100, first installed in 2022, uses a 5 × 5 array of 25 beams for up to 15×, with a column designed to minimize crosstalk between beams. ASML's roadmap extends to hundreds of beams; KLA and Applied have their own multi-beam efforts. As of 2025 multi-beam EBI remains a sampling tool for the most critical layers: the difference between inspecting 0.1 percent of a wafer and a few percent, not 100 percent.

### The sampling problem

A fab at 100,000 wafer starts per month with 1,200 steps performs about 1.4 billion wafer-steps a year. Inspecting every wafer after every step is impossible: an inspector costs ~$10 million to ~$40 million (for comparison, an EUV scanner is ~$200 million or more, Module 08) and takes minutes per wafer. So inspection is a **sampling** strategy. A fab defines perhaps 30 to 60 inspection points (post-litho, post-etch, post-CMP, post-clean at each critical layer) and at each inspects one to three wafers of the 25 in a lot, on some fraction of lots. The goal is not to find every defect on every wafer but to detect a **process shift** (a chamber that started adding particles, a resist batch that started collapsing) within hours, before many lots pass through.

> **Worked example: how many lots are exposed before the alarm?** A tool runs 30 lots a day, 1.25 lots per hour. The inspection point after it samples one lot in four, and the cycle from inspection through review to a decision takes ~6 hours (illustrative). When the tool starts adding particles, on average ~2 lots pass before the next sampled lot arrives; ~7.5 more lots run during the six-hour cycle; and a couple sit in the inspector's queue. Roughly a dozen lots, ~300 wafers, ~$4.5 million of work in progress at $15,000 a wafer, have been exposed to the fault before anyone knows. Halving the cycle time to 3 hours removes ~4 lots from the exposure; doubling the sampling to one lot in two removes only ~1, because in this case the cycle time dominates. That arithmetic is why fabs push review turnaround as hard as sampling rates, and why inline classification (section 5) that shortens the cycle is worth more than a more sensitive inspector.

Defect counts from each point go into **statistical process control (SPC)** charts (control charts, section 8) by defect type, with control limits from the historical distribution. A point above the upper limit triggers an alarm, the lot is held, and the engineer reviews. Adaptive sampling raises the rate when a tool is suspect and lowers it when stable, and the newest systems use **FDC** data (the process tools' own sensor traces, section 8) to predict which wafers are worth inspecting.

> **What can go wrong.** *Nuisance explosion after a film-stack change.* A recipe tuned for one film thickness sees a new interference colour when the stack changes and floods the review queue with thousands of nuisance counts per wafer; the recipe engineer sees it as a jump in the nuisance ratio at review and re-tunes the optical mode and the care areas. *Haze on dark-field.* A slightly rough or grainy film scatters weakly everywhere, and the tool reports a carpet of false particles; detected because the "particles" have no size distribution and vanish when the collection channel is changed, and fixed by a different polarization or channel. *E-beam damage.* The inspection beam itself can charge or shrink sensitive resist and thin oxides, so that inspected dies later behave differently from uninspected ones; fabs limit e-beam inspection on such layers to sacrificial sites and check with a split of inspected against uninspected dies at test.

Who makes it: KLA (bright-field, dark-field, and single-beam e-beam), Hitachi High-Tech (dark-field and e-beam), Applied Materials (e-beam), and ASML's HMI division (single- and multi-beam e-beam).

## 5. Defect Review and Classification

An inspector's output is a **defect map** of coordinates. A significant fraction, sometimes the majority, are nuisance (real but harmless) or false (noise). Sorting real from nuisance and identifying the real ones is review.

The **defect review SEM (DR-SEM)** drives to each coordinate (the inspector's positional accuracy of a few hundred nanometres must place the defect inside the SEM's field of view), takes a high-resolution secondary-electron image, often tilted or stereoscopic (tilting the beam or the stage, or combining two views from different angles, shows the defect's height and whether it sits on top of, inside, or under the pattern, which a top-down image cannot tell, and that alone often separates a particle that landed after etch from a defect that was etched into the film), and can add an EDX spectrum (section 2) to identify a particle's composition. Composition is a fingerprint of the source: aluminium points to a chamber liner or wall, stainless steel to a robot arm or a valve, tin to the EUV source (whose plasma is made from tin droplets, Module 08), and carbon-rich residue to resist. Applied Materials' SEMVision line is generally credited with the largest share of e-beam review (the current SEMVision H20, introduced in February 2025, uses a cold-field-emission source, a sharper electron emitter run at room temperature, for faster and higher-resolution imaging, as of 2025); KLA's eDR7380 and successors are strong where they are paired with KLA's inspectors, sharing coordinates and recipes; Hitachi's RS series competes. A review SEM handles a few hundred to a thousand defects per hour, so only a sample of the map is reviewed.

**Automatic defect classification (ADC)** assigns each image to a class and marks nuisance. Early ADC used hand-engineered features (size, aspect ratio, contrast); since about 2017 convolutional neural networks trained on labelled fab images have become standard, exceeding 90 percent accuracy on established classes, with humans labelling new classes as they appear. The inspector itself increasingly runs a first-stage classifier on the optical difference image (inline ADC) to suppress nuisance before review, where the biggest leverage lies.

> **Worked example: what inline ADC buys.** An inspector reports 5,000 candidates on a wafer, of which 80 percent are nuisance (illustrative; raw nuisance fractions of half to well over three-quarters are ordinary on a noisy layer). The review SEM handles ~500 defects an hour, so reviewing everything would take ten hours per wafer; in practice the recipe samples 300 candidates, which takes about 36 minutes and contains only 60 real defects, too few to say anything reliable about a class that makes up a tenth of them. Now let inline ADC remove three-quarters of the nuisance before review. The map shrinks to 5,000 × (0.20 + 0.80 × 0.25) = 2,000 candidates, half of them real; the same 300-image review sample now contains 150 real defects, the review time spent on nuisance falls from 80 to 50 percent, and, because the review sample is the slow step in the six-hour cycle of the sampling example in section 4, a smaller and cleaner map is what shortens the cycle. That, not a more sensitive inspector, is the lever the sampling arithmetic pointed at.

The human loop that keeps the classifier honest is small but permanent. Any image the network scores with low confidence is routed to a person instead of being auto-labelled; a random sample of the images it called nuisance (a few percent) is re-reviewed every shift so that a silent mislabel shows up as a disagreement rate rather than as a yield loss; and when a genuinely new class appears, a defect engineer labels a few dozen to a few hundred examples of it and the network is retrained on them, a matter of days rather than the months a hand-engineered rule set once took. A large fab staffs this with a handful of defect engineers per shift for the whole line, which is the reason inline ADC pays: without it the same people would be looking at nuisance.

> **What can go wrong.** *ADC confidently mislabels a new class.* A classifier trained on last year's defects assigns a never-seen residue to "nuisance" with high confidence, and a real yield loss is filtered out before any human sees it; detected when sort yield (section 6) falls while the defect charts stay flat, and cured by a human relabelling loop with periodic audits in which a random sample of "nuisance" images is re-reviewed by a person. *Coordinate error.* If the inspector's stage or the review tool's alignment is off by more than the SEM's field of view, the review SEM images a clean patch next to the defect and reports "not found"; the recipe engineer watches the not-found rate and re-calibrates the two tools against a wafer with known defects. *EDX blind to thin residue.* A nanometre-thin film of residue produces too little X-ray signal to identify, so composition comes back as the substrate; the fallback is a FIB cross-section or surface-analysis techniques on a sacrificial wafer.

### Killer vs nuisance, and the catalogue of defect types

A **killer defect** causes an electrical failure; a **nuisance defect** does not. A 30 nm particle on a metal-1 layer with 30 nm spaces is a probable short; the same particle on a 500 nm-pitch pad layer is harmless. The **kill ratio** for a defect class and layer is the fraction of dies containing such a defect that fail because of it. It is estimated by overlaying defect maps on **sort maps** (the maps of which dies passed or failed electrical test at wafer sort, Module 14), and it converts inspection data into yield predictions.

> **Worked example: kill ratio from a map overlay.** Overlay the inspection map from a metal-1 layer on the sort map of the same wafers. Dies that carried a class-X defect (say a bridge candidate) failed 70% of the time; dies with no class-X defect failed 30% of the time, the baseline from every other cause. The extra failure attributable to class X, as a fraction of the dies that would otherwise have passed, is KR ≈ (0.70 − 0.30) / (1 − 0.30) = 0.40 / 0.70 ≈ 0.57 (illustrative). So a class-X defect kills its die a little more than half the time, and a lot that shows 0.2 class-X defects per die can expect roughly 0.2 × 0.57 ≈ 11% extra yield loss from that class alone.

The main defect families on a logic process:

- **Particles**: flakes from chamber walls, wear debris from moving parts, resist fragments, etch residue, people. Still the largest category and the reason every chamber gets periodic cleans.
- **Scratches**: from CMP (a hard slurry agglomerate dragged across the surface) or wafer handling.
- **Bridges and opens**: from lithography (defocus, dose error, **scumming**, which is resist residue left in the bottom of a trench, and stochastics), etch (incomplete etch leaving a bridge, over-etch cutting a line), or CMP (**dishing**, the polishing of a wide soft copper area into a bowl, leaving residual metal between lines).
- **Missing or merged contacts and vias**: at EUV layers the dominant failure is **stochastic**, random rather than systematic, arising from the small number of photons and the randomness of resist chemistry (worked example below).
- **Voids** in copper fill (a later **electromigration** failure, the drift of metal atoms under current that hollows out a line, Module 12) or in dielectric gap fill.
- **Residue**: incompletely stripped resist, etch polymer, slurry, **watermarks** (the ring of dissolved material a drop of rinse water leaves behind when it dries on the surface instead of being spun or blown off).
- **Pattern collapse**: tall, narrow resist lines or fins pulled over by **capillary forces**, the surface tension of the rinse water tugging neighbouring lines together as it dries.
- **Crystal defects**: **stacking faults** (a mistake in the crystal's atomic stacking order) in **epitaxial** silicon-germanium (grown as a continuation of the underlying crystal, Module 11), and **slip lines** (planes of atoms that have slid past each other under thermal stress).

> **Worked example: EUV photon counting and the yield it costs.** A dose of 30 mJ/cm² is 0.030 J/cm² = 3 × 10⁻¹⁶ J/nm². One 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV = 92 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 1.47 × 10⁻¹⁷ J. Dividing gives ~20 photons per nm². A 20 nm contact hole has an area of π × 10² ≈ 314 nm², so it receives ~6,000 incident photons, of which the resist absorbs only a fraction (Module 08 works through the absorbed count). Random fluctuations in that count, and in the chemistry each photon triggers, occasionally leave a contact unopened or merge two neighbours. Now the arithmetic that turns a rare event into a yield problem: a large die carries 10¹⁰ to 10¹¹ contacts. If each fails with probability 10⁻¹¹, the expected number of failures per die on that layer is 10¹⁰ × 10⁻¹¹ = 0.1, and the chance that a die has none is exp(−0.1) ≈ 90% (the Poisson formula of section 9). One layer, at a failure rate of one in a hundred billion, costs 10 points of yield. That is why the mature-layer target is below 10⁻¹² per feature (section 12).

> **Why it matters downstream.** Kill ratio × defect count per die is the yield forecast for a held lot. When an excursion (section 8) puts 25 wafers on hold, the yield engineer overlays the inspection results with the kill ratios for each class and predicts how many dies will survive; that number, against the wafer's cost and the customer's delivery date, is what decides whether the lot ships, is reworked, or is scrapped. A wrong kill ratio scraps good wafers or ships bad ones.

## 6. Reading a Wafer Map

Defects and failures are never uniformly distributed, and the spatial pattern is often the fastest route to the cause. Yield engineers read **wafer maps** (of defects, parametric values, or sort bins) the way radiologists read X-rays:

| Signature on the map | Likely cause | What that is |
|---|---|---|
| Ring of failures at the wafer edge | Edge-bead removal set wrong, plating or deposition roll-off, CMP edge exclusion, PVD clamp-ring shadow, focus loss at the edge | Edge-bead removal is the solvent rinse that strips resist from the wafer's rim; plating is **electroplating**, the copper fill of the interconnect trenches from a liquid bath under an electric current (Module 12), and roll-off is a film thinning toward the edge, where the plating current or the deposition flux is not uniform; the clamp ring holds the wafer in a sputter tool and shadows its rim; CMP polishes the rim less well; the scanner loses focus where the wafer curls |
| Arc-shaped scratch, sometimes concentric | CMP: a hard particle in the slurry or a conditioner diamond dragged across the rotating wafer | The conditioner is a diamond-studded disc that roughens the polishing pad; a loose diamond becomes a cutting tool |
| Radial streaks or spiral from centre | Spin processes (coat, develop, spin-rinse-dry); a pre-existing particle leaves a comet tail downstream | Liquid flung outward over a spinning wafer flows around any obstacle and leaves a wake |
| Concentrated spot at centre | Showerhead centre, chuck centre, nozzle drip in a wet tool | The showerhead's central holes, the chuck's central lift pins, or a dispense nozzle parked over the centre |
| One quadrant or half-wafer | One faulty station of a multi-station tool (chamber, spin cup, CMP head) | Tools with several stations process different wafers, or different parts of one wafer, on different hardware |
| Same defect in every exposure field | Reticle or **pellicle** defect; repeats at the field pitch | The pellicle is the thin membrane stretched over the mask to keep particles out of focus; a defect on either prints in every field |
| Every nth field, or a checkerboard | A stage or focus error that recurs at fixed positions in the scanner's exposure sequence | The scanner prints the wafer's fields in a fixed serpentine order, and to save time it scans consecutive fields in alternating directions. An error tied to one point in that order (the stage settling badly just after it reverses direction, say), or to the scan direction (an error that appears only on up-scans hits every second field, a checkerboard), or a flatness defect at one spot on the wafer table, lands on the same field positions on every wafer, so the failures sit on a lattice while their neighbours are fine |
| Random, no structure | The true random baseline (D0, section 9) | Particles and stochastic failures with no common cause |
| Side-to-side gradient | **Hotplate** or chuck temperature gradient, gas-flow or plating-current asymmetry | The hotplate is the resist bake plate; a tilt of a degree across it shifts CD from one side to the other |

The map alone rarely names the tool; **commonality analysis** does. The MES knows which chamber processed every wafer at every step. When a set of lots shows a loss, software ranks every (step, tool, chamber) combination by how well "went through it" separates failing from passing lots. A scratching CMP head is found in minutes because the failing lots all share head #3 on polisher CMP-07 at metal-2 and nothing else.

> **Worked example: a miniature commonality table.** Six lots, three chambers (A, B, C) at one etch step, with each lot's yield:
>
> | Lot | Chamber at etch step | Yield |
> |---|---|---|
> | 1 | A | 91% |
> | 2 | B | 62% |
> | 3 | C | 90% |
> | 4 | B | 58% |
> | 5 | A | 92% |
> | 6 | B | 65% |
>
> Chamber B's lots average 62%; A and C average 91%. The score for this (step, chamber) combination is the separation of the two averages relative to the scatter within each group (here about 29 points against a scatter of a few points, a very strong signal); a combination whose groups overlap scores near zero. The software computes such a score for every one of the thousands of (step, tool, chamber) combinations the six lots passed through, ranks them, and prints chamber B at the top within minutes. The engineer then pulls chamber B's FDC traces (section 8) for the days in question and finds, say, a drifting pressure reading.

> **What can go wrong.** *Confounded routes.* If the fab's **dispatching** (the MES logic that decides which tool and chamber each waiting lot goes to next) always sends lots that used etch chamber B on to CMP head 3, the two are indistinguishable in the data: both score identically and the analysis cannot say which is guilty. *Small samples.* With six lots, some innocent combination somewhere among thousands will separate the groups by chance and score high; the more combinations tested, the more false culprits appear. *Reversal by a hidden variable.* A chamber can look bad overall while being the best chamber for each product individually, if it happened to run the harder product more often (the statisticians' Simpson's paradox). In every case the engineer confirms before acting with a deliberate **split lot**: one lot's 25 wafers are divided between the suspect and a known-good chamber with everything else identical, and the yield difference at test is the verdict.

Who makes it: the yield-management platforms are PDF Solutions' Exensio, Onto Innovation's Discover, and Synopsys' Silicon.da, alongside in-house systems at TSMC, Samsung and Intel that hold the largest datasets. Machine-learning classifiers now read map signatures automatically (the vendors do not publish accuracy figures), so an edge ring or a scratch is labelled and routed to the responsible module before an engineer opens the map.

## 7. In-Line Electrical Test: PCM and WAT

The transistors that matter, the billions in each die, are buried under 15 or more layers of wiring and have no exposed terminals; nobody can put a probe on one. And a finished die cannot be functionally tested until every layer is complete, roughly three months after the first transistor was made (Module 05). A fab therefore prints copies of its transistors and wires, connected to large metal **pads** that a needle can touch, in the scribe line, and measures them as soon as the first metal layer exists.

That timing is the point. Testing at first metal gives an electrical readout of the transistors after ~6 weeks instead of ~14 (the exact weeks depend on the flow), so a shift in threshold voltage is caught while the lots that share it are still in the fab, rather than months later at sort when every lot behind them carries the same shift. This is **wafer acceptance test (WAT)** in TSMC's vocabulary, **process control monitoring (PCM)** elsewhere, or "e-test."

A **parametric tester** (Keysight 4080 series, Keithley S500) with a **probe card** (a board carrying fine needles that touch the pads; Module 14) contacts 25 to 50 pads at 5 to 17 sites per wafer and runs **DC** (steady-voltage) and low-frequency measurements in seconds per site. The structures, and the terms they need:

- **Transistor I-V** (current against voltage): **threshold voltage** (the gate voltage at which the transistor turns on), **on-current** and **off-current** (the drain current when fully on and when nominally off), **subthreshold slope** (how many millivolts of gate voltage it takes to change the off-current tenfold; smaller is a sharper switch), and **DIBL (drain-induced barrier lowering)**, how much the drain voltage lowers the threshold, a short-channel weakness, for every **device flavour** (the variants of the transistor a process offers: n-type and p-type; low-, standard- and high-threshold-voltage versions that trade speed against leakage; thick-oxide versions for higher-voltage input/output circuits). The most direct check of the entire FEOL.
- **Resistances**: sheet resistance via **van der Pauw** structures (a cloverleaf shape whose sheet resistance can be computed from two resistance readings between its lobes); contact and via resistance via **Kelvin** or **four-terminal** structures (separate current and voltage leads, so contact resistance is excluded, the same trick as the four-point probe of section 1); and **via chains** of hundreds of thousands to millions of vias in series (one open via reads as an open chain, so the chain is a yield monitor for opens at that layer).
- **Serpentine and comb structures**: a long minimum-pitch **serpentine** (a snaking line; a break anywhere reads as an open) interleaved with a **comb** (parallel fingers; any bridge to the serpentine reads as a short) over a significant area; failures give a direct estimate of defect density at that layer.
- **Capacitances**: gate capacitance, expressed as **effective oxide thickness (EOT)**, the thickness of pure silicon oxide that would give the same capacitance; interconnect capacitance (low-k and line profile).
- **Ring oscillators**: loops of **inverters** (an inverter is the simplest logic gate, one n-type and one p-type transistor whose output is the opposite of its input; a loop of an odd number of them can never settle, because each signal comes back around inverted, so it oscillates, and the faster the transistors switch, the higher the frequency). The frequency is a compact measure of gate delay, and it is correlated with the transistor metrics above to build the process-corner model (the fast and slow extremes of the transistor model, defined below).
- **Leakage and breakdown**: gate, junction, dielectric.

> **Worked example: from WAT failures to defect rates.** A via chain of 10⁶ vias reads open at 5% of the sites on a lot. If via opens are independent, the chance that a chain survives is exp(−N · p) with N vias and p the per-via open probability, so p = −ln(0.95) / 10⁶ ≈ 0.051 / 10⁶ ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁸ per via. A comb/serpentine structure covering 0.01 cm² of a metal layer fails (short or open) at 3 of 100 sites: the layer's defect density is D = −ln(0.97) / 0.01 ≈ 0.030 / 0.01 ≈ 3 defects/cm² at that layer (both illustrative). Neither number is what a product die sees, because a product die's wiring is only partly at minimum pitch, but each is a per-layer input to the defect-density model of section 9, and tracking them lot by lot is how a fab knows which layer's D0 is moving.

WAT runs at first metal (early feedback) and at end of line, often at intermediate layers too. Every parameter has a spec window; a wafer outside it is scrapped regardless of how it later tests, because out-of-spec transistors cannot be trusted for reliability. The WAT database, spanning millions of wafers, underlies the foundry's **SPICE model** (the circuit-simulator description of the transistor that chip designers use to predict their circuits' behaviour) and its **process corners** (the fast and slow extremes of that model, which the foundry guarantees the silicon will fall between), its yield model, and its ability to say "this lot's ring oscillators are 3 percent slow because gate CD ran 0.4 nm wide, traced to etch chamber B."

> **What can go wrong.** *Probe scrub.* Each touchdown drags the needle tips across the aluminium pads; after many touchdowns on a re-probed wafer, or with a worn card, the tips punch through the pad into the dielectric and read open. *Thermal drift.* Threshold voltage moves by about a couple of millivolts per degree, so a chuck that has not settled reads a shifted lot. *Edge-site bias.* Sites at the wafer edge sit in the region of every process's worst uniformity, and a recipe that averages all sites equally lets a few edge sites drag the wafer mean out of spec. *Tester recipe error.* A wrong limit file can scrap a lot of good wafers in a minute. All four are caught by the same habits: correlation across sites (a real process shift moves all sites together; a probe problem hits one), a re-probe of a suspect wafer on a second tester, and a hold on any scrap decision until an engineer has looked at the raw curves.

Who makes it: Keysight and Keithley (part of Tektronix) supply the parametric testers; the probe cards come from the same suppliers as production sort cards, led by FormFactor (Module 14).

> **Why it matters downstream.** WAT is the hinge between the fab and the design world. Its transistor curves, aggregated over millions of wafers, become the SPICE model and its corners, and the corners become the design margins every chip on the node is built with (Module 19): a foundry whose corners are wide forces designers to leave speed on the table. And the ring-oscillator frequency measured here predicts which speed bin each wafer's dies will land in at sort (Module 14), weeks before they get there.

## 8. SPC, APC, FDC, and the Excursion

### Statistical process control

Every measurement in a fab has noise in it. The question a control chart answers is whether the latest reading is noise or a real shift, and the cost of getting it wrong runs both ways: react to noise and you adjust a process that was fine, adding variation; ignore a real shift and you ship it. **Statistical process control (SPC)** is the discipline of drawing that line from the process's own history.

> **Intuition: the process's voice and the customer's demand.** A control chart has two kinds of limit that beginners confuse. **Control limits** are the process's own voice: where it naturally wanders when nothing is wrong, computed from its history. **Specification limits** are the customer's demand: the range the product must fall in to work. They have nothing to do with each other. A process can be perfectly in control (wandering only where it always wanders) and still out of spec, if its natural spread is wider than the customer allows; and a process can be inside spec but out of control, if it has just shifted to somewhere it has never been, which is a warning that something has changed even though nothing has failed yet.

Every metrology and WAT parameter feeds a **control chart**. The **Shewhart chart** plots each lot's mean and its range (largest minus smallest reading) against time, with control limits at ±3σ of the historical distribution. Why 3σ: a stable normal process crosses ±3σ by chance only about 0.3% of the time, so an alarm at that level is almost always real, at the price of missing small shifts. To catch those, the chart also applies the **Western Electric rules**: one point beyond 3σ, two of three beyond 2σ on one side, four of five beyond 1σ, or eight consecutive points on one side of centre; each of these patterns is as unlikely by chance as a single 3σ point, but each catches a sustained small shift that never crosses 3σ.

**Process capability** relates the process spread to the specification:

C<sub>pk</sub> = min(USL − µ, µ − LSL) / (3σ)

where USL and LSL are the upper and lower specification limits, µ is the process mean and σ its standard deviation. It asks how many "3σ units" fit between the mean and the nearer spec limit. A C<sub>pk</sub> of 1.33 means the nearer limit is 4σ from the mean, so about 60 parts per million fall outside if the process stays centred; it is the usual target for critical parameters. For a 20 nm CD with a ±2 nm spec, that means a 3σ CD variation below 1.5 nm, which is why metrology precision must be near 0.2 nm (the 10 percent precision-to-tolerance rule of section 2: the tool's 6σ must stay under 0.4 nm of the 4 nm window, so its 3σ must be near 0.2 nm, and any looser tool would eat into the 1.5 nm the process itself is allowed).

> **Worked example: capability of an etch CD.** Spec 18 to 22 nm; the process runs at µ = 20.2 nm with σ = 0.5 nm. C<sub>pk</sub> = min(22 − 20.2, 20.2 − 18) / (3 × 0.5) = min(1.8, 2.2) / 1.5 = 1.2, which fails the 1.33 target. Two fixes. Centre the process: at µ = 20.0, C<sub>pk</sub> = 2.0 / 1.5 = 1.33, with no change in spread. Or keep the offset and tighten: σ must fall to 1.8 / (3 × 1.33) ≈ 0.45 nm. Centring is a recipe offset and costs nothing; cutting σ by 10% is months of work. That is why APC exists.

### Advanced process control

SPC detects; **advanced process control (APC)** corrects. In **run-to-run (R2R) control**, the metrology result from lot n adjusts the recipe for lot n+1. The usual estimator is an **exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA)** of the process offset:

x̂<sub>n+1</sub> = λ · y<sub>n</sub> + (1 − λ) · x̂<sub>n</sub>

where y<sub>n</sub> is the offset measured on lot n, x̂<sub>n</sub> is the controller's previous estimate of the offset, x̂<sub>n+1</sub> is its new estimate, and λ (lambda, typically 0.2 to 0.4) is how much it trusts a single lot. One step: with λ = 0.3, a previous estimate of 0.4 nm and a new reading of 1.0 nm, the new estimate is 0.3 × 1.0 + 0.7 × 0.4 = 0.58 nm, and the etch time is trimmed by the amount that removes 0.58 nm, not 1.0 nm. The controller believes the trend, not the last data point. Module 05 walks a three-lot sequence of this loop and covers the bookkeeping of the thousands of control threads (tool × chamber × product × layer) that a fab maintains; the same loop runs on the scanner (overlay tool measures lot n, the per-field corrections of section 3 are updated for lot n+1), on CMP (a pre-polish thickness map sets per-wafer polish time), and in **feedforward** form, where a downstream step is adjusted from upstream data, such as tuning etch time to the incoming film thickness.

> **What can go wrong.** *Over-correction.* Set λ near 1 and the controller swings the recipe by the full measured error after every lot, chases metrology noise, and produces a CD chart that oscillates with a variance larger than it had uncorrected. *Correcting on a bad measurement.* A mis-probed site or a shrinkage artefact reads 2 nm off; the controller obediently moves the process 0.6 nm and the next three lots are wrong. Guard rails: outlier rejection (a reading far outside the expected range is flagged for review, not fed to the controller), step-size limits (no single lot may move the recipe more than a set amount), and a maximum-adjustment window beyond which the controller stops and calls an engineer.

### Fault detection and classification

**Fault detection and classification (FDC)** watches the tools rather than the wafers. Every modern tool streams hundreds of sensor traces at 1 to 10 Hz over SECS/GEM or the higher-bandwidth **EDA** (equipment data acquisition) interfaces (Module 05): **RF** (radio-frequency, the oscillating power that sustains a plasma) forward and **reflected power** (the **matching network** is the tuning circuit between the RF generator and the chamber that adjusts itself so the generator sees the electrical load it was designed to drive; when it drifts out of tune, part of the power is reflected back toward the generator instead of entering the plasma, like an echo on a badly terminated cable, and that echo is the reflected-power reading), pressure, gas flows, chuck temperature and **helium backside pressure** (the cooling gas fed under the wafer, which leaks out if the wafer is chipped or a particle holds it off the chuck), **throttle-valve position** (the pump's flow-control valve, which opens wider when something is outgassing), **optical emission spectra** (the light the plasma emits, whose colours track its chemistry), and **endpoint** signals (the change in that light when an etch breaks through to the layer below). FDC software builds a statistical envelope for each trace from known-good runs (a **univariate** envelope, one trace at a time) or a **multivariate** model that compresses hundreds of traces into a few health scores, and alarms on departures: rising reflected power means a matching-network drift, a changed endpoint shape means the wrong incoming thickness, a helium-leak spike means a chipped wafer or a particle under it. FDC catches many faults before any wafer is measured and drives **predictive maintenance** (scheduling a part's replacement from the trend in its own sensor data, such as a slowly rising reflected power or a throttle valve that opens a little further every week, rather than on a fixed calendar or after a failure).

> **Worked example: building a univariate envelope.** Take 30 known-good runs of one etch step and, for each sensor trace, line the runs up in recipe time and compute the mean and σ across the 30 at every sample. For the helium backside pressure the controller holds a set point, say 10.0 Torr with σ = 0.05 Torr across the runs (illustrative), so the ±3σ envelope is 9.85 to 10.15 Torr; the helium flow the controller needs to hold it is the leak rate, normally a trickle. A wafer that is chipped, or held a few microns off the chuck by a particle under it, cannot seal: the pressure sags below its envelope for the whole step while the flow trace climbs many σ above its own as the controller tries to compensate, and, because the helium is the thermal contact between wafer and chuck, the wafer runs hot and the etch rate shifts, which the CD metrology would otherwise see only hours later. Now the subtlety that decides whether FDC is usable at all: a single sample outside ±3σ means nothing. With 300 traces sampled at 10 Hz through a 60-second step, one run produces 300 × 600 = 180,000 comparisons, and a stable process crosses 3σ by chance 0.3 percent of the time, so about 540 samples per run sit outside their envelopes for no reason. FDC therefore alarms on persistence and on summaries, not on samples: a run of consecutive samples outside the envelope, or a per-step statistic (the step's mean, its maximum, the area outside the envelope) compared against that statistic's own limit from the 30 runs. That cuts the comparisons per run from 180,000 to a few thousand and the chance alarms to a handful, which are ranked and reviewed rather than acted on one by one.

> **What can go wrong.** *A legitimate recipe change.* An engineer raises the etch step's pressure set point by 10 percent as an approved change; every trace shifts, hundreds of envelopes are violated on the next run, and a healthy tool is flagged as faulty. The guard is versioning: each model is tied to a recipe version, and a recipe change triggers a model rebuild from the first known-good runs under the new recipe rather than an alarm. *A drifting sensor.* A pressure gauge that reads 2 percent high after a year has changed the number, not the process; an envelope rebuilt from recent runs has followed it and stays quiet, while an old envelope alarms on a chamber that is fine. It is caught by cross-checking against a second gauge or the monitor wafers of the tool's periodic requalification, and fixed by recalibrating the gauge and rebuilding the model. *A fault diluted in a multivariate score.* A model that compresses hundreds of traces into one health score can bury a real departure on one trace under the noise of the other 299; the fix is to keep univariate limits on the dozen traces that physics says matter most (reflected power, helium leak, endpoint time) alongside the summary score.

### The excursion

An **excursion** is a departure from normal behaviour that affects product: a tool adding particles, a chemical batch out of spec, a drifting implanter dose. The reaction is scripted in an **out-of-control action plan (OCAP)**, a written procedure that says who does what within what time. Within minutes of an alarm the tool is put on hold. Lots that passed through it since the last known-good measurement are identified from the MES and held. Engineers pull them to inspection and review and **disposition** them (the formal decision, recorded against each lot): scrap, rework (resist can be stripped and recoated; almost nothing else can be undone), or release with a risk note. The tool is diagnosed, repaired, requalified with monitor wafers and a **partition test** (running monitor wafers through subsets of the tool's chambers or steps to isolate the faulty one), and released.

> **Worked example: the cost of one undetected day.** Assume a tool that runs 30 lots a day, 25 wafers per lot, at $15,000 per N4-class wafer (TSMC's 4 nm-class node), and assume the worst case in which every affected wafer is scrapped: 30 × 25 × $15,000 = $11.25 million, over $11 million for a single day. Real excursions rarely scrap everything (kill ratios below 1, partial rework, harvesting), and wafer cost ranges from $15,000 to $20,000 at this class of node, so the true figure varies by several times in either direction; but the order of magnitude, eight figures per day, is why a good fab's response time is hours and why in-line inspection pays for itself.

Who makes it: Applied Materials' SmartFactory suite and PDF Solutions' Exensio cover SPC, APC and FDC; the largest fabs run in-house systems built on the same ideas.

## 9. Yield Theory

### Three kinds of yield

**Yield** is the fraction of dies that work, and it decomposes:

- **Random defect yield** (Y<sub>random</sub>): losses from randomly distributed defects such as particles and stochastic failures. Scales with die area; described by defect density models.
- **Systematic yield** (Y<sub>sys</sub>): losses hitting every die the same way through a design-process interaction: a layout pattern that prints marginally, a lithography hot spot. Independent of area to first order; fixed by changing the **design rule** (the geometric limits a layout must obey), the OPC, or the process.
- **Parametric yield** (Y<sub>param</sub>): dies whose transistors all exist but whose speed, leakage, or power falls outside the sellable window. **Binning** (section 10), sorting dies into speed grades sold at different prices, converts some of this into lower-priced product.

Y = Y<sub>sys</sub> × Y<sub>param</sub> × Y<sub>random</sub>. Read this as successive survival fractions. Multiplying separately measured fractions requires care if the same dies appear in more than one failure category: use conditional fractions, or an independence assumption, rather than counting the same loss twice. At the start of a node, systematic and parametric losses dominate and are fixed one by one; at maturity, random defects dominate and the work is grinding D0 down.

### Defect density and the Poisson model

This model asks a deliberately narrow question: if a die fails whenever it contains a killer defect, what fraction should contain none? It does not yet include spare memory, disabled compute blocks or speed grading. Those come later. An **expected count** is an average over many dies: a value below one does not mean a fraction of a physical defect sits on each die.

> **Intuition: rain on a paved courtyard.** Rain falls at random on a courtyard of paving stones. If the average is one drop per stone, some stones get two or three and some get none. The chance that a given stone stays completely dry depends only on the average number of drops per stone, and it equals e raised to minus that average: e⁻¹ ≈ 37% for one drop per stone, e⁻² ≈ 14% for two. A bigger stone collects more drops on average and is harder to keep dry. Replace drops with killer defects and stones with dies, and that is the whole of the Poisson yield model.

Suppose killer defects fall at random with average density **D0** (defects per cm²) and a die has area A (cm²). The expected number per die is A · D0. If defects are independent, the count follows the Poisson distribution and the probability of zero defects, the **Poisson yield model**, is:

Y = exp(−A · D0)

Its weakness is pessimism for large dies: real defects cluster (a particle shower, a scratch, an edge ring hit many neighbouring dies and leave others clean), so a large die is somewhat more likely to be clean than Poisson predicts at the same average D0.

### Murphy, Seeds, and the negative binomial

> **Intuition: showery rain.** The same total rainfall can fall as a steady drizzle or as a few heavy showers. Showers soak some stones many times over and leave more stones completely dry than the drizzle would, so for the same average, clustered rain gives more dry stones. Defects on a wafer are showery: they arrive in scratches, particle bursts and edge rings. The classical fix is to say that the defect density is not one number but varies from place to place, and to average the Poisson answer over the range of densities seen across the wafer. The negative-binomial model's parameter α says how showery the defects are.

Early models assumed D0 varies across the wafer with some distribution and averaged the Poisson yield over it (the average of exp(−A · D) over all the local densities D, each weighted by how often it occurs):

- **Murphy's model** (Bell Labs, 1964) used a triangular distribution (densities near the average most common, falling off linearly to zero on either side): Y = [(1 − exp(−A · D0)) / (A · D0)]².
- **Seeds' model** used an exponential distribution (low densities most common, with a long tail of high ones): Y = 1 / (1 + A · D0), the most optimistic classical model.
- The **negative binomial model** (Stapper, IBM) assumes a gamma distribution (a flexible hump whose width is set by one parameter) with a **clustering parameter α**:

Y = (1 + A · D0 / α)<sup>−α</sup>

As α goes to infinity this becomes Poisson (no clustering); smaller α means more clustering and higher large-die yield. Empirical α for modern processes is about 1 to 3, with α near 2 a common default. A multi-layer process has a D0 and α per layer and the yield is the product, but the single-parameter form is what appears in nearly every industry discussion, and D0 is the number that TSMC, Samsung, and Intel plot on symposium slides, usually with an unlabelled y-axis.

> **Worked example: yield vs die area at D0 = 0.1 defects/cm².**
>
> A mature leading-edge node reaches a killer defect density near 0.1/cm². Compare a 100 mm² smartphone processor with an 800 mm² data-center GPU (A in cm², so A·D0 = 0.1 and 0.8 respectively).
>
> | Die area (mm²) | A·D0 | Poisson exp(−A·D0) | Murphy | Seeds 1/(1+A·D0) | Neg. binomial, α = 2 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 50 | 0.05 | 95.1% | 95.1% | 95.2% | 95.2% |
> | 100 | 0.10 | 90.5% | 90.6% | 90.9% | 90.7% |
> | 200 | 0.20 | 81.9% | 82.2% | 83.3% | 82.6% |
> | 400 | 0.40 | 67.0% | 67.9% | 71.4% | 69.4% |
> | 600 | 0.60 | 54.9% | 56.6% | 62.5% | 59.2% |
> | 800 | 0.80 | 44.9% | 47.4% | 55.6% | 51.0% |
>
> Two lessons. The models agree for small dies and diverge for large ones: at 800 mm² the spread between Poisson and Seeds exceeds 10 points, so the choice of model and α matters enormously for a GPU and hardly at all for a phone chip. And the phone die keeps 90 percent of its wafer while the GPU keeps roughly half. Now repeat at D0 = 0.5/cm², typical of early risk production (the first low-volume months of a node): the 100 mm² die yields exp(−0.5) = 61 percent, but the 800 mm² die yields exp(−4) = 1.8 percent under Poisson, or (1 + 2)<sup>−2</sup> = 11 percent under negative binomial with α = 2. Nobody ships an 800 mm² die on a young process; big-die products arrive one to two years after a node's first phone chips, once D0 has fallen.

### Critical area: what D0 actually counts

D0 is not the density of everything an inspector sees. A 30 nm particle can only cause a short where two lines are closer together than 30 nm, and only cause an open where it lands on a line narrower than itself; on a wide power rail or an empty region it does nothing. The fraction of a die's area where a defect of a given size would kill it is that size's **critical area**, and D0 is properly a killer-defect density weighted by the layout: the physical defect density at each layer multiplied by the kill ratio of section 5, summed over layers. This is what ties the module together. The kill ratio measured from map overlays says how often a defect class kills; the comb/serpentine and via-chain structures of section 7 measure the per-layer failure rate directly on structures of known critical area; and the yield model turns the two into a prediction for a product die whose critical area the design tools compute from its layout. A design with more of its area at minimum pitch has a larger critical area and a worse yield at the same physical defect density, which is one of the levers Module 19 will pull.

### Why chiplets help

> **Worked example: four chiplets against one big die.** Splitting an 800 mm² monolithic design into four 200 mm² chiplets raises per-chiplet yield from 45 to 82 percent (Poisson, D0 = 0.1, from the table). All four must be good and be assembled, so the compound yield 0.82⁴ = 45 percent looks like no gain at all. The gain appears with **known-good-die (KGD)** testing: each chiplet is tested before assembly and only good ones are packaged, so the effective silicon yield is 82 percent, and the only compounding left is the assembly yield, typically above 95 percent for a mature CoWoS or hybrid-bonded flow (CoWoS is TSMC's silicon-interposer packaging; hybrid bonding joins dies face to face without solder; Module 17): 0.82 × 0.95 ≈ 78 percent of the silicon ends up in a working product, against 45 percent for the monolithic die.

Only the compute chiplets need the leading-edge node's D0 and wafer cost; I/O and cache can sit on an older, cheaper, higher-yielding node. AMD's MI300 and EPYC, Intel's Meteor Lake, and Apple's Ultra parts exploit this; NVIDIA's Blackwell uses two reticle-sized dies rather than many small ones because its bandwidth requirement favours one wide seam.

### The yield learning curve

D0 is a function of time. When a new process enters **risk production** (the first months of a node, when a few customers' designs run before yield is proven), D0 is typically several tenths per cm² and even small dies yield well below 70 percent. Over the following six to eight quarters the fab drives D0 down through thousands of individual fixes: a chamber-clean recipe, a filter, a design-rule tweak, a better resist, an OPC update for a hot spot. The curve of D0 against time, the **yield learning curve**, is the most closely guarded and most eagerly leaked chart in the industry.

TSMC has shown these curves for N7, N5, N3, and N2 at its symposia, plotted at the same relative stage of development. At the 2025 North American symposium it disclosed that N2's D0, two quarters before high-volume production, was lower than N3, N5, or N7 had been at the equivalent point, despite N2 being its first **gate-all-around** node (the nanosheet transistor, whose gate wraps the channel on all four sides, Module 11); the N2 curve started higher than N5's but fell steeply and tracked N3's. TSMC does not label the absolute axis, but industry estimates place a mature TSMC logic node near or below 0.1/cm² and a node in early risk production around 0.4 to 0.6/cm², so "D0 falls from roughly 0.5 to roughly 0.1 in about two years" is a fair description of the shape, not an official number. For Intel 18A, Intel's then-CEO Pat Gelsinger said at a Deutsche Bank conference in September 2024 that D0 was "below 0.4" and "healthy"; press claims about 18A yield during 2025 (die yield this time, not D0) ranged from single-digit percent to a healthy majority of dies, and were rumours and analyst estimates, while Intel's own statements described 18A as on track for Panther Lake (launched on 18A in early 2026, with some layers later dual-qualified on High-NA EUV, Module 08) and its D0 as in line with previous nodes at the same stage. Treat specific competitor D0 numbers with suspicion; the shape of the curve is well established, the absolute values rarely are.

> **What can go wrong.** *Comparing D0 across companies.* One fab counts every defect an inspector reports, another counts only defects with a kill ratio above a threshold, a third quotes the number that fits its yield model; the same wafer produces three different "D0" values, and a chart comparing them is meaningless without the definitions. *Poisson for a reticle-size die.* At A · D0 near 1 the Poisson model can understate yield by 10 points or more against the clustered reality (the table above), so a business case for a large die built on Poisson is too pessimistic and one built on Seeds too optimistic. *One D0 for a fifteen-layer process.* A single number hides which layer is losing dies; the fix is a per-layer D0 built from the WAT structures of section 7 and per-layer kill ratios, with α calibrated on the fab's own sort maps rather than defaulted to 2.

## 10. Redundancy, Repair, Harvesting, and Binning

Yield models describe dies with zero killer defects. Designers cheat this three ways.

**Redundancy and repair** are universal in memory. A **DRAM** (dynamic random-access memory, the main-memory chip type, Module 15) or **SRAM** (static random-access memory, the fast on-die memory used for caches) array carries spare rows and columns (a few percent extra); at wafer test a failing row is identified and a **fuse**, a tiny metal link that the tester blows with a laser or a current pulse, permanently remaps its address to a spare. A memory die with 20 scattered single-bit failures ships as a perfect part, which is why memory yields exceed 90 percent on dies with defect counts that would kill unrepairable logic. Large caches on logic dies (an H100 has 50 MB of L2 cache, the on-die memory shared by all its cores) use the same technique, plus **error-correcting codes (ECC)**, extra check bits stored alongside the data that let a few wrong bits be detected and corrected on the fly.

**Harvesting** does the same at the level of functional blocks. NVIDIA's GH100 die physically contains 144 streaming multiprocessors (SMs, the GPU's core compute blocks); the H100 **SXM5** product (the high-power module format that plugs into a server baseboard) enables 132 and the H100 **PCIe** product (the standard expansion-card format) enables 114. A die with a killer defect in one SM, or two SMs that fail speed, is still a fully sellable H100; one with more failures becomes a lower-tier product. AMD, Intel, and every GPU and multi-core CPU vendor do this (a 96-core and a 64-core EPYC may be the same dies), and it is why large dies are viable at all.

> **Worked example: good H100 dies per wafer.**
>
> The GH100 die is about 814 mm² (roughly 26 × 31 mm, close to the 26 × 33 mm = 858 mm² **reticle limit**, the largest area one exposure field can cover and therefore the largest monolithic die a scanner can print). A common estimate for **dies per wafer (DPW)** that accounts for partial edge dies is DPW = π · (d/2)² / A − π · d / √(2 · A), with d the wafer diameter and A the die area in the same units. With d = 300 mm and A = 814 mm²: π · 22,500 / 814 = 86.8, minus π · 300 / √1,628 = 942.5 / 40.35 = 23.4, gives about 63. A 3 mm edge exclusion (d = 294 mm) and scribe lines bring it to about 60, matching published estimates.
>
> At mature TSMC **4N** (TSMC's NVIDIA-tuned variant of its 5 nm-class process) take D0 = 0.1/cm² (A = 8.14 cm², A·D0 = 0.814). Poisson yield for a defect-free die is exp(−0.814) = 44 percent, about 27 perfect dies; negative binomial with α = 2 gives (1 + 0.407)<sup>−2</sup> = 50.5 percent, about 31.
>
> Now add harvesting. Suppose ~70 percent of the die area is SMs and other disableable blocks, and ~30 percent (NVLink, NVIDIA's chip-to-chip link; memory controllers; PCIe; and the L2 fabric and crossbar, the on-die network and switch that carry data between every SM and the shared L2 cache and memory controllers, which every SM depends on and which therefore cannot be switched off) is not. A defect in the unrepairable 2.44 cm² is fatal: the survival probability is exp(−0.244) = 78 percent. In the repairable 5.70 cm² the expected defect count is 0.57; the SXM configuration tolerates 12 dead SMs, and the probability of more than 12 Poisson events with mean 0.57 is negligible. Effective yield is therefore about 78 percent, or 47 to 49 sellable dies per wafer instead of 27 to 31. At a 4N wafer cost near $16,000 to $18,000, silicon cost per sellable die is on the order of $330 to $380, trivial next to the module's price and small even next to its **HBM** (the stacks of high-bandwidth memory mounted beside the GPU, Module 15) and **CoWoS** packaging cost (the silicon-interposer packaging that joins GPU and HBM, Module 17). Put the two numbers side by side: ~$350 of GPU silicon in a module that sells for ~$30,000. For a data-center GPU, harvesting roughly doubles effective yield, and wafer yield is not the dominant cost; packaging capacity and HBM are.

**Binning**, whose full test flow is covered in Module 14, treats parametric variation. After test, dies are sorted by maximum frequency and leakage into speed grades sold as different SKUs (stock-keeping units, distinct products with distinct prices). A die 5 percent slower than target becomes the lower-clocked part. A 1 percent improvement in ring-oscillator speed shifts the whole distribution and moves a measurable fraction of dies into the higher-priced bin.

> **What can go wrong.** *Spares exhausted.* A memory array with 2% spare rows cannot absorb a scratch that takes out 3% of them, so a clustered defect that redundancy was meant to hide kills the die anyway; the design response is to spread spares across the array rather than pool them. *Fuse regrowth.* A blown fuse can partially reconnect over years under heat and electric field, silently un-repairing the array; fabs qualify fuse structures with accelerated stress (section 13) and modern designs use one-time-programmable cells with margin. *Harvested-part spread.* A die with two SMs disabled because they failed speed may have other SMs that barely passed, so harvested parts can show a wider spread of power and frequency than full parts, which the binning limits and the product's power specification must absorb.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The fuse and bin decisions described here are executed at wafer sort and final test, where every die's map of good and bad blocks is written into it permanently (Module 14). And the arithmetic above is an input to the design itself: when Module 19 chooses the die size and the number of SMs for a GPU, it is trading the yield curve of section 9 against the harvesting margin of this section, and the answer is why the GH100 has 144 SMs when the product needs 132.

## 11. The Economics of Process Control

This is a support section: the market and the money, at less depth than the physics above.

**KLA** (Milpitas, California; the 1997 merger of KLA Instruments and Tencor) holds about 55 to 56 percent of the process control equipment market and a considerably larger share of optical patterned-wafer inspection, where it has few real competitors at the leading edge. Its fiscal 2025 revenue was about $12 billion, over 80 percent from semiconductor process control. The remainder is split among **Applied Materials** (e-beam review and inspection, CD-SEM), **Hitachi High-Tech** (the CD-SEM leader, dark-field inspection, review), **ASML** (YieldStar overlay, HMI e-beam), **Onto Innovation** (film and OCD metrology, packaging inspection), **Nova** (OCD, integrated metrology, composition by XRF and **XPS**, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, a surface-composition technique), **Lasertec** (the sole supplier of actinic EUV mask inspection for years), **Camtek** (packaging), **Zeiss** (mask review), and **Bruker**, **Rigaku**, and **Thermo Fisher** in X-ray and electron microscopy. Skyverse and other suppliers in China also participate in inspection and metrology. Their products should be compared by the specific measurement or defect class and customer qualification, rather than assigned a single unsupported number of years of delay. The product example in Key Players shows why inspection and metrology need separate comparisons.

Process control takes about 12 percent of **wafer fab equipment (WFE)** spending in recent years, the "one dollar in eight" of the opening, within a 10 to 15 percent range depending on the mix of nodes being built, and it rises about a point per node as each adds more critical layers, tighter budgets, and new failure modes (stochastics, gate-all-around (GAA) channel thickness, backside power delivery, the power wiring on the back of the wafer, Module 12). A $20 billion gigafab therefore carries $2 billion to $3 billion of metrology and inspection tools, comparable to its entire etch fleet. Per wafer, the **cost of ownership** of inspection (the tool's price, floor space, staff and consumables, spread over the wafers it processes) is a few hundred dollars for a leading-edge logic wafer, a few percent of wafer cost, and the return is measured in yield points.

> **Worked example: what one yield point is worth.** A fab runs 100,000 wafers a month at $15,000 each. A 1 percent yield gain means 1 percent more sellable output from the same wafers: 100,000 × 12 × 0.01 = 12,000 wafers' worth of product a year, and 12,000 × $15,000 = $180 million per year. Against that, a $30 million inspector that buys a fraction of a yield point pays for itself in months.

### AI and digital twins

**AI in yield** is in production: neural-network ADC is standard; **virtual metrology** predicts a wafer's CD or thickness from its FDC traces without measuring it, extending sampling coverage to every wafer; deep-learning models trained on OCD spectra bypass RCWA for speed; and yield-management systems (PDF Solutions Exensio, Synopsys Silicon.da, Onto Discover, TSMC's internal systems) correlate tens of thousands of parameters per wafer against sort results to find root causes faster than a human can (as of 2025). TSMC has said AI-driven process control is one of its main tools for compressing the learning curve. Hybrid metrology (section 2) is the other data-fusion technique already in production.

A **digital twin** of a step or tool is a physics-based or data-trained model that reproduces the tool's response to its inputs. It lets APC be tuned offline, hot spots be predicted from layout before exposure, and inspection be targeted where risk is highest. ASML's computational lithography (Brion) is the most mature example: models of scanner, resist, and etch generate predicted CD and overlay maps checked against a few measured sites. The fab-level twin integrating all of it is where the large fabs are investing (as of 2025).

## 12. Metrology and Inspection for EUV

EUV introduced two problems the existing fleet could not solve, and one measurement it made central.

### Stochastic defects on the wafer

The first problem is **stochastic defects**: randomly located, at parts-per-billion rates per feature, 10 to 20 nm in size. Optical inspection cannot see them reliably; single-beam EBI would need days per wafer. The response has been multi-beam EBI targeted at post-develop and post-etch inspection of EUV contact and via layers, statistical methods that bound the per-feature failure rate, and large-area electrical monitoring (via and contact chains in WAT) to catch what inspection misses. Stochastic defectivity is now a standard column in every EUV layer's spec, with a mature-layer target below about 10⁻¹² per feature.

How the e-beam is actually deployed on an EUV contact layer follows from section 4. The layer is inspected twice. **After-develop inspection (ADI)** images the resist itself, at a landing energy of a few hundred eV so the beam does not shrink the pattern, because a stochastic defect found in resist can still be fixed: the wafer is stripped, recoated and re-exposed (the one rework the flow allows, section 8), whereas a defect found after etch is permanent. **After-etch inspection (AEI)** images the etched holes at a higher landing energy, where voltage contrast reports which contacts are really open to the substrate below, and tells the engineer which of the resist defects transferred. Both run on care areas: the densest contact arrays in a few dies, the same sites on every sampled wafer, at a pixel of ~5 to 10 nm so that a missing 20 nm contact is a patch of several dark pixels and not a single doubtful one, compared cell-to-cell within the array. Section 4 gave the coverage: a 25-beam tool reaches a few percent of a wafer per hour, so in the minutes a production wafer can be given, ADI and AEI see many millions of contacts, enough to count failures at a rate of 10⁻⁶ but nowhere near enough to verify 10⁻¹², and that gap is what sets the statistics.

The statistics need one idea. To be confident that a failure rate is below 10⁻¹² you would have to inspect many times 10¹² features and find almost none, which no tool can do. So fabs measure where failures are frequent, at lower dose or smaller feature size, where the rate might be 10⁻⁶ and a few million contacts give a solid count, and extrapolate along the steep curve of failure rate against dose or size to the production condition. The extrapolation is only as good as the model of the curve, which is why the WAT via chains, which do sample 10⁶ vias per structure at the real condition, remain the final check.

> **Worked example: how many contacts must be inspected.** Counting statistics are Poisson (section 9), so the relative uncertainty on a count of n failures is about 1/√n; ten failures give a rate known to ~30 percent, which is good enough for a trend. At a rate of 10⁻⁶ that needs 10⁷ contacts. At a 40 nm pitch each contact occupies 40 × 40 = 1,600 nm², so 10⁷ contacts cover 1.6 × 10¹⁰ nm² = 0.016 mm², which at a 5 nm pixel is 6.4 × 10⁸ pixels: under two seconds of beam time at 400 MHz, minutes with stage overhead. Feasible on every sampled wafer. Now try to verify the specification directly. If you inspect N features and find zero failures, the Poisson formula says the rate is below 3/N with 95 percent confidence (the chance of seeing no events when three are expected is e⁻³ ≈ 5 percent; statisticians call this the rule of three). To show a rate below 10⁻¹² you therefore need N = 3 × 10¹² contacts with not one failure: 4.8 × 10¹⁵ nm² = 4,800 mm² of dense contact array, about six reticle-size dies' worth with no room for anything else, or 1.9 × 10¹⁴ pixels, 5.6 days on a single beam or ~9 hours on a 25-beam tool at 15×, per layer, per process condition, and any process change resets the clock. Direct measurement at the specification is therefore out of reach, and the fab does what the paragraph above describes: it measures at three or four doses where the rate is 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁷ (10,000 down to 10 failures in 10⁸ contacts, each point taking minutes), plots the logarithm of the failure rate against dose or contact size, where the points fall close to a steep straight line near the **stochastic cliff** (the region where the failure rate climbs by orders of magnitude over a modest loss of dose or size), and extrapolates that line to the production dose. The extrapolated rate is what appears in the layer's specification column.

> **What can go wrong: stochastics.** *Extrapolating across a change of mechanism.* The failures counted at 10⁻⁶ are nearly all underdosed contacts that did not open; at the production dose the floor may be set by something else entirely, a merged pair caused by resist residue or a particle, so a straight-line fit from the cliff undershoots the true rate by orders of magnitude. The guard is the WAT via chain at the real condition, and a disagreement between chain and extrapolation is treated as a discovery, not a nuisance. *A partly closed contact looks fine in resist.* A hole that opened to half depth in the resist images normally from above at ADI and only reads as an open after etch, which is why the AEI voltage-contrast count, not the ADI count, is the one that gates the lot. *The inspection changes the resist.* The ADI beam shrinks the resist it images (section 2), so inspected dies etch differently from their neighbours; fabs confine ADI to sacrificial dies or accept the split, and check at sort that inspected and uninspected dies yield alike.

The measurement EUV made central is roughness. **Line-edge roughness (LER)** is the nanometre-scale wobble of a printed edge along its length, and **line-width roughness (LWR)** is the resulting variation of the line's width; both come from the same photon and chemical randomness that produces stochastic defects, and both feed straight into transistor variability. They are measured by CD-SEM, which images a length of line and computes the standard deviation of the edge position, quoted as 3σ; typical LWR on EUV lines is ~1 to 2 nm 3σ (it depends on resist and dose). The catch is that the SEM's own image noise adds apparent roughness, so tools apply a **noise correction**: they measure how the apparent roughness falls as more frames are averaged, and extrapolate to what it would be with no image noise at all. Roughness that is really there stays constant as frames are added; roughness that is noise shrinks.

> **Worked example: separating roughness from image noise.** Suppose measured edge variation is 1.0 nm RMS and an independent image-noise estimate is 0.6 nm RMS. If the contributions are uncorrelated, variances add: physical RMS = √(1.0² − 0.6²) = 0.8 nm, or 2.4 nm at 3σ. Four averaged frames would reduce the independent noise term to 0.3 nm and the measured RMS to √(0.8² + 0.3²) ≈ 0.85 nm. These are illustrative inputs, not a tool specification. Correlated scan noise or beam-induced resist shrink invalidates the simple averaging model and must be checked experimentally.

### The mask

The second problem is the **EUV mask**. An EUV mask reflects rather than transmits: it is a stack of about 40 pairs of molybdenum and silicon layers, each pair ~7 nm thick, that reflect 13.5 nm light by interference (Module 08), with an **absorber** pattern on top (a patterned layer of light-absorbing metal that defines the dark areas of the image). A **phase defect** is a flaw the mirror's brightness does not reveal: a 2 nm bump on the substrate lifts every layer above it, so light reflected there travels a slightly shorter path and arrives out of step with light from its neighbours; instead of adding, it cancels, and the defect prints as a dark spot although the mirror surface looks perfect. Inspection with deep-ultraviolet light sees the surface, not the phase, so **actinic inspection**, inspecting with the same 13.5 nm light the scanner uses, so that the inspector sees what the scanner sees, is required.

Lasertec's ABICS inspects mask **blanks** (the finished mirror before any pattern is written) and its ACTIS A150 (2019) and the current ACTIS A300 series inspect patterned masks actinically (as of 2025). At a high level an ACTIS tool is an EUV microscope: a 13.5 nm source, reflective optics that image a patch of the mask onto an EUV-sensitive camera, and a die-to-die or die-to-database comparison of the images, exactly as in section 4; scanning a full mask takes hours (Lasertec does not publish the figure). Lasertec remains the only company selling such a tool (as of 2025), while KLA competes in EUV mask inspection with DUV optical (its Teron series) and e-beam systems rather than actinic ones. Zeiss's AIMS EUV performs actinic review: it is a microscope that mimics the scanner's optics, imaging a defect with the scanner's **numerical aperture** (the range of angles the lens accepts, which sets its resolution, Module 07) and illumination, so that the defect's printability can be judged before repair. Blanks are not defect-free; a blank with a handful of known phase defects is still usable if the pattern is placed so that each defect falls under absorber, where it cannot print, a technique called **pattern shift**, which is why blank inspection must locate defects to within tens of nanometres.

> **What can go wrong: the mask.** *A particle on the mask.* A particle that lands on the mask prints on every field of every wafer that follows, giving the repeating-field signature of section 6; it is caught by the wafer map, by periodic re-inspection of masks in production, and increasingly prevented by the pellicle (section 6). *Pellicle rupture.* An EUV pellicle is a film tens of nanometres thick that absorbs part of the beam and runs hot; if it tears, its fragments land on the mask and the mask is contaminated at once. Scanners monitor pellicle integrity and fabs re-inspect the mask after any suspected event.

Who makes it: Lasertec (actinic blank and patterned-mask inspection), Zeiss (AIMS EUV review), KLA (Teron DUV mask inspection and e-beam mask inspection).

## 13. Wafer-Level Reliability

This is a side note; Module 18 covers qualification and burn-in. Yield says whether a die works today; **reliability** says whether it works in ten years, and the wafer-level part of the answer fits on one page.

The idea that makes it possible is **acceleration**. Wear-out speeds up predictably with voltage and temperature, so instead of waiting ten years, engineers stress test structures at, say, 1.5× the operating voltage and 125 °C for hours, fit the trend, and extrapolate to operating conditions. An **acceleration factor** of ~1,000× (illustrative; real factors depend on the mechanism and the stress) turns ~100 hours of test into ~100,000 hours, roughly ten years, of predicted life. The four mechanisms, each with its own physics and acceleration law:

- **Hot-carrier injection (HCI)**: electrons accelerated by the high field near the drain gain enough energy to enter the gate oxide and create **interface traps**, broken bonds at the silicon/oxide boundary that capture electrons and shift the threshold voltage; measured by stressing at high drain voltage and tracking the drift.
- **Negative-bias temperature instability (NBTI)**: a **PMOS** transistor (the type that conducts when its gate is pulled low; its channel carries positive charge, Module 11) held with negative gate bias at high temperature accumulates positive charge and interface traps, raising threshold voltage with a power-law dependence on time (the shift grows as t raised to an exponent of about 0.15 to 0.25, so it is fast at first and then slows). The counterpart in **NMOS** transistors (the type that conducts when its gate is pulled high; its channel carries electrons), **positive-bias temperature instability (PBTI)**, became significant with high-k dielectrics, because hafnium oxide traps electrons in a way silicon oxide did not.
- **Time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB)**: a gate oxide or inter-metal low-k under constant voltage eventually breaks down. Time-to-failure follows a **Weibull distribution**, the distribution used for weakest-link failures, where the earliest breakdown spot anywhere in the die decides the die's fate; it is strongly voltage-accelerated. Low-k TDDB between minimum-pitch copper lines is the dominant BEOL reliability concern.
- **Electromigration (EM)**: current displaces metal atoms until a line voids (Module 12); tested at high current density and temperature and extrapolated with **Black's equation**: MTTF = A · J<sup>−n</sup> · exp(E<sub>a</sub> / kT), where MTTF is the median time to failure, J the current density, n an exponent of about 1 to 2, E<sub>a</sub> the activation energy of the atomic motion, k Boltzmann's constant and T the absolute temperature, and A a constant fitted to the process. Doubling the current density cuts the life by 2 to 4 times; raising the temperature raises exp(E<sub>a</sub>/kT) much faster, which is what makes a 300 °C test predictive of a 100 °C life.

> **Worked example: the temperature acceleration in Black's equation.** The ratio of lifetimes at two temperatures is exp[(E<sub>a</sub>/k) · (1/T<sub>use</sub> − 1/T<sub>test</sub>)]. Take E<sub>a</sub> ≈ 0.9 eV (a typical value for copper interconnect, where the atoms move along the copper's top interface), k = 8.617 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K, so E<sub>a</sub>/k ≈ 10,400 K; a use temperature of 100 °C = 373 K and a test at 300 °C = 573 K. Then 1/373 − 1/573 = 0.002681 − 0.001745 = 0.000936 K⁻¹, the exponent is 10,400 × 0.000936 ≈ 9.8, and the acceleration is e<sup>9.8</sup> ≈ 17,000×. Doubling the current density on top of that, with n = 1.5, adds another 2<sup>1.5</sup> ≈ 2.8×, for roughly 5 × 10⁴ overall: a 200-hour test stands in for about 10⁷ hours, over a thousand years, so a much smaller temperature step is enough for a ten-year claim, and the engineer chooses the mildest stress that still finishes in days, because the further the extrapolation the more the answer depends on E<sub>a</sub> being right (an error of 0.1 eV in E<sub>a</sub> changes the acceleration of the 200 °C step above by a factor of about 3).

Each new process passes a qualification suite on these mechanisms (JEDEC's JEP001, the industry guideline for qualifying a foundry process, and related standards) before customers commit designs, and wafer-level reliability structures are monitored on a sampling basis in production. The measurements run on the same parametric testers as section 7, with heated chucks and long-duration stress channels added, or on dedicated wafer-level reliability systems from specialist vendors; the acceleration laws and the sample sizes are set by the JEDEC standards rather than by the tool.

## Summary

- Metrology measures a known parameter at a few sites to sub-nanometre precision; inspection sweeps the whole wafer for anything unexpected; review classifies what inspection found; yield engineering turns all of it into a D0 and a decision.
- Ellipsometry measures the polarization change (Ψ, Δ) of obliquely reflected light across many wavelengths and fits an optical model; it reaches 0.01 nm 3σ repeatability but model-limited accuracy, so recipes are calibrated against TEM. XRR, XRF, the four-point probe and FTIR cover density, composition, sheet resistance and chemical bonds.
- A CD-SEM images line edges with a ~500 eV electron beam to ~0.2 nm 3σ precision but charges and shrinks resist; OCD fits a grating's reflection spectrum to a 3D model in under a second and sees buried layers; TEM cross-sections are the ground truth that calibrates both.
- Overlay tools (KLA Archer image-based, ASML YieldStar diffraction-based) measure layer-to-layer shift to ~0.1 nm against a ~2 nm budget; the target-to-device offset is the largest error; results feed per-field scanner corrections.
- Bright-field inspection compares each die with its twin under broadband plasma light at ~20 nm sensitivity in minutes; dark-field catches particles through d⁶/λ⁴ scattering; e-beam resolves 5 to 15 nm defects and electrical opens by voltage contrast but would need ~80 days per wafer single-beam, so even 25-beam tools sample.
- A fab performs ~1.4 billion wafer-steps a year and inspects 1 to 3 wafers per lot at 30 to 60 points; the goal is to catch a process shift within hours, not to find every defect.
- Review SEMs with EDX and neural-network ADC turn coordinates into classified defects; the kill ratio, estimated by overlaying defect maps on sort maps, links each class to yield loss.
- Wafer-map signatures (edge ring, arc scratch, repeating field, quadrant) point to a tool family; commonality analysis on MES route data names the chamber, confirmed by a split lot.
- WAT/PCM structures in the scribe line (transistors, via chains, comb/serpentine, ring oscillators) give the first electrical readout at first metal, feed per-layer defect densities and the SPICE model, and gate wafer release.
- SPC detects (3σ control limits, Western Electric rules, C<sub>pk</sub> ≥ 1.33, meaning 4σ to the nearer spec limit); APC corrects run-to-run with an EWMA; FDC watches tool sensors; an excursion triggers an OCAP, and a day undetected can cost over $10 million.
- Yield = Y<sub>sys</sub> × Y<sub>param</sub> × Y<sub>random</sub>, with Y<sub>random</sub> = exp(−A · D0) (Poisson) or (1 + A · D0/α)<sup>−α</sup> (negative binomial, α ≈ 2); at D0 = 0.1/cm² a 100 mm² die yields ~90% and an 800 mm² die ~45 to 51%.
- D0 falls from ~0.5 to ~0.1/cm² over ~two years of yield learning; large dies wait for it; chiplets with known-good-die testing, memory repair and harvesting (H100: 132 of 144 SMs enabled) roughly double the effective yield of a large die.
- Process control is ~10 to 15% of fab equipment spending, led by KLA; EUV added stochastic defects, roughness metrology and actinic mask inspection (Lasertec) to the list; wafer-level reliability testing (HCI, BTI, TDDB, EM) answers whether the die still works in ten years.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Metrology and inspection are the same thing."** → Metrology measures a known parameter at a few sites with high precision; inspection sweeps the whole wafer for anything unexpected; review classifies what inspection found. Different physics, tools, and sampling plans.
- **"An ellipsometer measures thickness directly."** → It measures polarization change (Ψ and Δ) and infers thickness by fitting an optical model. A wrong model gives a wrong thickness with beautiful 0.01 nm repeatability; accuracy comes from TEM calibration.
- **"Control limits are the same as spec limits."** → Control limits are where the process naturally wanders, computed from its own history; spec limits are what the customer requires. A process can be in control and out of spec, or in spec and out of control, and each case calls for a different response.
- **"A wafer that passed inspection has no defects."** → Inspection samples one to three wafers per lot at a few dozen points, at a sensitivity of ~20 nm optically, and cannot economically image an entire wafer at the finest e-beam pixel size; "passed" means the sampled defect counts were inside their control limits, not that the wafer is clean.
- **"Yield is a property of the node."** → D0 is a function of time and fab. A node at 0.5/cm² in risk production and at 0.1/cm² two years later have completely different economics, and D0 is only the random component; systematic and parametric losses are separate.
- **"A 45 percent yield on a GPU die means 55 percent of the wafer is thrown away."** → Harvesting (132 of 144 SMs on H100) and memory repair turn most defective dies into sellable product; effective yield for a large harvested die is often near 75 to 80 percent.
- **"Optical inspection resolves 20 nm defects."** → It detects them as perturbations of the reflected or scattered field at wavelengths ten times larger than the defect. What it can see depends on the surrounding pattern and film stack, and it cannot say what the defect is; that is the review SEM's job.
- **"Faster e-beam inspection will replace optical inspection."** → Even 25-beam tools cover a few percent of a wafer per hour; optical covers all of it in minutes. Multi-beam EBI extends sampling to defects optical cannot see; it does not replace full-wafer coverage.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 12 finished the physical wafer: transistors in the FEOL, contacts in the MOL, and 15 to 18 levels of copper and ruthenium interconnect in the BEOL. In reality every one of those steps was interleaved with the measurements described here: film thickness after each deposition, CD and overlay after each lithography and etch, defect inspection at 30 to 60 points along the flow, WAT at first metal and end of line, all feeding SPC charts and APC loops back to the scanners, etchers, and deposition tools. The inputs to this module are wafers in process, monitor wafers, reference standards, and a $2 billion to $3 billion fleet of tools (the one-dollar-in-eight figure of the opening) supplied largely by KLA, Applied, Hitachi High-Tech, and ASML; the outputs are dispositioned wafers with a WAT record and a predicted yield, a database the design team uses to update SPICE models and design rules, and a D0 number that decides whether the node is profitable. Module 14 takes the finished wafer to wafer sort, where each die is contacted by a probe card and tested functionally, the harvesting and binning decisions described here are actually made, and the yield model meets the real bin map.
