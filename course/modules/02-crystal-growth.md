# Module 02: Crystal Growth: Czochralski and Float Zone

Module 01 ended with polysilicon: chunks and granules of 9N to 11N silicon (**9N** means 99.9999999 % pure, one foreign atom per billion), chemically extremely pure but not yet the structure needed for the wafers in this course. Polysilicon is a jumble of millimetre-sized grains, each a little crystal pointing in a different direction. Where grains meet, the atomic pattern is interrupted. Such **grain boundaries** can trap charge and interfere with predictable current flow, so the active silicon of these wafers must be one continuous crystal. The job of this stage is to convert a few hundred kilograms of that rubble into one single crystal, 300 mm across and about two metres long, in which every atom sits on the same **diamond-cubic lattice** with the same orientation from one end to the other. (A **crystal lattice** is the repeating three-dimensional pattern the atoms sit on; silicon's is called diamond cubic because each atom is bonded to four neighbours at the corners of a tetrahedron, exactly as carbon is in diamond.) The crystal must contain no **dislocations** at all (line defects, defined in the next section), and the concentrations of **dopant** (a deliberately added impurity such as boron or phosphorus, typically one atom in ten to a hundred million, whose spare or missing electron is what lets silicon conduct in a controlled way), oxygen and carbon must be held to within a few tens of percent along the whole length.

That is the hard part. Not "a crystal", but a crystal of 300 kg with zero line defects, grown from a melt at 1,414 °C that is slowly eating its own container, hanging from a neck the width of a pencil lead. Nearly everything in this module is a consequence of those constraints.

By the end you should be able to explain why the ingot must be one crystal and why it is grown along one particular direction, how Czochralski growth works and why one run takes two days, what the Dash neck does and why the whole ingot hangs from it, what the ratio v/G decides about the invisible defects in every wafer, why oxygen is deliberately left in the crystal, and where Float Zone growth fits.

## Before you start

- **Purity in nines and atomic ppm.** 9N is one foreign atom per billion. Silicon has 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so 1 part per million atomic (**ppma**) is 5 × 10¹⁶ atoms/cm³ and 1 part per billion atomic (**ppba**) is 5 × 10¹³ atoms/cm³ (Module 01).
- **What polysilicon looks like.** It arrives as **Siemens chunk** (broken pieces of the rods grown by **chemical vapour deposition (CVD)**, the deposition of a solid from a gas that decomposes on a hot surface) or **FBR granules** (pea-sized beads from the fluidised-bed route), at ~$20 to 40/kg for electronic grade (Module 01).
- **The crucible and the hot parts.** The melt sits in a crucible of **fused silica** (SiO₂ glass, made by **arc-fusing** quartz sand: melting it with an electric arc and letting it cool too fast to crystallise, so it sets as a glass), and the parts that heat and insulate it are graphite. Both are consumables that wear out in use (Module 01, Section 10).
- **Semiconductor basics.** Pure silicon barely conducts; it conducts because of added dopants. Boron gives **p-type** silicon (conduction by **holes**, missing electrons that move like positive charges); phosphorus, arsenic and antimony give **n-type** silicon (conduction by spare electrons). **Resistivity**, in ohm-centimetres (Ω·cm), is the grower's proxy for dopant concentration. A transistor's channel is the few nanometres of silicon just under the surface, beneath a **gate oxide** only 1 to 2 nm thick (Module 11).
- **School chemistry of freezing.** A dissolved impurity lowers a liquid's freezing point (salt on ice), and a solid usually holds less of it than the liquid it froze from (sea ice is fresher than sea water). Solubility falls on cooling (sugar in tea crystallises as the tea cools).
- **How heat moves.** Conduction, convection and radiation. At 1,400 °C radiation, which grows as the fourth power of absolute temperature, dominates: anything that can see a cold wall loses heat fast.
- **Diffusion and advection.** Atoms random-walk down a concentration gradient, faster when hot (**diffusion**). A defect can also be carried bodily along by a moving solid, like a leaf on a river (**advection**).
- **Electromagnetism.** A conductor moving through a magnetic field has currents induced in it that oppose the motion (Lenz's law). An alternating current in a coil induces currents in a nearby conductor and heats it (an induction hob).

## Why Single Crystal, and Why <100>

### Grain boundaries kill transistors

Three facts about silicon explain why the whole module exists. First, in a perfect silicon crystal there is a range of energies, the **band gap** (~1.1 eV; an **electron-volt** is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt), in which no electron can sit; that is why silicon can be switched off. Second, a **p-n junction** is the boundary between a p-doped and an n-doped region; it passes current in one direction and blocks it in the other (**reverse bias**), which is how a transistor stays off. Third, the transistor's switch is the **gate oxide**, the insulating SiO₂ film 1 to 2 nm thick between the gate electrode and the channel (Module 11). One more piece of vocabulary before the defect. In p-type silicon holes are the **majority carriers** (there are many of them) and electrons the **minority carriers** (few, and present only because something put them there); light, or a p-n junction pushed into forward bias, **injects** extra minority electrons, and each one survives until it meets a hole and **recombines** with it, the electron dropping into the missing bond so that both disappear. A broken or strained bond at a defect adds a stepping-stone energy level inside the gap, letting electrons cross where they should not (**leakage**) and letting an injected electron recombine early. The average time such an electron survives is the **minority-carrier lifetime**, the standard health metric of silicon: a long lifetime means few defects, and it is what a junction's leakage and a solar cell's efficiency both depend on.

A **grain boundary** is a two-dimensional sheet of atoms where two **crystallites** (single small crystal grains) with different orientations meet. Across it, the lattice is disordered over a width of a few atomic spacings. Three things happen there that a device engineer cannot tolerate:

1. **Dangling and strained bonds** create electronic states inside the band gap. These act as **generation-recombination centres**, stepping stones for electrons, which means leakage current in a reverse-biased junction and a short minority-carrier lifetime.
2. **Fast diffusion paths.** Dopants and metallic contaminants (Fe, Cu, Ni) diffuse orders of magnitude faster along a boundary than through the lattice. A junction that crosses a boundary is a junction whose depth you do not control.
3. **Etch and oxidation anisotropy.** The oxidation rate and etch rate of silicon depend on crystal orientation, and so does **implant channelling** (ions shot into the crystal travel further along open lattice directions, Module 10). A gate oxide grown across two grains has two thicknesses.

Polycrystalline silicon is perfectly good for solar cells of modest efficiency and for the gate electrodes and interconnect inside a chip (deposited "poly"), but the active channel of a transistor has to be single crystal. Even a single **dislocation** (a one-dimensional line defect where a half-plane of atoms terminates, leaving a row of strained bonds along its length) is a problem: metal atoms collect along it, it provides a conducting path through a junction that should block current, and under the thermal cycling of a fab it multiplies. The industry standard since the early 1960s has been zero dislocations in the ingot, full stop.

> **What can go wrong:** A single dislocation or grain boundary that reaches the channel region of a transistor gives that transistor leakage current and an early gate-oxide breakdown, and the metals that collect along the defect make both worse with every furnace step. A fab does not see the defect itself; it sees a yield signature at the level of the **die** (one chip-sized rectangle on the wafer, before the wafer is cut into individual chips; plural **dies**), a cluster or streak of failing dies on the wafer map, that a substrate engineer can trace back to a position in the crystal (Module 13). That is why the wafer maker's specification is not "few dislocations" but "none", checked on every ingot.

### Orientation: <100> versus <111>

> **Intuition:** Picture the unit cube of the lattice. A **(100)** plane is a cube face. A **(110)** plane cuts the cube diagonally through two opposite edges (a face diagonal). A **(111)** plane slices off a corner, passing through three corners adjacent to it. These are **Miller indices**: round brackets name one plane, curly brackets {111} name the whole family of equivalent planes (one corner-cut for each of the cube's eight corners), angle brackets <100> name a direction, and in a cubic crystal the <hkl> direction is perpendicular to the (hkl) plane. The {111} planes are the most crowded ("close-packed") planes in silicon and the {100} planes the more open ones, which is the whole reason the silicon-to-oxide interface is cleaner on (100).

Why this lattice? Each silicon atom has four outer electrons and makes four bonds, and four bonds repel each other into the corners of a tetrahedron; stack tetrahedra so that every atom has exactly four neighbours and the diamond-cubic structure is the result. Silicon has a **lattice constant** (the edge length of the repeating cube) of 0.5431 nm and 5.0 × 10²² atoms per cm³. For scale, a 300 mm wafer is ~550 million unit cubes across, and 5 × 10²² per cm³ means one part per billion is 5 × 10¹³ atoms per cm³ (Module 01). A wafer is a slice perpendicular to a chosen crystal direction, and that direction is decided once, at the seed: every atom that freezes onto the seed copies the orientation of the atoms below it, so the whole ingot inherits the seed's orientation and nothing done later can change it. (The slicing saw of Module 03 can only choose how far off that axis to cut, and prime wafers are cut within a fraction of a degree of it.) Two directions matter industrially:

- **<111>**: the direction perpendicular to the close-packed {111} planes. {111} is the slowest-growing, slowest-etching, most densely packed plane. Historically **bipolar transistors** (the pre-CMOS transistor type, switched by current rather than by a gate) were built on (111) wafers, and today it is the standard substrate for **epitaxy** (growing a crystalline film that continues the substrate's lattice, Module 03) of **gallium nitride (GaN)**, the material of many power and **radio-frequency (RF)** devices (RF meaning the gigahertz signals of wireless links and radar), because the hexagonal GaN lattice matches the three-fold symmetry of (111).
- **<100>**: perpendicular to the cube faces. The atomic density of the (100) surface is lower (6.8 × 10¹⁴ atoms/cm² versus 7.8 × 10¹⁴ for (111)), which means fewer bonds must be terminated at the Si/SiO₂ interface. The measured **interface trap density** D_it at a good thermally grown oxide on (100) is around 10¹⁰ eV⁻¹cm⁻², several times lower than on (111). In plain terms: roughly one imperfect bond per 100,000 surface atoms, spread over the energy range of the band gap. Electron **mobility**, how fast electrons (or holes) drift per volt per centimetre, so higher mobility means a faster transistor, is also highest on (100) in an **inversion layer**, the nanometre-thin sheet of electrons the gate pulls up under the oxide, which is the channel.

That is the whole reason planar **CMOS** (circuits built from paired n-channel and p-channel transistors) moved to (100) in the 1970s and never left: lowest interface state density, best n-channel mobility, plus the practical bonus that (100) wafers cleave cleanly along the {110} planes at right angles, which made scribe-and-break **dicing** (cutting the finished wafer into chips, Module 16) easy. Holes, the carriers of the p-channel, are the exception: they move fastest on (110) surfaces and, on a (100) surface, along <100> directions rather than the traditional <110>. Some hybrid-orientation experiments (**pMOS**, the p-channel half of CMOS, built on (110) while the n-channel stayed on (100)) came and went; the industry stayed with (100) ingots and instead rotated the chip layout by 45° relative to the notch, so that channels run along <100> instead of <110>, which raises hole mobility on the same ingot with no change to the crystal at all.

> **Why it matters downstream:** A **FinFET** is a transistor whose channel is a vertical fin of silicon (Module 11). FinFETs on a (100) wafer with fins along <110> present (110) sidewalls, which happen to favour hole mobility, so the orientation chosen for planar transistors in the 1970s still works in the 3D era. The crystal grower's choice of <100> is baked into every transistor architecture built since.

The **notch** ground into a 300 mm wafer (the flat, on older wafers) marks a <110> direction so lithography and dicing can be aligned to the lattice. The ingot is grown along <100>; the notch is put in later, in Module 03.

## A Brief History in Three Names

**Jan Czochralski**, a Polish metallurgist in Berlin, discovered the method in 1916 and published it in 1918. The often-told story is that he dipped his pen into a crucible of molten tin instead of the inkwell and pulled out a thread of single-crystal metal; whether or not it happened that way, the method was for decades a laboratory tool for measuring how fast metals crystallise. **Gordon Teal** and J. B. Little at Bell Labs applied it to germanium in 1950, and Teal with Ernest Buehler grew the first silicon single crystals and grown p-n junctions in 1952, which turned the transistor into a manufacturable device. **William Dash** at General Electric (1958 to 1959) worked out how to grow the crystal with zero dislocations. Float Zone came from Bell Labs (Theuerer, 1952) and Keck and Golay (1953). The 1950s pullers grew 25 mm ingots; today's grow 300 mm ingots weighing 300 kg, and every one of them still starts with Dash's trick: without it there would be no dislocation-free silicon at any diameter.

## Anatomy of a 300 mm CZ Puller

Before the machine, the job. A 300 mm puller must hold ~400 kg of liquid silicon at 1,420 °C in an atmosphere free of oxygen and nitrogen for two days; keep the temperature of the melt surface steady to well under a degree, because the crystal freezes onto it at a rate set by that temperature; pull a crystal upward at ~1 mm/min while holding its diameter to a fraction of a millimetre; and do all of this while the crucible dissolves and the graphite around it wears. Every part of the machine answers one of those four constraints.

> **Intuition:** CZ is rock candy on a string. In the kitchen version, a string hangs in sugar syrup and sugar crystallises onto it. In the puller, the string is a small perfect seed crystal, the syrup is liquid silicon, and each new atom that freezes copies the lattice of the atoms below it, so the whole 300 kg ingot is one continuation of a 10 mm seed. The seed is withdrawn slowly enough that freezing keeps pace, and rotated so that the crystal grows round. Everything else in the machine exists to keep that simple picture true for 2 m of crystal.

A modern 300 mm Czochralski puller is a tower roughly 8 to 12 m tall including its pit, split into a **furnace chamber** below and a **pull chamber** above, separated by an **isolation valve** (a large vacuum gate) so the crystal can be withdrawn without venting the hot zone. Walk around it from the outside in.

**Chamber.** Double-walled stainless steel, water-cooled between the walls. The water jacket keeps the steel below ~100 °C and sets the cold boundary that every heat-flow calculation in the hot zone terminates on. Quartz viewports give the **CCD camera** (a digital video camera) and the operator a line of sight to the melt surface; they are often purged with a trickle of argon to keep fog off them.

**Atmosphere.** The chamber runs under flowing argon at reduced pressure, typically ~10 to 50 mbar (1 to 5 % of atmospheric pressure), at flows on the order of 50 to 150 **standard litres per minute** (litres of gas measured at room conditions), delivered down a purge tube around the crystal and pumped out at the bottom. Why the flow? The melt slowly dissolves its silica crucible, and most of that oxygen leaves the melt surface as **silicon monoxide (SiO)** gas; the argon sweeps it away, together with carbon monoxide from the graphite parts (the full story is in the Oxygen section). Lower pressure and higher flow both increase SiO evaporation, which is one of the ways oxygen content is set. SiO condenses as a brown powder on every cold surface, and the powder is **pyrophoric** (it can ignite spontaneously in air), so puller cleaning is a fire hazard treated with respect. Silicon must never see oxygen or nitrogen at these temperatures: hot silicon reacts with O₂ to grow an oxide skin and with N₂ to grow a silicon nitride crust, and either one floating on the melt stops single-crystal growth. So the chamber is **helium-leak-checked** before every run: helium is sprayed on every joint outside while a **mass spectrometer** (an instrument that sorts gas molecules by mass and so can pick out helium at parts per million) sniffs the pumped gas inside.

**Crucible.** The melt sits in a **fused-silica crucible**. Quartz is crystalline SiO₂; fused silica is the same SiO₂ melted and cooled too fast to crystallise, a **glass**. A glass has no sharp melting point; it softens gradually, and at 1,420 °C fused silica flows like very stiff honey under load. For 300 mm growth the crucible is typically 32 inch (~810 mm) or 36 inch (~915 mm) in diameter with walls ~10 to 15 mm thick, holding roughly 300 to 450 kg. It is a two-layer arc-fused part (Module 01): an outer body of natural quartz sand (bubble-rich, opaque) and an inner ~2 to 3 mm layer fused from **synthetic quartz** (made from silicon tetrachloride, with metals at parts-per-billion levels) that is bubble-free and is the only layer the melt touches. Bubbles in the inner layer would open as the wall dissolves and eject silica particles into the melt, each a dislocation nucleus at the growth interface. Over a run the glass slowly reverts to crystal, a process called **devitrification**; here the crystal is **cristobalite**, the form of SiO₂ that is stable at high temperature. Many crucibles carry a barium-doped inner coating that seeds a continuous, adherent cristobalite skin which dissolves evenly; uncontrolled devitrification, by contrast, forms random patches that crack and flake, and every flake in the melt is a dislocation seed. Fused silica begins to deform measurably somewhere above ~1,000 to 1,100 °C, well below its ~1,650 °C softening point, so at 1,420 °C the crucible cannot carry a 400 kg melt on its own and sits in a machined **graphite susceptor** (a supporting cup that also conducts heat from the heater) that carries the load. Two facts about oxygen belong here, because they explain half of the recipe: the melt dissolves the crucible wall at ~10 µm per hour for the whole run, and the oxygen that survives in the crystal, ~1 % of what dissolves, is what gives CZ silicon its characteristic 10 to 18 ppma of oxygen. The crucible is single-use: after 50 hours at 1,420 °C it has dissolved a good fraction of a millimetre into the melt and cracks on cool-down. It costs several thousand dollars (more for synthetic-lined 36-inch parts). Suppliers include Shin-Etsu Quartz, Momentive Technologies, Heraeus Conamic, Japan Super Quartz (SUMCO) and Coorstek plus Chinese makers; the ultra-pure sand comes overwhelmingly from Spruce Pine, North Carolina (Sibelco and The Quartz Corp), a single point of failure we return to in Module 20.

> **What can go wrong:** *Bubbles* in the inner layer open as the wall dissolves and eject silica particles; the first sign is usually structure loss (the crystal's habit lines vanish, see the Recipe). *Devitrification flakes* do the same and are the reason the barium coating exists. The *crucible can sag or crack mid-run* if the susceptor fits badly or the charge point-loads the wall; a crack lets molten silicon reach the graphite, which shows as a sudden change in heater current and ends the run. *SiO fog* on a viewport blinds the diameter camera, so viewports are argon-purged and the control loop is designed to fall back on the load cell. A *leak* lets in air: oxygen and nitrogen form a skin on the melt that stops growth, and the **residual-gas analyser** (a small mass spectrometer sampling the chamber gas) is what flags it. In every case the fix is procedural: better charging, a new crucible lot, a rebuilt seal, and a remelt of whatever crystal was lost.

**Heater and hot zone.** Around the susceptor stands a cylindrical **graphite resistance heater**. Graphite (in the hot zone, **isostatic graphite**, a fine-grained grade pressed uniformly from all sides so that it machines cleanly and wears evenly) is the one affordable material that stays rigid at 1,500 °C (it never melts; it sublimes above ~3,600 °C) and can be machined, but it burns in air and reacts with SiO, so the chamber must be oxygen-free and the parts wear. A solid graphite tube would have far too little electrical resistance to heat with a safe voltage, so the tube is slotted into a zigzag **meander**, which lengthens the current path and lets a low-voltage, high-current supply (on the order of 100 to 200 kW for a 300 mm puller, about 50 to 70 domestic kettles running flat out for two days) heat it to roughly 1,500 to 1,600 °C. Outside the heater are layers of **carbon-felt** insulation (a blanket of carbon fibres, an insulator that survives 2,000 °C) and shields of graphite or **carbon-fibre composite (CFC)** (carbon fibres in a carbon matrix, stiff and strong when hot); above the melt sits a conical **heat shield** or reflector that hangs around the growing crystal. The collection of heater, susceptor, insulation, shields, purge tube and support is called the **hot zone**, and its geometry is the single most closely guarded piece of intellectual property in a wafer company. The hot zone sets the temperature gradient in the crystal near the interface, that is, how many kelvin the crystal cools per centimetre of height just above the melt (the "G" in v/G, below), the radial uniformity of that gradient, the melt surface temperature distribution, and the argon flow pattern that carries SiO away. Hot zones are rebuilt and re-machined after a certain number of runs; the graphite slowly converts to silicon carbide where SiO reaches it.

> **Worked example: where the heater's 150 kW goes.**
> Start with the black-body law used in Module 01: a surface at absolute temperature T radiates up to σT⁴ per square metre, with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴). At the melting point (1,414 °C = 1,687 K) that is 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 1,687⁴ ≈ 460 kW/m² for a perfectly black surface. Liquid silicon is a shiny metal with an emissivity of only ~0.2 to 0.3, so the exposed melt surface, ~0.4 m² in a 32-inch crucible once the 0.07 m² crystal cross-section is subtracted, emits ~40 to 60 kW; the heat shield above it returns a good part of that. The solid crystal is a duller radiator (emissivity ~0.5 to 0.6): the first half-metre above the melt, ~0.5 m² of side area at an average of perhaps 1,200 °C (σT⁴ ≈ 270 kW/m²), radiates ~70 kW, most of it straight to the water-cooled upper chamber. Add heat leaking through the carbon felt to the water jacket, the exposed crucible rim and the argon stream, and 100 to 200 kW is used up. Now the useful part: at 1 mm/min a 306 mm crystal freezes π × 0.153² × 0.001 ≈ 7.4 × 10⁻⁵ m³ of silicon per minute, ~0.17 kg/min, releasing 0.17 kg/min × 1.79 MJ/kg ≈ 5 kW of latent heat, only ~3 % of the heater power (all figures ~). The heater is not there to freeze silicon; it is there to keep 400 kg of liquid molten while the crystal radiates, and that radiation from the crystal's surface is exactly why G (the temperature gradient along the crystal just above the interface, in K/cm; the edge sees the cold chamber directly, so it cools faster than the core) is higher at its edge than at its centre, the fact on which the whole defect story below turns.

**Mechanisms.** The **crucible shaft** enters from below through a **rotary vacuum feedthrough** (a rotating shaft passing through the chamber wall through seals that keep air out); it rotates the crucible (typically 5 to 15 rpm) and lifts it during the run to keep the melt surface at a constant height relative to the heater as silicon is removed. From above, the **seed lift** is either a rigid shaft or, on nearly all large pullers, a **cable** (a stranded tungsten or steel wire) on a drum, because a rigid shaft long enough for a 2 m crystal plus its travel would be ~5 m long, impractically tall and prone to wobble. The cable carries the seed holder and rotates the crystal (10 to 20 rpm, opposite sense to the crucible). A **load cell** (a weighing sensor in the cable path) or the diameter camera feeds the control loop.

**Magnets.** Almost all 300 mm pullers are **magnetic CZ (MCZ)** machines: a **superconducting magnet** (a coil cooled by liquid helium or a **cryocooler**, a closed-cycle refrigerator that reaches a few kelvin, until it carries current with zero resistance, so once charged the field costs almost nothing to hold) or, on older tools, resistive copper coils surround the furnace chamber and apply a field of a few tenths of a tesla to the melt. For scale, 0.3 T is ~6,000 times the Earth's magnetic field and a tenth to a fifth of an MRI scanner's. Why the field matters, the field geometries in use and who builds the magnets are covered in the Magnetic Czochralski section below.

**Automation.** The control system runs the entire recipe: heater power, pull speed, seed and crucible rotation, crucible lift, argon flow and pressure, magnet current, and the **automatic diameter control (ADC)** loop. Each loop pairs a sensor with an actuator. The CCD camera measures the width of the bright meniscus ring at the crystal's edge, and the ADC loop turns any diameter error into a pull-rate correction (fast, minutes) and a heater-power correction (slow, tens of minutes); how the ring width encodes the diameter is explained in step 7 of the recipe. The load cell in the cable reads the crystal's weight, and the rate of weight gain is a second, independent measure of diameter that takes over if SiO fog blinds the camera. A **pyrometer** (a non-contact thermometer that reads temperature from the brightness and colour of the glow) aimed at the melt surface or the heater closes the temperature loop with heater power. Pressure gauges and **mass-flow controllers** (valves that meter a gas by mass rather than volume) hold the argon; position encoders on the crucible shaft hold the lift. What an operator actually watches, on screens beside the puller, is the live image of the meniscus and the four habit lines, the diameter trace against its setpoint and the heater-power trace, because a jump in any of them is the first sign of a bridge collapse, a crucible crack or structure loss. A modern puller runs with minimal operator intervention from seed dip to cool-down; operators charge crucibles, load seeds, and intervene on structure loss.

Semiconductor pullers come from a handful of builders: **PVA TePla** (Germany; its Crystal Growing Systems unit makes the EKZ series and is also the leading Float Zone furnace supplier), **Ferrotec** (Japan/China, grown large in solar), **Linton Crystal Technologies** (Rochester, New York), **S-TECH** (Korea), and **Zhejiang Jingsheng (JSG)** (China), whose catalogue includes IC-grade 8–12 inch silicon crystal-growth and processing equipment as well as solar equipment. The Rochester lineage matters: Hamco (1952) became **Kayex**, which introduced the first 300 mm puller (KX300) in 1987 and built much of the pre-2000 fleet; Linton has owned the Kayex IP exclusively since 2013. The big wafer makers also design or heavily customise their own pullers and hot zones and treat them as proprietary; a vendor puller is a starting point, not the finished tool.

## The CZ Recipe, Step by Step

A 300 mm run takes on the order of two and a half to three and a half days from charging to a cool ingot on the floor. Here is the sequence.

### 1. Charge loading

The crucible is placed in the susceptor and filled with polysilicon: Siemens chunk (broken pieces of the CVD-grown rods of Module 01, acid-etched to remove crushing contamination) and/or FBR granules (pea-sized beads from the fluidised-bed route), usually mixed so granules fill the voids between chunks. Charge sizes for 300 mm are ~300 to 450 kg, with some machines built for 500 kg or more. Charging is skilled work: large chunks must not point-load the softening quartz wall. Boron dopant may go in with the charge; volatile arsenic and antimony are added later through a doping tube. The chamber is closed, pumped, leak-checked and back-filled with argon.

> **What can go wrong:** *Bridging.* If the stack wedges itself against the wall, the charge underneath melts away and leaves a **bridge**, an arch of unmelted chunks hanging over liquid; when it finally collapses it splashes silicon onto the heater or cracks the crucible. *Popcorn splash.* Chunks with trapped gas or a rough, porous "popcorn" surface (Module 01) can spit as they melt. Operators stack against both: large chunks at the bottom and against the wall, small pieces and granules filling the gaps, nothing leaning, and a melt-down power ramp gentle enough that the pile slumps from the wall inward. A collapsed bridge shows as a jump in heater power or a splash on the camera; the run continues if the crucible survived and is abandoned if silicon reached the graphite.

### 2. Meltdown

Heater power ramps over several hours to bring the charge through 1,414 °C, the melting point of silicon. The bulk melt is **superheated** (held above its melting point) to roughly 1,420 to 1,450 °C, hotter at the crucible wall and coolest at the centre of the free surface where the crystal will grow. Meltdown of 400 kg takes ~6 to 10 hours; the **latent heat** alone is a large part of it. Latent heat is the energy needed to turn solid at 1,414 °C into liquid at 1,414 °C with no change in temperature: ~1,790 J/g, ~720 MJ for 400 kg. That is 200 kWh, two to three weeks of a household's electricity, which a 150 kW heater could deliver in ~1.5 h if nothing were lost; the rest of the 6 to 10 h is radiation loss and the thermal inertia of a tonne of graphite. Liquid silicon is denser than solid (2.57 versus 2.33 g/cm³), so the charge slumps as it melts from the wall inward. Once molten, the melt is held 30 to 60 minutes for thermal stabilisation with the surface centre brought to just above the melting point.

### 3. Dopant addition and the segregation coefficient

The dopant sets the **resistivity** of the wafer: how strongly the material resists current, in ohm-centimetres; 10 Ω·cm means a 1 cm cube measures 10 Ω between opposite faces. Undoped silicon is ~200,000 Ω·cm, and one boron atom for every ~40 million silicon atoms (1.35 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, about 27 ppba) brings it to 10 Ω·cm, so resistivity is the grower's proxy for dopant concentration. Resistivity in turn sets how the transistors' **isolation** behaves, that is, how well neighbouring transistors are kept from leaking current into one another through the substrate they share (Module 11). Four elements cover the market: **boron** (p-type, the overwhelming majority of logic and memory substrates), **phosphorus** (n-type, moderate to high resistivity), **arsenic** (n-type, heavily doped substrates for n/n+ epi, a lightly doped layer on a heavily doped substrate, explained in the Epi section), and **antimony** (n-type, heavily doped, with less **autodoping** than arsenic during epi, autodoping being dopant that evaporates from the hot substrate and re-enters the layer growing on it). Gallium is used in solar (it avoids the boron-oxygen light-induced degradation) but barely at all in semiconductor wafers.

> **Intuition:** Sea ice is fresher than the sea water it froze from, because the ice lattice has no comfortable place for salt ions; they stay in the liquid, and the remaining water gets saltier. The same happens at the growing silicon interface. The **segregation coefficient** measures how welcoming the silicon lattice is to each element: boron fits well (0.8, most of it goes into the crystal), antimony fits badly (0.023, almost all of it stays in the melt).

Each dopant partitions between solid and liquid according to its **equilibrium segregation coefficient** k₀ = C_solid / C_liquid at the interface. C_solid is its concentration in the newly frozen solid, and C_liquid is its concentration in the liquid touching that solid. The ratio compares concentrations, not the fraction of all dopant atoms that have entered the ingot. A value below one means the growing crystal takes a less concentrated share, so the remaining melt becomes enriched as growth continues:

| Element | k₀ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Boron | 0.8 | near-uniform along ingot |
| Phosphorus | 0.35 | significant axial gradient |
| Arsenic | 0.3 | volatile, added after meltdown |
| Antimony | 0.023 | very volatile, hard to reach low resistivity |
| Gallium | 0.008 | solar only; strong gradient |
| Aluminium | 0.002 | contaminant |
| Oxygen | ~1.25 | enriched in solid, but supply falls over run |
| Carbon | 0.07 | contaminant, accumulates in melt |
| Nitrogen | 7 × 10⁻⁴ | deliberately added at ~10¹⁴ cm⁻³ in some products |

Because k < 1 for every dopant (oxygen, which is not a dopant, is the one common element with k > 1), the solid takes less than the liquid holds and the melt gets richer as the crystal grows. With good mixing the concentration in the solid follows the **Scheil equation** (normal freezing):

C_s(g) = k C₀ (1 − g)^(k−1)

where C_s is the dopant concentration in the solid, g is the fraction of the original melt that has solidified and C₀ is the starting melt concentration. It says that the last liquid to freeze is the dirtiest, assuming the liquid is perfectly mixed and nothing diffuses inside the solid once it has frozen. For boron (k = 0.8) the exponent is −0.2, so the concentration rises by only ~1.6× between the seed end and 90 % solidified. For phosphorus (k = 0.35) it rises ~4.5× over the same length, which is why n-type CZ ingots yield fewer wafers inside a tight resistivity spec than p-type, and why customers who need tight, high-resistivity n-type (power devices, detectors) buy Float Zone material instead.

The real crystal does not quite see k₀. Right next to the interface the melt is not stirred; rejected dopant piles up in a thin **boundary layer**, a fraction of a millimetre thick, so the crystal sees a richer liquid than the bulk and takes in more than k₀ predicts. The **effective segregation coefficient** k_eff therefore lies between k₀ and 1, and faster growth or slower stirring push it toward 1. Burton, Prim and Slichter quantified this in 1953: k_eff = k₀ / [k₀ + (1 − k₀) exp(−v δ / D)], with v the growth rate, δ the boundary-layer thickness and D the dopant's diffusivity in the liquid (~10⁻⁴ cm²/s). Worked number: with δ ≈ 0.2 mm and v = 1 mm/min, v δ / D ≈ 0.33 and exp(−0.33) ≈ 0.72, so boron's k_eff ≈ 0.8 / (0.8 + 0.2 × 0.72) ≈ 0.85 and phosphorus's ≈ 0.35 / (0.35 + 0.65 × 0.72) ≈ 0.43 (all ~). Crystal rotation is the stirring, which is why rotation rate is a recipe parameter and not a convenience.

There is a hard ceiling on how much dopant can be added, and it comes from the same pile-up. The **liquidus** is the freezing temperature of the liquid at a given composition; dissolved atoms lower it, as salt lowers water's freezing point, and **m**, the **liquidus slope**, is how many kelvin the freezing point drops per unit of dissolved concentration. For a dilute solution, school thermodynamics gives it from numbers already in this module: m ≈ (R T_m² / ΔH_f) × (1 − k), with R the gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)), T_m = 1,687 K the melting point and ΔH_f ≈ 50 kJ/mol the latent heat per mole, so R T_m² / ΔH_f ≈ 470 K per unit of **atomic fraction** (the fraction of all atoms that are dopant; 1 % is 0.01). Boron (k = 0.8) therefore has m ≈ 470 × 0.2 ≈ 90 K per unit fraction, i.e. ~1 K per atomic percent, and arsenic (k = 0.3) m ≈ 330 K per unit fraction, ~3 K per atomic percent (measured Si-B and Si-As phase diagrams are steeper than this ideal-solution estimate, by several times (~), which only tightens what follows). Now picture walking away from the interface into the melt. The real temperature climbs at G_L, the temperature gradient in the liquid. The freezing point also climbs: it is most depressed right at the interface, where the rejected dopant is piled thickest, and recovers to its bulk value one boundary layer away. If the freezing point climbs faster than the real temperature, there is a layer of liquid just ahead of the front that is below its own freezing point: **constitutional supercooling**. The criterion for avoiding it is

G_L / v > m C₀ (1 − k) / (k D)

with C₀ the bulk melt concentration and D the dopant's diffusivity in the liquid, as in the BPS formula. Read it in words: the real temperature must climb into the melt (G_L) faster than the freezing point recovers from the pile-up, and the pile-up is steeper for more dopant and more rejection (C₀(1 − k)/k) and for faster growth with less time to diffuse away (v/D), so heavy doping and fast pulling both break the rule.

> **Worked example: is a normal boron run anywhere near the limit?**
> Both sides of the criterion have units of K·s/cm². Left side: take G_L ≈ 5 K/cm in the liquid at the interface (a few K/cm is typical, and the worked example in step 7 shows why it cannot be much larger) and v = 1 mm/min = 1.67 × 10⁻³ cm/s, so G_L / v ≈ 3,000 K·s/cm². Right side for 10 Ω·cm boron: C₀ ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ is an atomic fraction of 1.7 × 10¹⁵ / (5 × 10²²) ≈ 3.4 × 10⁻⁸ (34 ppba), so m C₀ ≈ 90 × 3.4 × 10⁻⁸ ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁶ K; multiply by (1 − k)/k = 0.25 and divide by D ≈ 10⁻⁴ cm²/s: ≈ 0.008 K·s/cm². The run is nowhere near the limit, by a factor of ~4 × 10⁵. Now an arsenic substrate at 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in the solid, which with k = 0.3 needs C₀ ≈ 3.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in the melt, an atomic fraction of 6.7 × 10⁻⁴: m C₀ ≈ 330 × 6.7 × 10⁻⁴ ≈ 0.22 K, times (1 − k)/k = 2.3, over D: ≈ 5,000 K·s/cm². That exceeds the 3,000 available at 1 mm/min, so the front would break down; halving the pull rate to 0.5 mm/min doubles G_L / v to ~6,000 and restores a thin margin, and every further factor of ten in concentration would need another factor of ten in G_L / v. That is the whole reason arsenic and antimony substrates stop at ~10¹⁹ to low-10²⁰ cm⁻³ and are pulled slowly: the eighth decimal place of boron is free, the fourth decimal place of arsenic is not (all figures ~; m itself is an order-of-magnitude estimate, and a hotter melt with a larger G_L buys back some margin).

> **What can go wrong:** When constitutional supercooling sets in, the flat freezing front is no longer stable: any bump reaches into liquid that is colder than its freezing point and grows faster, and the interface breaks into **cells**, a honeycomb of bumps with dopant trapped in the valleys between them. The crystal is scrap. It shows up as resistivity striping across a slice and as the loss of the habit lines that mark dislocation-free growth. The remedy is to pull slower, lower C₀, or both, which is why the criterion caps arsenic and antimony substrates at roughly 10¹⁹ to low-10²⁰ cm⁻³ (a few milliohm-centimetres) and forces the pull rate down for heavily doped runs.

> **Worked example: how much boron goes into a 400 kg charge for 10 Ω·cm p-type?**
> From the standard resistivity-versus-dopant tables (SEMI MF723, the Irvin curves), which every wafer maker uses to convert between the two, 10 Ω·cm p-type corresponds to N_B ≈ 1.35 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ in the solid. With k = 0.8 the melt must start at C₀ = 1.35 × 10¹⁵ / 0.8 ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. The charge is 400 kg; using the solid density 2.33 g/cm³ for a volume that refers to the crystal it will become, that is 1.72 × 10⁵ cm³ of silicon. Boron atoms needed: 1.7 × 10¹⁵ × 1.72 × 10⁵ ≈ 2.9 × 10²⁰ atoms, which is 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ mol, or **about 5 milligrams of boron** in 400 kg of silicon: 13 parts per billion by weight. Nobody weighs 5 mg of elemental boron; it is added as a few grams of silicon-boron alloy pellets of known concentration, or the melt is set with a heavily doped "remelt" piece from a previous ingot. Now apply Scheil: at g = 0.5 the solid concentration is 1.35 × 10¹⁵ × 0.5^(−0.2) = 1.55 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ (about 8.7 Ω·cm); at g = 0.9 it is 1.35 × 10¹⁵ × 0.1^(−0.2) = 2.1 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ (about 6.4 Ω·cm). So a boron ingot targeted at 10 Ω·cm at the seed end drifts to ~6.5 Ω·cm near the tail; a customer spec of 8 to 12 Ω·cm (8 Ω·cm ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³, reached when (1 − g)^(−0.2) = 1.26, i.e. g ≈ 0.68) would take only the first ~65 to 70 % of the body, while a spec of 5 to 15 Ω·cm takes it all. Repeat with phosphorus (k = 0.35): at g = 0.9 the factor is 0.1^(−0.65) = 4.5, so a 10 Ω·cm n-type seed end ends at ~2.2 Ω·cm.

### 4. Seed dip and thermal equilibration

The **seed** is a small dislocation-free <100> single crystal, on the order of 10 to 20 mm across and 100 to 200 mm long, cut from a previous ingot and etched clean; it is clamped in a graphite or molybdenum seed holder on the cable. It is lowered to just above the melt and held there for tens of minutes to warm up, then dipped a few millimetres into the melt. Where liquid meets the seed it climbs up to meet it, like water climbing the wall of a glass: this curved skirt of liquid is the **meniscus**, visible on the camera as a bright ring. The melt temperature is fine-tuned by watching it: too hot and the seed melts back and thins; too cold and it grows a bulge with **facets** (flat crystal faces that appear when growth is slow enough for atoms to complete whole {111} planes). The operator (or the automation) waits until a stable meniscus forms and the seed is neither growing nor shrinking. Even so, the thermal shock of dipping a ~1,000 °C piece of silicon into a 1,420 °C melt always generates dislocations in the seed. They are removed in the next step.

### 5. The Dash neck

> **Intuition:** A dislocation is a wrinkle in a carpet. The carpet (the lattice) is perfect on both sides of the wrinkle, and the wrinkle slides easily along the plane of the carpet when pushed; that sliding is called **glide**. In silicon the carpet planes are the {111} planes, which are tilted ~35° to the pull axis, so a wrinkle that keeps sliding along one of them runs out of the side of a thin rod within a few rod-diameters. The thin neck gives a dislocation a short route to a free surface, where the line defect can end. It is a geometric filter, not a sieve that removes foreign atoms: this step repairs the continuity of the crystal, while feedstock purification controlled its chemistry.

Dash's insight was that geometry. Dislocations in silicon lie on and glide along {111} planes, which make an angle of ~35.3° with a <100> growth axis (equivalently, the perpendicular to a {111} plane is inclined at ~54.7° to the axis). If the crystal is grown thin enough and fast enough, every dislocation inclined to the axis will reach the free surface and glide out before the crystal has grown far enough to carry it along. So immediately after the dip, the pull rate is raised to several millimetres per minute (on the order of 3 to 6 mm/min, i.e. a few hundred millimetres per hour) while the melt temperature is raised, and the crystal is pulled down to a **neck** of roughly 3 to 4 mm diameter over a length of 100 to 200 mm. The high pull rate also matters for a second reason: at high growth speed the crystal is supersaturated in **vacancies** (missing atoms, treated fully in the point-defect section), and vacancies help dislocations **climb**, that is, step off their glide plane by absorbing vacancies, which is another way out to the surface. After a few tens of millimetres of neck, the crystal is dislocation-free, and remains so as long as nothing (a thermal shock, a particle, a mechanical jolt) nucleates a new one. The Dash neck is the reason the entire modern silicon industry works, and it is also the origin of the heavy-crystal problem discussed later.

The confirmation that the crystal is dislocation-free is visible to the naked eye. A dislocation-free <100> ingot grows with four **habit lines** (growth ridges) running along its length at 90° intervals, where {111} facets at the growth front intersect the surface. If a dislocation enters, the facets vanish within a few millimetres and the lines disappear.

> **What can go wrong:** **Structure loss** (also called "loss of zero-D", i.e. of the zero-dislocation state, or "dislocation slip") is the moment a dislocation enters a dislocation-free crystal. Detection is immediate and low-tech: the four habit lines vanish on the camera image, and image-processing software watches for exactly that. Once in, the dislocation multiplies and glides back up into the already-grown crystal by about one diameter (the **slip-back** distance), so the damage is not confined to the point of entry. The fix is to lower the crystal and melt it back to above the entry point plus the slip-back distance, then regrow; if the loss happened in the neck or crown, the seed is withdrawn and the dip, neck and crown sequence is repeated. The cost is hours of growth remelted and regrown, and a melt whose dopant and oxygen history has been disturbed (the remelted silicon carries its dopant back in, while the wetted crucible area and argon exposure have moved on). Structure loss part-way down a 300 kg body is a bad day; twice in one run is usually a scrapped ingot.

### 6. Shoulder (crown)

Once the neck is long enough the pull rate is dropped sharply from the neck's several millimetres per minute and the melt is cooled slightly so the crystal flares out. The **crown** or **shoulder** grows from the ~4 mm neck to the full ~306 mm body diameter over a height of some tens of millimetres, which for a radius of ~150 mm means a surface sloping at roughly 10 to 30° from the horizontal (~); at crown pull rates the flare takes on the order of an hour or two (~), and the finished crown weighs perhaps 5 to 10 kg (~). The crown is scrap (it cannot be sliced into full-diameter wafers), so a flat crown tempts the operator: less silicon wasted, less time. The cost is that a flat crown is where {111} facets, the flat faces that appear when growth slows, develop across the top, and the sudden change in thermal conditions at a sharp transition is a classic trigger for dislocations. The crown is therefore grown at a compromise angle, and the transition from crown to body, where the diameter is held and the crystal's outline turns from the crown's slope to vertical, is watched as another classic point of structure loss. The crown is also where the four habit lines first become visible, as ridges radiating from the neck at 90° intervals, so the camera shows within minutes whether the flare has stayed dislocation-free.

> **What can go wrong:** A crown grown too flat and too slowly develops large {111} facets across its top, visible on the camera as bright flat patches; the change in growth conditions at the facet edges is where dislocations nucleate, and the habit lines then fail to appear or vanish. A crown grown too steep wastes silicon and hours, and if the diameter overshoots the body target it must be pulled back in, which is another thermal transient at the worst place. The turn from crown to body is watched for the same signs. In any of these cases the crown is melted back to the neck and regrown at a cost of an hour or two, cheap compared with structure loss in the body, and a run that needs three or four attempts at the crown usually has a melt-temperature or hot-zone problem to find first.

### 7. Body growth: the long, quiet part

The body is grown at a constant diameter, slightly larger than the finished wafer (on the order of 305 to 310 mm for 300 mm wafers, since the ingot will be ground to size), for a length of ~1.8 to 2.5 m, at a pull rate of roughly **0.5 to 1.5 mm/min** with the lower end of that range typical for 300 mm and heavily doped runs. Here is what the machine is doing during those 30 to 50 hours:

**Heat balance.** At the interface, heat arrives from the melt (superheat carried by convection) plus the latent heat released by solidification, and leaves by conduction up the crystal and radiation from its surface. Growth rate is set by

v ρ L = k_s G_s − k_l G_l

where v is the growth rate, ρ the density of solid silicon, L the latent heat per kilogram, k_s and k_l the thermal conductivities of solid and liquid silicon (~22 and ~60 W/(m·K) near the melting point), and G_s and G_l the temperature gradients in the crystal and the melt at the interface. It means: the crystal can only grow as fast as it can conduct the released latent heat up and away, and any superheat arriving from the melt subtracts from that. Heater power sets the melt-side term; the hot zone (shields, reflectors, radiation to the cold chamber) sets the crystal-side gradient G_s, on the order of 20 to 40 K/cm for 300 mm and unavoidably larger at the edge (which radiates) than at the centre. This radial variation of G is the central problem of defect control.

> **Worked example: the maximum pull rate.**
> Take G_s = 30 K/cm = 3,000 K/m and k_s ≈ 22 W/(m·K): the crystal can conduct away k_s G_s ≈ 22 × 3,000 ≈ 66 kW per square metre of interface. Freezing at v = 1 mm/min releases ρ L v = 2,330 kg/m³ × 1.79 × 10⁶ J/kg × (0.001 / 60) m/s ≈ 69 kW/m². So with a cold melt (G_l ≈ 0) the crystal can grow at most ~66 / 69 ≈ 0.95 mm/min at this gradient, and any superheat from the melt side lowers it further: with k_l ≈ 60 W/(m·K), even a 2 K/cm gradient in the liquid subtracts ~12 kW/m², a sixth of the budget. That is the physical origin of the ~1 mm/min ceiling, and of why 300 mm pulls slower than 200 mm: heat from the core of a fatter crystal has 150 mm to travel to the surface instead of 100 mm, so the achievable G is lower and v_max falls with it (all figures ~).

Give G its meaning: at 30 K/cm the crystal cools 3 °C for every millimetre above the melt. Higher up, the gradient relaxes (to perhaps ~10 to 20 K/cm), so the 1,100 to 1,000 °C window in which point defects cluster (next section) is a band ~50 to 100 mm long, and at typical pull rates a given slice of crystal spends ~1 to 2 h in it. Everything about the crystal's defects is decided in those two hours.

**Rotation.** The crystal rotates at ~10 to 20 rpm; the crucible counter-rotates at ~5 to 15 rpm. Crystal rotation averages out **azimuthal** (around-the-circumference) asymmetry of the thermal field, which would otherwise grow an oval or bent crystal; it also drives a **centrifugal pumping** flow under the interface: the spinning crystal drags the melt round and flings it outward, pulling fresh melt up from below like a spinning disc in a bowl of water, and that flow sets the thickness of the solute boundary layer and hence k_eff, and flattens the interface. Crucible rotation homogenises the dopant and carries hot, oxygen-rich melt from the wall toward the centre, so more crucible rotation means more oxygen in the crystal: one of the main ways oxygen is controlled.

**Crucible lift.** As silicon is withdrawn the melt level would fall and the interface would descend into a different thermal environment, so the crucible is raised at a rate matched to the volume removed: lift = v × (r_crystal / r_melt)² × (ρ_s / ρ_l), where r_crystal and r_melt are the radii of crystal and melt surface and ρ_s / ρ_l is the solid-to-liquid density ratio. Holding the melt surface fixed relative to the heater and heat shield is what keeps G constant along the body.

> **Worked example: the crucible lift rate.**
> Pull rate 0.8 mm/min, crystal radius 153 mm, melt radius ~395 mm (a 32-inch crucible less its wall), density ratio 2.33 / 2.57 ≈ 0.91. (153 / 395)² ≈ 0.15, so lift ≈ 0.8 × 0.15 × 0.91 ≈ 0.1 mm/min: about an eighth of the pull rate. Over a 40 h body the crucible rises ~250 mm, roughly the depth of melt consumed, which is why the crucible shaft needs as much travel as the melt is deep.

**Diameter control.** A CCD camera looks down through a viewport at the meniscus, the bright ring where the liquid climbs to meet the crystal and reflects the hot crucible wall. Liquid silicon meets the solid at a fixed **growth angle** of 11°: at the line where liquid, solid and gas meet, the liquid surface leaves the crystal's vertical side wall tilted 11° outward from it, a property of the material like a contact angle, not something the machine sets. So the meniscus is not free to take any shape. For the crystal to keep a constant diameter, the liquid must leave the wall at exactly that angle and curve down to the flat melt surface, which fixes one particular meniscus height and width (about 7 mm tall for silicon, ~); a meniscus taller than that means the crystal is narrowing, a shorter one that it is widening. If the diameter starts to drift, therefore, the bright ring changes width, and that width is what the camera measures, continuously and to well under a millimetre. The **ADC** loop adjusts two actuators with very different time constants: pull rate for fast corrections (pull faster and the crystal narrows within minutes) and heater power for slow drift (tens of minutes, given the thermal mass of 400 kg of melt and a tonne of graphite). Older pullers instead weigh the crystal with a load cell and compute the growth rate from the rate of weight change.

**Growth striations.** The melt below the interface is not still. The **Grashof number** is the ratio of the buoyancy lifting hot melt to the viscosity resisting it; above ~10⁹ the flow is turbulent, a pot at a rolling simmer, and 400 kg of silicon at 1,420 °C (Grashof number above 10¹⁰) is far past that. On top of the turbulence, the crystal rotating through a slightly asymmetric thermal field sees a temperature oscillation once per revolution. Both make the microscopic growth rate fluctuate, even momentarily remelting. Because k_eff depends on growth rate, dopant and oxygen end up modulated in thin bands, **striations**, visible in an etched cross-section like tree rings: resistivity variations of a few percent, and the main reason magnetic fields are applied.

### 8. Tail (end cone)

When the melt is nearly consumed (some 5 to 10 % is normally left in the crucible; pulling to dryness changes the thermal field too much and the last melt is enriched in every k < 1 impurity), the crystal must be separated from the melt without a shock. So the pull rate is raised and the temperature increased to taper the crystal to a point over a **tail** or **end cone** of roughly 150 to 300 mm, at which point the contact area is small enough to detach harmlessly. Like the crown, the tail is scrap, and the operators' incentive is to make it as short as dislocation-free growth allows.

> **What can go wrong:** If a 300 mm interface is simply lifted off the melt, the abrupt loss of latent-heat input sends a thermal stress wave through the bottom of the crystal, dislocations nucleate at the bottom face, and they glide back up into the body a distance on the order of one diameter: **slip-back**, which scraps the last ~300 mm of an otherwise perfect ingot. It is detected after the fact, by etching the **slug** (a thick test disc) cut from the tail end and looking for dislocation etch pits. The remedy is a longer, slower tail and a gentler separation, traded against the silicon and hours the tail costs.

### 9. Cool-down and extraction

The crystal is raised into the pull chamber, the heater is ramped down over an hour or more, and the ingot cools for several hours in argon before the isolation valve is closed, the pull chamber vented, and the crystal lifted out with a hoist. Both the argon and the hours have reasons. The argon stays because the graphite hot zone, still above 1,000 °C, would burn in air, and a hot ingot exposed to air would grow an oxide skin. The cooling is slow because a 300 kg crystal that loses heat from its surface faster than heat can conduct out of its core develops a stress between its shrinking skin and its still-hot interior, and below the **brittle-to-ductile transition** (~600 to 700 °C for silicon, ~; above it the lattice yields by dislocation glide, below it it fractures) that stress cracks the ingot rather than deforming it. So the surface temperature is brought down at no more than a few kelvin per minute (~) and the ingot is not exposed to air until it is below a few hundred degrees. The crucible, now cracked and coated in solidified residual melt, is pulled and scrapped; the hot zone is inspected and cleaned of SiO; a new crucible is charged. This turnaround adds several hours to about half a day (~) to the cycle, which is why 30 to 50 h of growth become a 2.5 to 3.5 day cycle. Grown ingots are then measured and either shipped to the wafer line or, for structure-loss or out-of-spec sections, cut up and returned as remelt charge. Four instruments do the measuring. Resistivity at the ends is read with a **four-point probe**: four needles in a line, current through the outer pair, voltage across the inner pair. Oxygen and carbon are read by **FTIR** (Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy), which measures infrared absorption at the wavelengths where Si-O and Si-C bonds vibrate. The dislocation-free check uses **X-ray topography**, which images the strain field around dislocations, or a **preferential etch** (Secco or Wright etch), which pits each dislocation so it can be counted. Diameter and length are simply measured.

## Point Defects, v/G, and the Voronkov Theory

The most consequential physics in the module is not about dislocations at all. A dislocation-free CZ crystal is still full of **intrinsic point defects**: **vacancies** (missing atoms) and **self-interstitials** (extra silicon atoms squeezed between lattice sites). Both are present at the melting point at equilibrium concentrations around 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ and both are frozen in as the crystal cools. What they do next determines whether the wafer is usable for a 2 nm gate oxide.

> **Intuition:** Think of the growth interface as a reservoir that keeps both species topped up at their equilibrium levels, and the crystal above it as a conveyor belt moving away at speed v. Defects leave the reservoir two ways: carried along on the belt (advection, proportional to v, the leaf on the river), and by diffusing away from the interface into the cooler crystal, down the concentration slope that the temperature gradient G creates (the equilibrium concentration falls steeply as the crystal cools, so there is always a downhill path away from the interface). Interstitials diffuse faster; vacancies are slightly more numerous. Whether the belt or the diffusion dominates is the whole question, and the answer turns out to be the ratio of belt speed to temperature gradient.

**Vladimir Voronkov** (1982) showed that which species survives is governed by that single ratio: the growth rate v divided by the axial temperature gradient at the interface G. Here v says how quickly new crystal moves away from the melt; G says how much its temperature changes per unit distance along that direction. Increasing v and increasing G therefore push the ratio in opposite directions. G is a spatial temperature gradient, not the number of degrees the furnace cools per minute. Made precise, the flux picture is this. Each species leaves the interface with a flux equal to (its concentration × v), the advection, plus (its diffusivity × the concentration gradient that G sets up), the diffusion; both terms point the same way, away from the interface into the crystal. Just behind the interface the two species annihilate each other in pairs (a vacancy plus an interstitial is a perfect lattice), so what survives is whichever arrived in excess:

- At **high v/G**, the advection term dominates for both species, and the vacancies, the more numerous at the melting point, arrive in excess; they consume the interstitials and the crystal becomes **vacancy-rich** (labelled V-type).
- At **low v/G**, the diffusion term dominates, and the interstitials, which diffuse faster, arrive in excess; they consume the vacancies and the crystal becomes **interstitial-rich** (I-type).
- The crossover is at a critical value **(v/G)_crit ≈ 1.3 to 2 × 10⁻³ cm²/(min·K)**, i.e. ~0.13 to 0.2 mm²/(min·K). With G ≈ 3 K/mm, the critical pull rate is ~0.4 to 0.6 mm/min.

Because G is higher at the crystal edge than at the centre, the same pull rate can give V-type at the centre and I-type at the rim.

> **Worked example: one pull rate, two regimes on one wafer.**
> Suppose a hot zone gives G = 25 K/cm at the crystal centre and 35 K/cm at the edge (the edge radiates, so it always cools faster), and take (v/G)_crit = 1.5 × 10⁻³ cm²/(min·K), an illustrative midpoint of the published range. The critical pull rate is v_crit = (v/G)_crit × G: at the centre 1.5 × 10⁻³ × 25 = 0.0375 cm/min ≈ 0.38 mm/min; at the edge 1.5 × 10⁻³ × 35 ≈ 0.53 mm/min. Pull at 0.45 mm/min and the centre (v/G = 1.8 × 10⁻³, above critical) is vacancy-type while the rim (1.3 × 10⁻³, below critical) is interstitial-type; the boundary sits where G = 0.045 / (1.5 × 10⁻³) = 30 K/cm, and that is where the OSF ring described next appears. Pull faster than 0.53 mm/min and the whole wafer is vacancy-type; slower than 0.38 mm/min and it is all interstitial-type. In this hot zone, then, the 0.38 to 0.53 mm/min band is where the ring sits somewhere on the wafer, and for every 10 K/cm that G changes across the radius, the pull rate that puts the ring at that radius shifts by ~0.15 mm/min. Perfect-silicon growth (below) needs the opposite: a hot zone that flattens G so that centre and edge share nearly the same critical pull rate, at which point the usable window collapses to a few percent around v_crit, and a 3 % pull-rate error (~0.013 mm/min here) is the whole margin.

The boundary between the two regimes is decorated by a ring of **oxidation-induced stacking faults (OSF ring)**. A **stacking fault** is an error in the stacking order of the atomic planes, a two-dimensional defect: the regular ...ABCABC... sequence of {111} planes gains an extra plane (...ABC B ABC...) or loses one. Oxidising a wafer injects interstitials into the silicon beneath the oxide, and where oxygen-precipitate nuclei already exist, those interstitials condense around them into stacking faults. Such nuclei exist only in a narrow band at the V/I boundary, because a modest excess of vacancies, too few to form voids, helps oxygen precipitate, and that condition holds only just inside the vacancy side of the boundary (the P_v band described below). So the ring is invisible on an as-polished wafer and appears after an oxidation test. As pull rate rises the ring moves outward and disappears off the edge (fully V-type); as it falls the ring shrinks to the centre (fully I-type).

Each regime has its own signature defect, formed as the crystal cools through ~1,100 to 1,000 °C where the supersaturated point defects **agglomerate** (cluster together):

- **V-type: COPs.** Excess vacancies condense into octahedral **voids**, roughly 100 to 200 nm across, bounded by {111} faces, with a ~2 nm oxide lining on the inner walls, at densities of ~10⁵ to 10⁶ cm⁻³. When a wafer is polished and then cleaned in **SC-1** (the ammonia-peroxide step of the **RCA clean**, the standard wet-cleaning sequence of Module 05, which etches silicon slowly), the voids intersecting the surface open up into pits, and laser surface scanners count them as particles. Hence the name **crystal-originated particles (COPs)**, coined in the early 1990s when engineers realised the "particles" that would not wash off were coming from the crystal. A COP under a gate oxide is a **gate oxide integrity (GOI)** failure: the oxide thins over the pit and breaks down early. In the 1990s, when gate oxides were 5 to 10 nm, COPs became the dominant substrate quality problem.
- **I-type: large dislocation loops (L-pits).** Excess interstitials condense into dislocation loops and networks, historically called "swirl" defects from the pattern they make in an etched slice, now classed as L-pits or large dislocation pits. These are worse than voids: a dislocation loop in the active region is a leakage path, and they cannot be annealed out.

**How engineers see it.** Three tests map a wafer's regime. A **laser surface scanner** after an SC-1 clean counts COPs as light-scattering pits, so a COP map is a vacancy map. A preferential etch, or **copper decoration** (copper plated onto the wafer diffuses to and highlights extended defects), reveals dislocation loops as large pits. And a deliberate oxidation followed by an etch reveals the OSF ring. Every hot zone is characterised this way, wafer by wafer along a test ingot, before it is used for product.

> **What can go wrong:** The perfect-silicon window is a few percent wide in pull rate, and G is not a constant: heat shields warp, graphite converts to silicon carbide, insulation densifies, and after enough runs an aged hot zone has a different radial G profile from the one it was mapped with. A few-percent pull-rate error or an aged part moves one radial band of the crystal into the COP or loop regime. The wafer maker's response is to re-map G after every hot-zone rebuild by growing and etching a test ingot, and to sort: sections of an ingot that fall out of the window are downgraded to products where the defects do not matter, epi wafers (the defects are buried) or annealed wafers (the voids are closed).

Three strategies deal with COPs, and the industry uses all three:

1. **Epitaxy.** Deposit a few micrometres of defect-free silicon on top (Module 03); the voids are buried and the device sees a perfect surface. Standard for logic epi wafers.
2. **High-temperature annealing.** An argon or hydrogen anneal at ~1,200 °C, for on the order of an hour (~) in a vertical batch furnace of the kind described in Module 06, first dissolves the ~2 nm oxide lining of each void: at that temperature the silicon can take the oxygen back into solution, and near the surface it also diffuses out. Once the lining is gone the void walls are bare silicon, and at 1,200 °C silicon atoms migrate freely over those walls while interstitials flow in from the nearby free surface, so each void shrinks and the lattice heals from the outside in. That is why only the top few micrometres are cured, and why that is enough: the device lives in the top micrometre, and the buried voids do it no harm. These are **annealed wafers**, used heavily in **DRAM** (the working-memory chips of Module 15), where the wafer must be cheaper than an epi wafer and a few micrometres of perfect surface suffice. Nitrogen-doped crystals suit the anneal best, because their many small voids close faster than a few large ones. What can go wrong: 1,200 °C is deep in the regime where a wafer slips on its furnace supports (see the Oxygen section), so ramp rates and boat design are tuned to keep the stress below the yield stress; any metal contamination in the furnace diffuses through the whole wafer in that hour; and an anneal that is too short leaves voids only partly closed and the gate-oxide failure rate only partly reduced.
3. **Grow without them: "perfect silicon."** Hold v/G near critical across the whole radius so neither species is supersaturated enough to agglomerate. This needs a hot zone (heat shields, cooling jackets around the crystal) engineered to flatten the radial profile of G, and a pull rate that is slow and controlled to within a few percent. The window is narrow: on the V side a "P_v" band where vacancies exist but are too few to form voids (they seed oxygen precipitates instead, usefully), and on the I side a "P_i" band with interstitials but no loops. Growing a 2 m body entirely inside that window is what MEMC (now GlobalWafers) commercialised as "Perfect Silicon"; Shin-Etsu, SUMCO and Siltronic have their own versions. **Nitrogen doping** at ~10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ helps: nitrogen binds vacancies, raises the void nucleation temperature, and yields many small voids instead of few large ones, which an anneal or epi removes. Faster cooling through the ~1,100 °C nucleation range does the same.

The economic point: defect-free growth means a pull rate of perhaps 0.4 to 0.6 mm/min instead of ~1 mm/min, so a puller makes fewer wafers per day, and the choice between polished-perfect, epi and annealed wafers is a cost and application decision made customer by customer.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The vacancy-or-interstitial decision, made in the two hours each slice of crystal spends between 1,100 and 1,000 °C, fixes which product the fab can buy from that ingot. A COP that reaches the surface is a gate-oxide integrity failure in Module 11 and a "particle" that never washes off in Module 13's inspection data; an interstitial loop is a leakage path no anneal removes. The P_v band's vacancy profile also programs where oxygen will precipitate in Module 06's furnaces, which is the gettering promise of the next section. When a fab specifies "epi" or "annealed" or "polished, COP-free", it is choosing, without saying so, which of the three answers to v/G it is paying for.

## Oxygen and Carbon

### Where the oxygen comes from

The melt dissolves its crucible. SiO₂ + Si → 2 SiO: the reaction at the wall puts oxygen into the melt at concentrations near 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. Melt convection carries it to the free surface, where roughly 99 % of it evaporates as SiO gas and is swept out by the argon. The ~1 % that instead reaches the crystal interface is incorporated, with k ≈ 1.25, as **interstitial oxygen**: an oxygen atom wedged between two silicon atoms and bonded to both. Concentrations in CZ silicon are about **10 to 18 ppma** (1 ppma = 5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, so 5 × 10¹⁷ to 9 × 10¹⁷ cm⁻³), with a typical logic spec around 10 to 14 ppma and a tolerance of a few ppma along the whole length. Oxygen content is set by a balance of wall dissolution (crucible rotation, melt temperature, wetted area, which shrinks as the melt is consumed) against surface evaporation (argon pressure and flow, free surface area) and transport (convection, which magnets damp). Because the wetted area falls over the run, oxygen tends to fall from seed to tail even though k > 1, and recipes compensate by adjusting crucible rotation and argon pressure along the body.

> **Worked example: how much crucible ends up in the crystal?**
> The melt wets ~1 to 1.5 m² of crucible wall at the start of a 300 mm run (the bottom plus ~0.3 m of side wall in a 32-inch crucible), and over the run it dissolves ~0.3 to 0.5 mm of that wall (~10 µm/h, Module 01). That is ~0.4 to 0.7 litres of silica, at 2.2 g/cm³ roughly 1 kg of SiO₂, of which oxygen is 32/60 of the mass: ~0.5 kg, or ~30 mol of oxygen atoms. The 400 kg charge is 400,000 / 28.09 ≈ 14,200 mol of silicon. If every oxygen atom stayed, the crystal would hold 30 / 14,200 ≈ 2 × 10⁻³, i.e. ~2,000 ppma, a hundred times the real value. It does not stay: ~99 % leaves the free surface as SiO gas, so ~1 % remains, ~20 ppma, and a slightly higher escape fraction gives the observed 10 to 18 ppma. The retained amount is tiny in absolute terms: the 2.0 m, 343 kg body computed in the Ingot section below, at 12 ppma, holds 12 × 10⁻⁶ × (343,000 / 28.09) ≈ 0.15 mol of oxygen, about 2 to 3 g, the oxygen from roughly half a teaspoon of silica. The entire oxygen chemistry of a 300 kg crystal turns on the fate of the ~1 % of dissolved crucible that fails to escape (all figures ~).

**How oxygen is measured.** FTIR shines infrared light through a polished slice and looks for the absorption of the Si-O bond's vibration at a **wavenumber** of 1,107 cm⁻¹ (wavenumber is the infrared "colour", the number of wavelengths per centimetre; 1,107 cm⁻¹ is a wavelength of ~9 µm). The depth of that absorption dip is converted to ppma by a standard calibration factor (SEMI MF1188, the "new ASTM" calibration behind the 1 ppma = 5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ used here); older and regional calibrations (old ASTM, JEIDA, IOC-88) use different factors and give numbers that differ by tens of percent for the same wafer, so an oxygen spec always names its calibration. Oxygen is specified and measured per ingot position (seed end, several body positions, tail), because the value drifts along the body and the spec must hold everywhere the customer's wafers come from.

### Why oxygen is wanted, and where it is not

> **Intuition:** Sugar dissolved in hot tea crystallises out as the tea cools, because cold tea can hold less sugar. Oxygen dissolved in silicon at 1,420 °C is the sugar, and every furnace step in the fab, at 700 to 1,100 °C, is a cool-down: the silicon can hold far less oxygen at those temperatures than it took up from the melt, so the oxygen tries to come out of solution as tiny particles of SiO₂ wherever it can. The wafer maker's art is to decide where and when that happens.

Oxygen at ~10¹⁸ cm⁻³ is far above its solubility at device-processing temperatures, so during fab thermal cycles it precipitates as SiO₂ (more precisely SiO_x platelets and octahedra). That was a disaster in the 1970s (random precipitates in the device region) until it was turned into a feature:

- **Mechanical strength.** Picture a 300 mm wafer standing on three tiny quartz pins in a vertical furnace at 1,000 °C or more; at that temperature it sags under its own weight. If the stress exceeds the **yield stress**, the stress at which the crystal deforms permanently rather than springing back, the wafer **slips**: dislocations glide through it, it warps, and steps appear at the surface where whole planes of atoms have shifted. Dissolved oxygen pins dislocations and raises the yield stress, so a CZ wafer survives rapid thermal processing and the sag on three furnace pins without slipping; an oxygen-free Float Zone wafer frequently does not, which is a large part of why FZ stays at smaller diameters.
- **Intrinsic gettering (IG).** **Gettering** is deliberately trapping harmful metal atoms somewhere they do no damage: flypaper in the basement, below the living room. A sequence of a ~1,100 °C out-diffusion step (clearing oxygen from the top ~10 to 30 µm), nucleation at 650 to 800 °C and growth near 1,000 °C produces a precipitate-free **denuded zone** at the surface over a bulk dense with SiO₂ precipitates and the dislocation loops they **punch out** (a growing SiO₂ particle takes about twice the room of the silicon it replaces, and the squeeze pushes small dislocation loops out into the surrounding lattice), which together trap fast-diffusing Fe, Cu and Ni before they reach the junctions. MEMC's "magic denuded zone" (MDZ) uses a **rapid thermal anneal (RTA)**, seconds-long heating under lamps, to inject a vacancy profile that programs where precipitation happens. In modern logic flows the **thermal budget** (the total time-at-temperature a wafer sees in its whole process) is small, classic IG is less effective, and wafer makers instead engineer the vacancy profile during growth (the P_v band) or use epi.
- **Thermal donors.** Clusters of a few oxygen atoms, formed near 450 °C, each release two electrons: they behave like unintended phosphorus (**double donors**) and shift the resistivity. A 650 °C "donor kill" anneal breaks them up, and resistivity is specified after it.
- **Where it is unwanted.** Image sensors (precipitates are white pixels), power, RF and high-resistivity substrates all specify low oxygen, delivered by MCZ.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The 10 to 14 ppma written on a logic wafer spec is a promise to two later modules. To Module 06, it promises that the wafer will go through dozens of furnace and rapid-thermal steps without slip. To Modules 05 and 13, it promises a bulk that, after the right anneal sequence, will getter the iron, copper and nickel that every tool inevitably sheds, keeping the minority-carrier lifetime in the device layer high. A wafer with too little oxygen slips; one with too much, or with precipitates in the wrong place, leaks. Image sensors and power devices, which put their active volume deep in the wafer, want the opposite promise: no precipitates at all, hence low-oxygen MCZ or FZ.

> **What can go wrong:** *Oxygen drifting below spec toward the tail.* As the melt is consumed the wetted crucible area shrinks, dissolution falls, and the tail can drop under a 10 ppma lower limit; the recipe counters with ramps of crucible rotation (more oxygen-rich melt brought to the centre) and argon pressure (less SiO evaporation) along the body, and a tail that still falls short is sold to a lower-oxygen spec or remelted. *Precipitates in the device layer.* If the denuded zone fails, because the out-diffusion step was too short or the oxygen too high, SiO₂ precipitates form within the top micrometres and each one is a leakage site or a white pixel. *Carbon-nucleated precipitation.* Carbon in the crystal seeds oxygen precipitates at random, so a carbon excursion in the hot zone turns a controlled precipitate density into an uncontrolled one, visible as a wafer-to-wafer scatter in gettering and warpage.

### Carbon

This is a side note rather than a core process parameter: carbon is not added and not wanted, only kept down. Graphite hot-zone parts react with SiO to make CO, which dissolves in the melt and gives **substitutional carbon** (a carbon atom sitting on a silicon lattice site) in the crystal. With k = 0.07 it accumulates in the melt and rises toward the tail. Specs are typically below 0.1 to 0.5 ppma (5 × 10¹⁵ to 2.5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³), detected by FTIR at the Si-C absorption line at 605 cm⁻¹ (a wavelength of ~16.5 µm); the bottom of that range sits near the room-temperature FTIR detection limit (~10¹⁵ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, ~), so a tight carbon spec is often written simply as "below detection limit". Carbon matters mostly because it nucleates oxygen precipitation, making precipitate density uncontrollable (the carbon entry in the oxygen box above). Hot zones with **silicon carbide (SiC)**-coated graphite (a hard ceramic skin that does not react with SiO), CFC, and an argon purge routed to sweep CO out before it reaches the melt are how it is kept down; a carbon excursion is usually traced to a worn coating or a changed purge flow after a hot-zone rebuild.

## Magnetic Czochralski

The problem was set out under Growth striations in step 7: 400 kg of turbulent liquid metal under a 300 mm interface, every temperature flicker written into the crystal as a striation, and the same churning stripping oxygen off the crucible wall faster than a quiet melt would. At 200 mm the melts were small enough to live with it. At 300 mm they are not.

Liquid silicon is a metal with electrical conductivity around 10⁶ S/m (siemens per metre; copper is ~6 × 10⁷ S/m, so molten silicon conducts about as well as a poor metal alloy), and that is the handle. Follow the chain from school physics: a conductor moving through a magnetic field has currents induced in it; those currents feel a force that opposes the motion (Lenz's law). Every swirl in a melt sitting in a static field is a conductor moving through that field, so every swirl is braked. The melt behaves as if it were 10 to 100 times more viscous (~), which damps the turbulent buoyancy convection, quiets the temperature fluctuations behind striations, thickens the sluggish boundary layer at the crucible wall (less oxygen dissolved and transported), and stabilises the interface. Engineers quantify the braking with the **Hartmann number**, the ratio of magnetic to viscous friction, which for a 300 mm melt at a few tenths of a tesla runs into the thousands (~). Two geometries dominate:

- **Transverse (horizontal) MCZ**, typically 0.2 to 0.4 T across the melt, is the workhorse: strongest convection suppression and lowest oxygen. Its cost is symmetry. A horizontal field is not symmetric about the pull axis. The braking acts only on melt moving across the field lines, not on melt moving along them, and it is the same for a field pointing north as for one pointing south (it depends on the square of the field), so the flow pattern under the crystal is not one-sided but repeats every 180°: a two-fold, slightly oval pattern, damped across the field and freer along it. The rotating crystal sees that pattern sweep round relative to itself twice per turn. Rotation averages most of it out, but a faint two-fold pattern can survive in the oxygen and resistivity maps of a wafer.
- **Cusp MCZ** uses two coaxial coils with opposing currents. Picture their two fields meeting on the mid-plane between them and cancelling: the field is radial at the melt surface, axial at the crucible bottom and zero at the melt centre, at lower strength (~0.05 to 0.15 T at the wall). It damps the wall boundary layer while leaving the interface region nearly field-free, preserving the round symmetry of the process. Its oxygen reduction is intermediate: a cusp field typically trims oxygen by tens of percent rather than the roughly 50 % of a transverse field (~), and it is chosen where radial uniformity and a field-free interface matter more than the lowest possible oxygen.
- **Vertical (axial)** fields, a single coil around the furnace giving a few tenths of a tesla along the pull axis, are used least. An axial field brakes every radial motion of the melt, including the centrifugal pumping under the crystal that the rotation relies on for mixing, so the radial resistivity variation across a wafer worsens (from a few percent to many percent, ~), and because it also stills the flow that carries oxygen-rich melt to the free surface where SiO can evaporate, most reports find it raises the oxygen content rather than lowering it.

The machine is a set of superconducting coils inside a **cryostat** (a vacuum-insulated vessel holding the coils at a few kelvin) that surrounds the furnace chamber. Once charged, a superconducting coil holds its field with almost no power, which is why superconducting magnets displaced resistive copper coils that drew hundreds of kilowatts; the price is that the field takes hours to ramp up or down, so the magnet is a scheduled part of the run, not a switch. Older tools still carry resistive coils. The magnets come from Toshiba (~500 or more built since 1988), Sumitomo Heavy Industries, Mitsubishi Electric and JASTEC. As a quantitative handle, applying the field roughly halves the oxygen content compared with unmagnetised growth at the same crucible rotation (~), and it is how the ultra-low-oxygen 300 mm products for image sensors, power and RF are made.

> **What can go wrong:** A **quench**, the sudden loss of superconductivity when part of a coil warms above its critical temperature, dumps the field in seconds, the melt springs back into turbulence mid-run, and the crystal grown from that point carries striations and an oxygen step; the run is usually finished and the affected length downgraded. A *misaligned field* (coil axis off the pull axis by a few millimetres) shows as a radial variation in resistivity and oxygen across the wafer that no rotation averages out. And a field that is too strong in the wrong place, particularly a vertical component under the interface, *over-damps the mixing* that the rotating crystal relies on to keep the boundary layer uniform, and radial dopant uniformity suffers. Field strength, geometry and crystal rotation are therefore tuned together for each hot zone.

At 300 mm, with 400 kg melts far into the turbulent regime, MCZ is not optional: it is how large melts are kept quiet enough to grow defect-controlled crystals at all. Solar pullers, with looser oxygen specs, mostly skip magnets.

## The Ingot: Size, Mass, and Time

A finished 300 mm ingot has a body about 2 m long (the range runs from ~1.5 to over 2.5 m depending on charge and puller), weighs ~250 to 350 kg in the body alone, and carries a crown and tail of perhaps 20 to 40 kg between them. It has been in the puller for 30 to 50 hours of growth, plus meltdown, cool-down and turnaround, for a cycle of roughly two and a half to three and a half days.

> **Worked example: ingot mass and wafer count from geometry.**
> Body diameter as grown: 306 mm (radius 15.3 cm). Body length: 2.0 m. Volume = π × 15.3² × 200 = π × 234.1 × 200 ≈ 1.47 × 10⁵ cm³. Mass = 1.47 × 10⁵ cm³ × 2.33 g/cm³ ≈ **343 kg**. Add a crown and tail of perhaps 30 kg and ~30 to 40 kg of melt left in the crucible, and the charge had to be at least ~400 to 420 kg, which is exactly the 32 to 36 inch crucible class. How many wafers is that? A 300 mm wafer is 775 µm thick, the diamond-wire **kerf** (the width of ingot the saw wire turns to dust, Module 03) is ~150 to 170 µm, and grinding, slicing damage and edge losses add a bit more, so budget ~1.05 to 1.1 mm of ingot per wafer. 2,000 mm / 1.08 mm ≈ **1,850 wafers** gross from the body. Each finished wafer has mass π × 15² × 0.0775 × 2.33 ≈ 128 g, so 1,850 wafers carry 237 kg of silicon; the other ~105 kg of the body went to kerf, grinding **swarf** (grinding dust) and edge grinding. Silicon utilisation from charge to finished wafer is on the order of 55 to 60 % before any losses to structure loss, out-of-spec ends or wafer-line breakage. Cost intuition: at roughly $20 to 40 per kg for electronic-grade polysilicon the 400 kg charge cost ~$8,000 to 16,000, about $4 to 9 per gross wafer. The crucible, argon, electricity (a ~150 kW hot zone for ~50 hours is ~7 MWh, on the order of $500 to $1,000), depreciation on a multi-million-dollar puller and the wafering line that follows together cost far more than the silicon. A prime 300 mm polished wafer sells for roughly $100 to 200 and an epi wafer for more (as of 2025), so the crystal is a minority of the wafer's cost but a majority of its quality.

Volume context: world 300 mm demand as of ~2025 is on the order of 7 to 9 million wafers per month. **SEMI**, the industry's trade association, reports shipments as total wafer area: 12,973 million square inches in 2025, roughly three quarters of it 300 mm. At ~1,500 to 1,800 good wafers per ingot that is roughly 4,000 to 6,000 ingots per month. Wafer makers do not publish fleet sizes, but at ~8 to 12 ingots per puller per month the arithmetic implies several hundred 300 mm pullers worldwide (~350 to 750); the total fleet of semiconductor pullers across all diameters, including 200 mm and smaller tools, numbers in the low thousands.

## The Heavy-Crystal Problem

The whole ingot hangs from the Dash neck. A 3 mm neck has a cross-section of 7 mm². Three hundred kilograms on it is a tensile stress of about 400 MPa. For scale, 1 MPa is about 10 atmospheres, and 400 MPa is around the stress at which ordinary structural steel starts to yield. Dislocation-free silicon is intrinsically very strong: its **theoretical strength**, what a flawless lattice could carry before bonds break, is several GPa, and small defect-free whiskers approach that, but no real part with surface scratches ever does. A real neck has surface roughness, a slightly non-round section, thermal stress, and possibly dynamic loads from the cable and rotation, and necks have broken. When a neck breaks, a 300 kg red-hot crystal drops into a crucible of molten silicon. Puller designs accept that this will eventually happen and build for containment, but the run and probably the hot zone are lost. The neck is therefore monitored as closely as any part of the crystal: the camera records its diameter along its whole length, and the load cell watches for any change in how the weight is carried.

> **Worked example: neck stress versus diameter.**
> Stress σ = m g / (π r²). For m = 300 kg, g = 9.81 m/s²: load = 2,943 N. At 3 mm diameter (r = 1.5 mm): σ = 2,943 / (7.07 × 10⁻⁶ m²) ≈ 416 MPa. At 4 mm: 234 MPa. At 5 mm: 150 MPa. At 6 mm: 104 MPa. Practical design limits, with safety margin against surface flaws, are usually quoted at a couple of hundred MPa, which explains why the industry's comfort zone for a plain 3 to 4 mm neck tops out at roughly 200 to 300 kg, right where 300 mm ingots live, and why a 450 mm ingot (see below), with a body approaching a tonne, could never hang from a Dash neck at all.

Three solutions are in production use:

1. **Thicker necks, grown faster.** Dash necking still works at 5 to 6 mm if the pull rate is high enough, at the cost of time and a higher chance of failing to clear the dislocations; some recipes grow a thin section first and then expand to a thicker support section before the crown.
2. **Heavily boron-doped seeds.** Boron above ~10¹⁹ cm⁻³ hardens the lattice enough that the thermal shock of dipping does not generate dislocations, allowing dislocation-free growth from a large seed with no thin neck. Shin-Etsu pioneered this "neck-less" approach in the 1990s; it suits heavily boron-doped (**p+**) substrates best, but a doped seed can serve a lightly doped melt if the boron it carries in is tolerable.
3. **Mechanical crystal support.** After the crown is grown, a set of jaws or a split collar closes around it and carries the weight for the rest of the run. The jaws ride on a support that rotates with the crystal, either the seed shaft or cable assembly itself or a separate co-rotating spindle, so there is no sliding contact between jaw and crystal; their faces are heat-resistant (graphite or ceramic) and shaped to bear on a **ledge**, a short section grown a little wider than the body just under the crown, or on the underside of the crown itself, both of which are scrap anyway. The closing is slow and centred, and the load is transferred by degrees: the jaws take up the weight gradually as the crystal is pulled on, so the neck never sees a step change in tension and the crown never sees a knock, and once the transfer is complete the neck carries only the few kilograms above the ledge. SUMCO, Shin-Etsu, MEMC and others patented variants through the 1990s and 2000s, and they are standard on 300 mm pullers. What can go wrong: jaws that close off-centre or too fast deliver exactly the mechanical jolt the Dash section warned against, nucleating dislocations at the crown that slip back into the top of the body; jaws that do not run true put a wobble on the crystal and an oval into the diameter trace; and a ledge grown too thin cracks under a 300 kg load. The safeguards are a rehearsed, slow closing sequence and a ledge sized with the same margin as the neck arithmetic above.

## Yield: What Gets Thrown Away

From a 400 kg charge, the accounting looks like this in a typical, successful run:

- Residual melt left in the crucible (**pot scrap**, ~5 to 10 %), returned to the poly supplier or a lower-grade user.
- Crown, neck and tail: ~5 to 10 % of crystal mass, cut off and remelted.
- Resistivity out-of-spec ends (the last 10 to 20 % of an n-type body, less for boron): remelt or sold as a different spec.
- Dislocated sections from any structure-loss event: remelt.
- Oxygen out-of-spec sections: remelt or downgrade.

The body sold into the wafer line is therefore commonly 70 to 80 % of the charge on a good run and much less on a bad one. Structure loss is the big variable: a run that loses structure twice and recovers still yields, but its cycle time has grown by many hours, and one that loses structure late in a long body may be scrapped entirely. Growers track "dislocation-free yield" as a top-line metric; the exact figures are proprietary.

> **Worked example: from 410 kg of chunks to saleable wafers.**
> Take the ingot of the earlier example: a ~410 kg charge leaves ~35 kg of pot scrap in the crucible, ~30 kg in crown, neck and tail, and a 343 kg, 2.0 m body. A boron ingot sold to a wide 5 to 15 Ω·cm spec uses the whole body: 2,000 / 1.08 ≈ 1,850 gross slices, and after ~3 % breakage and out-of-spec loss in the wafer line (Module 03) about 1,800 saleable wafers, 230 kg of silicon out of 410 kg charged, ~56 %. A phosphorus ingot with a tight spec loses its last 10 to 20 % of body to Scheil enrichment: 1.6 to 1.8 m usable, ~1,480 to 1,670 gross, ~1,450 to 1,600 saleable; that is the 1,500 to 1,800 range quoted throughout the course. Now add one structure-loss event 1.2 m into the body. Dislocations slip back ~one diameter, so ~0.3 m of good crystal above the entry point is lost as well: ~280 wafers, worth ~$30,000 to 55,000 at 2025 polished-wafer prices, plus everything grown after the event. Stop the run there and the ingot yields ~0.9 m, ~830 wafers; the ~190 kg of melt still in the crucible plus the ~50 kg dislocated section go back to remelt. Melt back and regrow instead, and ~0.3 m of body is melted and grown twice at roughly the growth rate, ~10 to 15 h added to a 30 to 50 h run, with a disturbed dopant and oxygen history to sort through afterwards (all figures ~).

## Float Zone

### Why no crucible

Everything that limits CZ silicon traces back to the crucible: it is where the oxygen comes from, where most of the carbon path starts (the graphite that supports it), and, through the segregation of impurities it leaches, one reason CZ cannot reach very high resistivity. **Float Zone (FZ)** growth solves the crucible problem by not having one. Nothing but argon touches the molten silicon, so oxygen is below ~10¹⁶ cm⁻³ (two orders of magnitude below CZ), carbon is similarly low, and resistivity can reach 10⁴ Ω·cm and beyond, which corresponds to roughly one net dopant atom per 10¹¹ silicon atoms. The price is that a molten zone with no container has to be held up by something else.

> **Intuition:** Hang a drop of water from the tip of a needle: surface tension holds it against gravity, but only up to a size, and a bigger drop falls. A float zone is a short column of liquid silicon held between a solid rod above and a solid crystal below by nothing but its own surface tension, helped by the push of the coil's field. Surface tension can hold only so tall a column of liquid silicon against gravity, and that height, not the diameter, is what limits the process.

### How induction melts and holds silicon

The heat comes from **induction heating**: a single-turn, water-cooled copper **RF induction coil** carries an alternating current at ~2 to 3 MHz, which induces circulating **eddy currents** in the silicon inside it, and those currents heat the silicon by its own electrical resistance, the same physics as an induction hob. There is a catch that an induction hob does not have: cold silicon barely conducts, so the feed rod must be preheated (by a graphite ring or a lamp) before the coil can couple to it; once molten, silicon is a metal and couples strongly. The same induced currents are pushed away from the coil by its own field, and that **electromagnetic pressure** on the surface of the liquid helps hold the zone up and shape it. The molten zone is held by surface tension (silicon's is an unusually high 0.72 N/m) plus that field pressure, and is traversed from the seed end at the bottom to the top of the rod, leaving single crystal behind. The zone also refines: every impurity with k < 1 is rejected into the liquid and carried along with the zone, so it is swept upward to the last-frozen end of the rod, which is cut off. That is **zone refining**, and it is why FZ silicon is cleaner than its feed.

### The needle-eye machine and recipe

The dominant industrial form is the **needle-eye technique**. A polycrystalline feed rod (a Siemens rod from Module 01, ground round, typically 100 to 200 mm in diameter and crack-free, a demanding spec of its own) is held vertically above the coil; the coil's hole is smaller than both feed rod and crystal, molten silicon flows down through the "eye" onto the growing crystal below, and diameter is set by the balance of feed rate, growth rate and zone temperature rather than by a container. The seed is dipped into a melt drop at the bottom of the feed rod and Dash-necked exactly as in CZ; both crystal and feed rod rotate (in opposite senses, for round crystals and mixing); growth rates are ~2 to 4 mm/min, faster than CZ because the zone is small and the crystal cools quickly. The chamber runs in argon at slightly above atmospheric pressure to a few bar, to suppress **arcing** (electrical breakdown of the gas) from the multi-kilovolt coil. Diameter control is optical, a camera watching the zone and the meniscus below the coil. FZ furnaces come almost entirely from **PVA TePla** (FZ-35 and FZ-40 series).

> **Worked example: how tall can a molten zone be?**
> Heywang (1956) showed that a freely hanging molten zone is stable only up to a height set by surface tension γ, density ρ and gravity g: h_max ≈ 2.8 √(γ / (ρ g)). With silicon's γ = 0.72 N/m and liquid density 2,570 kg/m³, γ / (ρ g) = 0.72 / (2,570 × 9.81) ≈ 2.9 × 10⁻⁵ m², the square root is ~5.3 mm, and h_max ≈ 15 mm (~). Nothing in that formula depends on diameter. A 100 mm crystal is grown through a zone ~15 mm tall, and so is a 200 mm crystal: a thin liquid disc 200 mm across and 15 mm thick, with a whole crystal hanging off its underside. At 300 mm the disc is thinner still relative to its width, its surface tension has more liquid to restrain, and the needle-eye coil's field pressure is doing most of the work; that is why production stops at 200 mm.

### Doping and neutron transmutation

Doping is by gas: **phosphine (PH₃)** for n-type or **diborane (B₂H₆)** for p-type, added in trace amounts to the argon so that they decompose at the zone and enter the melt. For the most uniform n-type material, though, the dopant is made inside the finished crystal by **neutron transmutation doping (NTD)**: the ingot is irradiated in a research reactor, where ³⁰Si (3.1 % natural abundance) captures a neutron to become ³¹Si, which beta-decays (2.6-hour half-life) into ³¹P. Because the neutron flux penetrates the whole ingot uniformly, NTD gives radial resistivity variation under ~5 %, far better than any melt; reactors at Petten (Netherlands), OPAL (Australia) and elsewhere sell it as a service, and the ingot is annealed afterwards to repair the radiation damage.

> **What can go wrong:** The *zone can spill or collapse*: too much power or too fast a feed makes the liquid disc too tall or too wide for surface tension to hold, and molten silicon runs down the outside of the crystal or drops onto the coil, ending the run. *Arcing* between the coil and the rod, at multi-kilovolt RF and reduced pressure, burns the coil and the crystal; hence the over-pressure of argon. *Feed-rod cracks* (from stresses frozen into the Siemens rod) let a piece drop into the zone, a dislocation seed and often a spill. *Structure loss* at the neck or anywhere along the body is detected exactly as in CZ, by the habit lines, and remelting a float zone is easier than remelting a crucible crystal. And the *finished wafers are fragile*: with no oxygen to pin dislocations, an FZ wafer slips at furnace stresses a CZ wafer shrugs off, which is one reason fabs handle FZ material at smaller diameters and with gentler thermal ramps.

### Who makes it and who buys it

**Production FZ tops out at 200 mm** (as of 2025). Siltronic, which has offered 200 mm FZ since 2002, and Topsil, now part of GlobalWafers, are the volume producers, with Shin-Etsu also present; 300 mm FZ has been demonstrated in research but is not a commercial product as of ~2025. Topsil's FZ300 programme (2025 to 2028) aims to build a 300 mm FZ growth machine, and Siltronic serves 300 mm customers who need FZ-like low oxygen with an ultra-low-oxygen MCZ product instead. The feed rod is expensive, each ingot is small, and the oxygen-free crystal is mechanically fragile in the fab, so FZ wafers cost a multiple of CZ. The market is **power devices**: **insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs)**, gate-switched power transistors for hundreds to thousands of volts (a gate turns them on and off with almost no drive current, while a bipolar structure inside carries the large current), and diodes from 600 V to 6.5 kV and the **thyristors** (latching power switches) of high-voltage DC transmission, where high resistivity holds off voltage and low oxygen means no thermal donors or precipitates in a ~100 µm **drift region** (the thick, lightly doped layer of a power device that holds off the voltage); **radiation detectors** and photodiodes (long lifetime, low leakage); **RF and high-resistivity substrates**; and some **MEMS** (micro-electromechanical systems, the mechanical sensors etched out of silicon).

## 450 mm: The Diameter That Did Not Happen

The industry stepped 100 → 125 → 150 → 200 → 300 mm on a rough 10-year cadence, each step cutting cost per die by ~30 % via area scaling. The arithmetic is two lines: 450² / 300² = 2.25× the area and about 2.3× the dies per wafer (edge effects favour the bigger wafer slightly), while a tool for the bigger wafer typically costs ~1.3 to 1.5× as much and still processes one wafer per pass; cost per die therefore falls to roughly 1.4 / 2.25 ≈ 0.6 to 0.7 of the old value, the ~30 % saving, and it materialises only if every tool in the line is built for the bigger wafer at about that price, which is why the step needed every tool vendor to agree. 300 mm entered volume production around 2001 to 2002, and 450 mm was scheduled for the 2010s. For crystal growth it was demanding but tractable: SUMCO, Shin-Etsu, Siltronic and MEMC all grew 450 mm ingots in 40-inch-class crucibles with charges approaching a tonne, and shipped test wafers to the **Global 450mm Consortium (G450C)**, formed in 2011 by Intel, TSMC, Samsung, IBM and GlobalFoundries at SUNY Albany. A 450 mm ingot carries 2.25× the mass per unit length of a 300 mm one, so crystal support, melt convection and v/G control all scaled painfully, but defect-controlled 450 mm material was demonstrated.

What killed it was not the crystal. The tool makers, above all ASML, faced a full redevelopment of every process tool for a diameter only the three or four largest fabs could use, and in late 2013 ASML paused its 450 mm programme. Intel, the main sponsor, had an under-utilised fab shell (Fab 42) and pulled resources in 2014; TSMC reprioritised node development over 450 mm; by 2017 G450C had wound down. The underlying economics: **extreme ultraviolet (EUV)** lithography (Module 08), multi-patterning (printing one layer in several exposures, Module 07) and packaging had become the cost and capacity constraints, not wafer area, and no tool vendor would fund a new platform for a handful of customers. The wafer makers wrote off their 450 mm pullers or repurposed them for extra-long 300 mm ingots. As of 2025 there is no 450 mm roadmap at any foundry.

## Epi Versus Bulk: What the Crystal Grower Is Growing For

The crystal is grown to a substrate specification, and there are three families. The notation used for them reads layer/substrate, with + meaning heavily doped and − lightly doped, so p/p+ is a lightly doped p-type layer on a heavily doped p-type substrate.

- **Polished bulk wafers**: the ingot slice, ground, polished, cleaned and used as-is. DRAM and **NAND** (the flash-storage chips, Module 15) use these (annealed variants for DRAM), as does much trailing-edge logic and analog. The crystal's own defect engineering (perfect-silicon growth, nitrogen doping, oxygen control) must deliver the surface quality directly.
- **Epitaxial wafers** (Module 03): a substrate with a few micrometres of **epitaxial** silicon on top, a layer deposited by CVD atom by atom in continuation of the substrate's lattice, whose doping is set by the CVD, not by segregation. Advanced logic mostly uses **p/p−** (lightly doped epi on a lightly doped substrate); the older **p/p+** (epi on a ~0.005 to 0.02 Ω·cm boron substrate) gives near-immunity to CMOS **latch-up** (a parasitic switch formed by adjacent n and p regions that can turn on and short the supply to ground; a low-resistance substrate starves it), strong gettering, and a substrate whose COPs are buried. Vertical power **metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs)**, the gate-switched transistor of Module 11 in a form built to carry amps rather than microamps, use **n/n+** on arsenic- or antimony-doped substrates because the substrate is the **drain**, the terminal the current leaves by: in a vertical power transistor the current flows down through the whole wafer, so the substrate is part of the device. Heavily doped n+ substrates are where segregation bites: arsenic evaporates, antimony has k = 0.023, and both hit constitutional supercooling, so n+ ingots are pulled slowly and priced accordingly.
- **SOI** (silicon-on-insulator): a thin silicon layer on an oxide film on a handle wafer, made by **Smart Cut**, in which a hydrogen-implanted wafer is bonded to a handle and split along the implant (Soitec, Module 03); the crystal requirements are those of a polished wafer.

As of ~2025 the leading edge uses polished "perfect silicon" and p/p− epi for logic and annealed or polished wafers for memory, with 3D NAND's enormous film stress pushing oxygen specs upward for warpage resistance.

## Solar CZ: Same Physics, Different Economics

By tonnage, most CZ silicon now goes to photovoltaics, and solar pullers have become a different species. Mono ingots for wafers up to ~210 mm square (**G12** is the 210 mm square wafer format, cut from an ingot ~295 mm in diameter) are grown in 32- to 40-inch crucibles, and to maximise silicon per crucible the process either **recharges** the hot crucible with polysilicon several times (**RCZ**, recharge CZ), pulling 4 to 8 ingots per crucible, or feeds granular polysilicon continuously into an outer ring of the crucible at the rate crystal is withdrawn (**continuous CZ, CCZ**). Both reload a hot crucible, which step 1 called dangerous, and both re-use a crucible the Anatomy section called single-use; here is how. Recharging is not a second cold stack: between pulls a **feed tube** or a basket lowered from the pull chamber delivers granules or pre-warmed chunks into the melt slowly enough that nothing can bridge and nothing splashes, and the melt is never allowed to freeze, so the crucible never goes through the cool-down that cracks it. Solar crucibles are thicker-walled, sit in their susceptor for a campaign of days rather than hours, and the purity spec is loose enough that the extra dissolved wall and the slow build-up of carbon and metals over 4 to 8 pulls are tolerable; when the melt has picked up too much of everything, the last ingot is pulled and the crucible is finally scrapped. CCZ holds the melt composition constant, so dopant along the ingot is flat regardless of k. That mattered first for gallium-doped p-type (k = 0.008, an impossible Scheil profile in a batch melt), and now mainly for phosphorus-doped n-type, since **TOPCon** (tunnel-oxide passivated contact, the n-type cell design that became dominant around 2023 to 2025, as of 2025) took over the market. A solar crucible sees over a tonne of silicon in its life, ingots run 3 to 5 m, and a solar puller from JSG or Ferrotec costs a small fraction of a semiconductor puller and runs without magnets; thousands operate in Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Yunnan and Xinjiang. Solar scale in turn underpins the crucible, graphite and puller supply chains that semiconductor wafer makers depend on.

## Summary

- Polysilicon is chemically pure but made of random grains; a transistor needs one crystal, so CZ turns ~400 kg of chunks into one 300 mm, ~2 m, ~300 kg single crystal with the (100) orientation whose surface bonds the gate oxide best.
- CZ is a seed dipped into a 1,420 °C melt in a fused-silica crucible and pulled up at ~1 mm/min while crystal and crucible counter-rotate; a run is 30 to 50 h of growth in a 2.5 to 3.5 day cycle.
- The crucible is a consumable reactant: it dissolves at ~10 µm/h, ~99 % of the oxygen leaves as SiO gas swept out by argon at 10 to 50 mbar, and the ~1 % that stays gives CZ silicon its 10 to 18 ppma of oxygen.
- Dipping the seed always makes dislocations; the Dash neck (3 to 4 mm, 3 to 6 mm/min, 100 to 200 mm long) lets them glide out of the side because they move on {111} planes tilted to the axis; four habit lines prove the crystal is dislocation-free.
- Dopants partition between solid and liquid by k₀ (B 0.8, P 0.35, As 0.3, Sb 0.023); Scheil's equation says the tail is richer, by ~1.6× for boron and ~4.5× for phosphorus at 90 % solidified, which is why n-type ingots yield fewer in-spec wafers.
- Pull rate is capped by how fast the crystal can conduct away latent heat (~1 mm/min at G ≈ 30 K/cm) and by constitutional supercooling in heavily doped melts.
- Even a dislocation-free crystal carries ~10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ vacancies and interstitials; v/G decides which survives (critical ~1.3 to 2 × 10⁻³ cm²/(min·K)); vacancies make COP voids, interstitials make dislocation loops, and G rising toward the edge can put both on one wafer, separated by the OSF ring.
- The industry removes COPs by epi, by a ~1,200 °C anneal, or by growing inside the narrow v/G window ("perfect silicon", helped by nitrogen doping), trading throughput for defect-free surfaces.
- Oxygen is a feature: it hardens the wafer against slip in furnaces and, precipitated in the bulk under a denuded zone, getters metals; only image-sensor, power and RF wafers want it low.
- A magnetic field brakes the turbulent 400 kg melt (Lenz's law), quieting striations and cutting oxygen; at 300 mm MCZ is standard, with transverse fields of 0.2 to 0.4 T or cusp fields of 0.05 to 0.15 T from superconducting coils.
- The whole ingot hangs from the neck: 300 kg on 3 mm is ~400 MPa, so 300 mm pullers use thicker fast-grown necks, boron-hardened seeds or mechanical support jaws; 450 mm ingots were grown but the diameter died for tool-economics reasons between 2013 and 2017.
- Float Zone has no crucible: an RF coil melts a ~15 mm zone held by surface tension, giving oxygen below 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ and resistivity to 10⁴ Ω·cm for power and detector devices, but the zone-height limit caps production at 200 mm.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"The crucible is just a container."** → It is a consumable reactant. It dissolves at ~10¹⁸ cm⁻³ oxygen into the melt for the entire run, is the source of nearly all oxygen in a CZ wafer, defines the bulk mechanical strength and gettering behaviour of every wafer built on it, and is scrapped after a single use.
- **"A dislocation-free crystal is a defect-free crystal."** → Dash necking removes line defects; the crystal still contains ~10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ point defects that agglomerate into voids (COPs) or loops depending on v/G. Controlling those is the modern crystal grower's main job.
- **"Faster pulling is just about throughput."** → The pull rate, through v/G, decides whether the crystal is vacancy-rich or interstitial-rich. Perfect-silicon growth deliberately pulls slower than the puller could.
- **"Oxygen in the wafer is a contaminant to be minimised."** → For most logic and memory wafers it is deliberately kept at 10 to 14 ppma for mechanical strength and intrinsic gettering. Only image sensors, power and RF products want it low, and they pay for MCZ or FZ.
- **"The neck is the weak point, so it should be made thick."** → A thick neck does not filter dislocations; the thin, fast-grown neck is the only reason the crystal is dislocation-free at all. Thickening it to 5 to 6 mm works only with a faster pull and a higher risk of keeping dislocations, so the industry's real answers are to take the weight off the neck with support jaws or to avoid making dislocations at all with boron-hardened seeds.
- **"MCZ is an optional refinement."** → At 300 mm a 400 kg melt is deep in the turbulent regime; without a field the striations and oxygen scatter would not meet spec, and essentially every 300 mm semiconductor puller is a magnetic one. It is optional only on solar pullers with looser specs.
- **"450 mm was abandoned because the ingots could not be grown."** → 450 mm ingots and wafers were grown and shipped to G450C. It stalled because the process-tool vendors (ASML above all) would not fund a full tool generation for a handful of customers, and because EUV and packaging, not wafer area, had become the cost levers.
- **"Float Zone is the higher-quality process, so it should replace CZ."** → FZ offers low oxygen and high resistivity but its established commercial range here stops at 200 mm; larger diameter is an engineering development challenge. Cost and mechanical behavior also matter. CZ dominates 300 mm supply, and carefully controlled CZ oxygen can be useful for CMOS.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This stage consumes the electronic-grade polysilicon of Module 01 (Wacker, Hemlock, OCI, Tokuyama, GCL and Daqo chunk and granules, at roughly $20 to 40/kg), synthetic-lined quartz crucibles built from Spruce Pine sand, isostatic graphite and CFC hot-zone parts, argon, dopant alloys and enormous amounts of electricity, and it turns them into dislocation-free single-crystal ingots of specified orientation, resistivity, oxygen and defect type. Its customers are almost always in the same building: Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic and SK Siltron are vertically integrated from crystal to polished wafer, and the ingot moves straight to the cropping, grinding, slicing, lapping, etching, polishing and epitaxy line described in Module 03, which converts a 2 m, 300 kg ingot into ~1,500 to 1,800 polished 300 mm wafers that ship to TSMC, Samsung, Intel, Micron and SK hynix in **FOUPs** (front-opening unified pods, the sealed 25-wafer carriers of Module 05).
