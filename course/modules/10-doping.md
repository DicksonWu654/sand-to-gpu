# Module 10: Doping: Ion Implantation and Annealing

A transistor needs regions that supply mobile charge and regions where that charge can be controlled. An ordered silicon crystal gives us the starting structure (Module 2); **doping**, deliberately adding selected foreign atoms, gives different regions their electrical roles. Pure silicon at room temperature has about 10¹⁰ **free carriers** per cm³ (electrons, or the electron-vacancies called holes, loose enough to move and carry current) among 5 × 10²² atoms, and a **resistivity** of ~2.3 × 10⁵ Ω·cm. Resistivity is the resistance of a 1 cm cube of a material measured face to face, in ohm-centimetres (Ω·cm); 2.3 × 10⁵ Ω·cm is closer to glass than to copper.

Engineers place controlled numbers of foreign atoms into selected volumes of that crystal to make **n-type** silicon (extra mobile electrons) and **p-type** silicon (extra mobile holes): about 20 parts per billion of foreign atoms for the lightly doped regions a device sits in, about one atom in 500 for the heavily doped regions the metal wires touch (Section 1 names all six regions). Their location matters as much as their number: the atoms must go into a specific 3D region, a few nanometres deep and a few tens of nanometres wide, defined by an opening in a photoresist or hard-mask layer. They must sit on **lattice sites**, positions in the crystal's regular grid, in place of a silicon atom, because only there do they donate or accept an electron. And they must do all this without wrecking the crystal or wandering off during the hundreds of degrees of thermal processing that follow.

For the first thirty years of the industry this was done by **diffusion**: baking wafers in a gas containing the dopant and letting it soak in from the surface. Since the 1980s it has been done overwhelmingly by **ion implantation**: strip an electron from the dopant atom to make an ion, accelerate it through tens to thousands of kilovolts, sort it by mass, and fire it into the wafer. The **dose**, the number of ions delivered per cm² of wafer, is then set by an ammeter and a clock rather than by a chemical equilibrium, which is why implantation took over. The price is crystal damage: every ion knocks hundreds of silicon atoms off their sites on the way in. The second half of this module is about the **anneal**, the heating step that repairs the crystal and moves the dopant onto lattice sites, and about the surprising ways dopant moves while it happens. A leading-edge logic wafer still sees roughly 20–40 implant steps, even though the highest-concentration doping in the transistor (the source and drain) has largely moved to **epitaxy**, growing fresh silicon crystal that continues the wafer's lattice, with the dopant mixed in from the gas as it grows (Module 6).

By the end you will be able to explain how an implanter sets depth with a voltage and dose with an ammeter; why the crystal has to be repaired afterwards; why a cold anneal can spread dopant further than a hot one; and which implants a 2026 transistor still needs and which it has given up.

## Before you start

- **Valence electrons and the periodic table** (school chemistry). The outermost electrons of an atom are the ones that form bonds. Silicon has four; the group-V elements phosphorus (P), arsenic (As) and antimony (Sb) have five; the group-III elements boron (B), gallium (Ga), indium (In) and aluminium (Al) have three. That one-electron surplus or deficit is the whole basis of doping.
- **The silicon crystal** (Module 2). In a crystal every atom sits on a regular, repeating grid; a 300 mm wafer is one single crystal containing ~5 × 10²² atoms per cm³. A **lattice site** is one of those grid positions. This module is about putting a foreign atom onto a lattice site, or repairing the grid after knocking atoms off it.
- **Resistance and resistivity** (school physics). A bar of material has resistance R = ρ·L/A, where L is its length and A its cross-section. The material property ρ, the **resistivity**, is quoted in Ω·cm: a 1 cm cube of 10 Ω·cm silicon measures 10 Ω from one face to the opposite face.
- **Charged particles in fields** (school physics). An ion carrying one electron's worth of charge that falls through a voltage V gains an energy of V electron-volts: through 10,000 V it gains 10 keV. A magnetic field bends a moving ion into a circle whose radius grows with the ion's mass and speed, which is how an implanter sorts ions by mass.
- **Plasma** (Modules 6 and 9). A gas in which some atoms have lost an electron, so it contains free ions and free electrons and conducts electricity. An implanter's ion source is a small plasma; plasma doping uses a large one.
- **Photoresist and hard masks** (Module 7). Patterned layers that block ions everywhere except in their openings. Here they are 1–10 µm thick, far thicker than for etching, because ions at hundreds of keV travel a long way.
- **Arrhenius rates** (school chemistry). A process that needs thermal energy to clear a hurdle speeds up exponentially with temperature: in this module's temperature range, roughly doubling every 30 °C. Diffusion, activation and crystal regrowth are all Arrhenius.
- **The transistor at a glance**. A transistor has a **source** and a **drain** (where current enters and leaves), a **channel** between them, and a **gate** above the channel that switches it on and off. Section 1 defines the six doped regions you need for this module; Module 11 gives the full picture.

## 1. The Physics of Doping

### Bands, donors and acceptors

The first question is why adding a small number of atoms changes conductivity so much. A material can contain many electrons but have few that can contribute to current. What matters is whether electrons have accessible states into which they can move when an electric field is applied. **Energy bands** describe those allowed states; they are ranges of energy, not physical layers stacked inside the wafer.

An isolated silicon atom has the ladder of discrete energy levels that school chemistry draws as shells and orbitals. In a crystal the atoms sit so close together (~0.24 nm apart) that their outer orbitals overlap, and a rule of quantum mechanics, the **Pauli exclusion principle** (no two electrons in a system may occupy exactly the same state), forbids the 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³ from all keeping an identical copy of the same level. Each atomic level therefore splits into 10²² slightly different levels, spaced so finely that they merge into a near-continuous **band** of allowed energies, with gaps between bands where no level exists at all. In silicon the outer levels form two such bands: a **valence band**, completely filled by the electrons that form the bonds, and above it an empty **conduction band**, separated by a **band gap** of 1.12 eV in silicon. (An **electron-volt, eV**, is the energy an electron gains falling through 1 volt; a **milli-electron-volt, meV**, is a thousandth of that.) An electron in the full valence band cannot carry current: to carry current it would have to speed up, which means moving into a slightly higher-energy state, and in a full band every such state is already occupied, so nothing can change. An electron in the conduction band has empty states all around it and is free to move. At room temperature thermal jostling lifts only ~10¹⁰ electrons per cm³ across the gap, which is why pure silicon is nearly an insulator. Doping adds electrons to the conduction band, or removes them from the valence band, in known numbers.

Silicon is in group IV of the periodic table: each atom has four valence electrons and bonds to four neighbours at the corners of a tetrahedron. Substitute a group-V atom (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony) for a silicon atom and four of its five valence electrons complete the four bonds; the fifth has no bond to join and is held to the positive phosphorus core only by a weak electrical attraction.

> **Intuition: the hydrogen-like donor.** The fifth electron orbits the phosphorus core the way the electron in a hydrogen atom orbits its proton, with two differences. The surrounding silicon weakens the attraction about 12-fold (silicon's **dielectric constant** ε_r ≈ 11.7, a measure of how strongly a material screens electric fields), and the electron moves through the crystal as if it were lighter than a free electron. Both effects swell the orbit from 0.05 nm in hydrogen to ~2–3 nm, ten or more atoms across, and shrink the binding energy from hydrogen's 13.6 eV to ~0.045 eV. An electron that loosely held is easy to shake free.

The binding energy is ~45 meV for P, ~54 meV for As and ~39 meV for Sb. (This same quantity is also called the dopant's **ionisation energy**, since it is the energy needed to free the extra carrier from its dopant atom; the two names describe one number, and this module uses "ionisation energy" from here on.) The thermal energy at room temperature, kT (Boltzmann's constant k times the absolute temperature T), is ~26 meV, so 45 meV is under two kT; and because the conduction band offers vastly more places for the electron than the single donor level does, essentially every donor has given up its electron to the conduction band at 300 K. These atoms are **donors**, and silicon containing them is **n-type** (negative carriers). Substitute a group-III atom (boron, gallium, indium, aluminium) and one bond is short an electron. The missing electron is a **hole**: a gap in the bond network that a neighbouring electron can hop into, which moves the gap the other way, so the hole behaves like a mobile positive particle. The hole's ionisation energy is ~45 meV (B), ~72 meV (Ga) or ~160 meV (In). These atoms are **acceptors**, and the silicon is **p-type**. On a diagram of allowed energies the donor level sits ~45 meV below the bottom of the conduction band, labelled E_C, and the acceptor level ~45 meV above the top of the valence band, E_V.

One more idea carries into every later module. The **Fermi level** is an energy reference that helps describe how likely electronic states are to be occupied. A water-level analogy helps with equilibrium: connected water settles to one level; connected semiconductor regions at equilibrium share one Fermi level. Before contact, that level is higher in n-type silicon (more electrons) and lower in p-type. When the regions meet, charge redistributes and creates an electric field. On an energy diagram, the band edges shift across the boundary, often described as **band bending**. The drawing shows changing electron energies, not silicon physically bending. The boundary between the regions is a **p-n junction**.

Here is what physically happens at that boundary. The n side is crowded with mobile electrons and the p side with mobile holes, so electrons spill across into the p side and drop into holes there (each such event removes one electron and one hole), and holes spill the other way and do the same. Every electron that leaves the n side uncovers the donor atom it came from, which is a fixed positive ion; every hole that leaves the p side uncovers a fixed negative acceptor ion. So a strip on either side of the boundary ends up with no mobile carriers at all, only fixed charged dopant ions, positive on the n side and negative on the p side. This carrier-free strip is the **depletion region**. The two sheets of opposite fixed charge set up an electric field across it, pointing from n to p, which pushes any further electrons back toward the n side and holes back toward the p side; the spilling stops when that push exactly balances the tendency to diffuse. The voltage step across the region at that balance is the **built-in potential**, ~0.7–1.0 V in silicon.

An applied voltage shifts the balance, and the two directions have names that recur throughout this module. **Forward bias** (the p side made positive relative to the n side) opposes the built-in field, narrows the depletion region and lets carriers flood across: current flows. **Reverse bias** (the p side made negative) adds to the built-in field, widens the depletion region and blocks current, apart from a small **leakage** current from the few carriers generated inside the region. Two rules about the width matter later. It grows with reverse voltage, because a larger field needs more uncovered dopant charge to support it, which is how source and drain depletion regions can grow until they meet (punch-through, below). And it shrinks as doping rises, because a heavily doped layer supplies the same fixed charge from a thinner strip: at 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ a depletion region is roughly 100 nm wide, at 10²⁰ cm⁻³ only a few nanometres, thin enough for electrons to tunnel through, which is the trick behind the contact implants of Section 8. The depletion region is where a junction's electric field lives, where a reverse-biased junction blocks current, and where implant damage does the most harm (Section 6).

The dopant only works if it sits **substitutionally**, on a lattice site in place of a silicon atom. Picture the crystal as a car park with marked bays. A **vacancy** is an empty bay: a lattice site with no atom on it. An **interstitial** is a car parked in the aisle between bays: an atom squeezed in where there is no site at all. An interstitial boron atom, or a boron atom paired with a silicon interstitial or clustered with other borons, contributes nothing. **Activation** in this module always means the fraction of the implanted atoms sitting on lattice sites and ionised.

### Why these elements?

Phosphorus and boron are the workhorses because they are small and light, have low ionisation energies (the ~45 meV of the previous subsection, so every atom contributes a carrier), and have high **solid solubility**: the most dopant the crystal will hold on lattice sites at a given temperature, like sugar in tea, beyond which the extra precipitates out as useless clusters. Arsenic is used where a heavy, slow-diffusing donor is needed, for abrupt n⁺ junctions (a superscript plus means very heavily doped, ~10²⁰ cm⁻³ or more) and because a heavy ion easily knocks the crystal into a disordered, glass-like state, which turns out to be useful (Section 6). Antimony diffuses even more slowly and is used for deep layers under a device and for layers that stop current sneaking beneath the channel (Section 8). Indium and gallium were investigated for steep p-type channel profiles, but their deeper acceptor levels leave a fraction un-ionised at room temperature; indium is essentially unused in modern logic, though gallium has a niche in some p⁺ contacts. Aluminium is irrelevant in silicon but is *the* p-type dopant for silicon carbide (Section 9).

### The transistor in six regions

You cannot read a doping table without a picture of what is being doped. Here is an n-channel **MOSFET** (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) in cross-section, the flat "planar" kind that dominated until ~2011 and has every region a modern transistor has:

```
          metal plug                gate                metal plug
              │              ┌──────────────┐               │
              ▼              │  gate metal  │               ▼
     ┌────────────────┐      ├──────────────┤      ┌────────────────┐
     │   n⁺ source    │ ext  │  gate oxide  │  ext │    n⁺ drain    │
     └────────────────┴──────┴───channel────┴──────┴────────────────┘
                          p-well  (lightly doped p-type tub)
     ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
                          p-type substrate  (the wafer)
```

- **Source and drain**: two heavily doped n-type islands; current flows from one to the other when the transistor is on.
- **Channel**: the thin strip of silicon between them, directly under the gate, of the opposite type (p here), so with the gate off there are two back-to-back p-n junctions and no current flows. The name "n-channel" is not a contradiction: it refers to the electrons that a positive gate pulls into this p-type strip when the transistor is on (the strip is then said to be **inverted**, its surface behaving as n-type), while the doping type describes the strip at rest. A **p-channel** transistor is the mirror image: an n-type strip, p⁺ source and drain, and a negative gate voltage pulling in holes.
- **Gate**: the electrode above the channel, separated from it by the **gate oxide**, an insulator 1–2 nm thick. A positive gate voltage pulls electrons into the channel and connects source to drain. The **threshold voltage, V_T**, is the gate voltage at which the channel turns on; setting it precisely is much of what doping is for.
- **Well**: the lightly doped tub (p-type here, ~10¹⁷–10¹⁸ cm⁻³) the whole transistor sits in. Wells of opposite type let n-channel and p-channel transistors share one wafer, which is the "C" in **CMOS** (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, the pairing of n- and p-channel transistors that every logic chip is built from).
- **Extension** (the lightly doped drain, LDD, in older flows): the shallow tip of the source and drain that reaches under the edge of the gate to meet the channel.
- **Contact**: the spot where a metal plug lands on the source or drain; the most heavily doped silicon in the device, because metal-to-silicon resistance falls as doping rises.

Two more words appear constantly. **Isolation** is whatever keeps neighbouring transistors from talking to each other: oxide-filled trenches between them (Section 8) plus wells and depletion regions that block current through the silicon. **Punch-through** is the failure where the source and drain depletion regions grow until they touch, so current flows under the channel regardless of the gate; wells and dedicated stopper layers are doped to prevent it.

### Carrier concentration and resistivity

**Mobility**, μ, is how fast a carrier drifts per unit electric field, in cm² per volt-second (cm²/V·s); think of it as the carrier's ease of travel through the lattice. It falls as doping rises because the ionised dopant atoms are charged obstacles that scatter carriers: electron mobility in silicon is ~1,400 cm²/V·s at 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ but only ~80–100 cm²/V·s at 10²⁰ cm⁻³; hole mobility is ~470 falling to ~50 cm²/V·s over the same range.

Silicon has 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³. The industry's working doping range, 10¹⁵ to a bit over 10²⁰ cm⁻³, corresponds to 20 parts per billion (ppb) to 0.2 %. The bottom of the range is set by what a crystal grower can control (Module 2: Czochralski-pulled wafers, the standard kind, ship at 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ cm⁻³) and by the need for a junction's depletion region to be wide enough for isolation (lighter doping gives a wider depletion region); the top of the range is set by solid solubility and by the point where adding more dopant no longer lowers resistance because mobility collapses.

Resistivity is ρ = 1/(q·n·μ), where q is the electronic charge (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), n the carrier concentration per cm³ and μ the mobility. In words: resistance falls with more carriers and with faster carriers, and because μ falls as n rises, the two partly cancel at the top of the range.

> **Worked example: resistivity from doping.** For n-type silicon at n = 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ with μ ≈ 1,400 cm²/V·s: ρ = 1/(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁵ × 1,400) = 1/(2.24 × 10⁻¹) ≈ 4.5 Ω·cm. At n = 10²⁰ cm⁻³ with μ ≈ 90 cm²/V·s: ρ = 1/(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10²⁰ × 90) = 1/1,440 ≈ 7 × 10⁻⁴ Ω·cm, or ~0.7 mΩ·cm. So five orders of magnitude more dopant bought only four orders of magnitude less resistivity, because mobility fell 15-fold along the way. And to see how dilute the light end is: 10¹⁵ dopant atoms among 5 × 10²² silicon atoms is 10¹⁵ / 5 × 10²² = 2 × 10⁻⁸, twenty atoms per billion.

So 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ n-type silicon is ~4.5 Ω·cm and 10¹⁵ p-type ~13 Ω·cm (holes are slower), while 10²⁰ of either type is ~1 mΩ·cm. Five orders of magnitude of doping (10¹⁵ to 10²⁰ cm⁻³) buy about four orders of magnitude of resistivity. This is why each region of a transistor has a characteristic doping:

| Region | Typical doping (cm⁻³) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ | As grown; light doping means low leakage and wide depletion regions, which help isolation |
| Wells | 10¹⁷–10¹⁸ | Heavy enough that the source and drain depletion regions stay small, setting junction depth and stopping punch-through |
| Channel / V_T adjust (planar era) | 10¹⁷–10¹⁸ | Dopant under the gate shifts the gate voltage at which the channel turns on |
| Source/drain (S/D) extensions (LDD) | 10¹⁹–10²⁰ | Abrupt and shallow for low resistance, but graded so that electrons accelerated by a too-sharp drain edge (**hot carriers**) do not damage the gate oxide |
| Deep source/drain and contacts | 10²⁰–10²¹ | Lowest possible series resistance and metal-contact resistance |
| Poly gate (legacy) | ~10²⁰ | A **polysilicon** gate (silicon made of many small crystals) that is too lightly doped loses carriers near the oxide when biased (**poly depletion**), which acts like a thicker oxide and weakens the gate |

The upper limit deserves emphasis. Boron's equilibrium solid solubility in silicon is ~1–2 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³ at 1,000 °C and rises to ~4 × 10²⁰ at 1,100 °C; arsenic's chemical solubility is over 10²¹ but only ~2–3 × 10²⁰ is electrically active because As atoms cluster around vacancies; phosphorus can reach ~5 × 10²⁰ active. Above these limits, extra dopant precipitates or forms inactive clusters, and the millisecond anneals discussed later exist mainly to freeze in **metastable** activation above the equilibrium limit: not the lowest-energy state, but stuck there because escaping is slow at room temperature.

## 2. Thermal Diffusion: The Original Method

Until roughly 1980, doping meant diffusion. Wafers were loaded in quartz boats into a tube furnace at 900–1,200 °C with a dopant source: POCl₃ vapour or BBr₃ for gaseous sources, or solid boron-nitride wafers interleaved with the silicon. The dopant formed a glass (phosphosilicate or borosilicate) on the surface, and the silicon underneath took up dopant at its solid solubility limit. The furnaces were the ancestors of the vertical batch furnaces that TEL, Kokusai and ASM still sell for annealing (Section 7).

The mathematics is **Fick's laws**. The first law says flux is proportional to the concentration gradient, J = −D·∂C/∂x, where J is the number of atoms crossing unit area per second, C the concentration and x the depth. The symbol ∂ marks a **partial derivative**, the rate of change with respect to one variable while the others are held fixed: ∂C/∂x is how steeply the concentration changes with depth at one instant, and the minus sign says atoms drift from where they are crowded toward where they are sparse. The second law, ∂C/∂t = D·∂²C/∂x², follows from conservation (atoms are neither created nor destroyed, only moved) and says how the profile evolves in time t. Here ∂C/∂t is how fast the concentration at a given depth changes with time, and ∂²C/∂x² is the curvature of the profile, how fast its slope changes with depth. In words: where the profile is dished (curving upward) the concentration rises, and where it is peaked (curving downward) it falls, so peaks flatten and dips fill in, at a rate set by D. The constant of proportionality, the **diffusivity** D (cm²/s), is Arrhenius: D = D₀·exp(−E_a/kT), where D₀ is a prefactor, E_a is the **activation energy** (the energy hurdle an atom must clear to hop to the next site, in eV), k is Boltzmann's constant and T the temperature in kelvin. The exponential is the fraction of atoms with enough thermal energy to jump, and at these temperatures it doubles roughly every 30 °C. E_a is ~3.5 eV for boron and phosphorus and ~4 eV for arsenic. Boron's D is ~2 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s at 1,000 °C and ~2 × 10⁻¹³ at 1,100 °C, a factor of ten for a hundred degrees; arsenic is about ten times slower still.

> **Intuition: √(Dt), the wander distance.** A diffusing atom takes random hops, and after time t it has typically wandered a distance √(Dt) from where it started; this one number is the single most useful quantity in this module. Because of the square root, doubling the time adds only 41 % to the distance, while raising the temperature by 100 °C multiplies D by ten and the distance by three. Temperature, not time, is the big lever, which is why anneal design is about peak temperature and getting away from it quickly.

Two boundary conditions give the two classic profiles. **Predeposition**, with the surface held at solid solubility C_s, gives a complementary error function: C(x,t) = C_s·erfc(x / 2√(Dt)), with total dose Q = 2·C_s·√(Dt/π). Its shape is flat at the surface at the solubility limit, then a smeared cliff falling away with depth. **Drive-in**, where the source is removed (the glass is stripped) and a fixed dose Q is pushed deeper, gives a Gaussian: C(x,t) = (Q/√(πDt))·exp(−x²/4Dt), a bell curve centred on the surface whose width grows as √(Dt) and whose peak falls to keep the area (the dose) constant. A two-step predep-then-drive-in sequence was how every junction in a 1970s process was made.

> **Worked example: diffusion lengths.** A 30-minute drive-in at 1,000 °C with D_B ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s gives √(Dt) = √(2 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 1,800 s) = √(3.6 × 10⁻¹¹) ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁶ cm = 60 nm. A modern 1-second spike anneal at 1,050 °C (D_B ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹⁴) gives √(Dt) ≈ 2.4 nm. A millisecond laser anneal at 1,300 °C (D_B ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹²) for 1 ms gives √(Dt) ≈ 0.6 nm. The entire history of annealing can be read from this line: to keep a junction under 10 nm you need thermal budgets whose √(Dt) is a nanometre or two, which means either much lower temperatures or much shorter times, and shorter is the direction the industry chose because activation needs the temperature.

Diffusion lost for four reasons. First, dose control: the dose depends on surface concentration, temperature, time and gas flow, and reproducibility was a few percent at best, versus < 1 % for implantation. Second, the surface concentration is pinned at solid solubility; you cannot make a 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ layer directly. Third, diffusion is isotropic (the same in every direction): dopant spreads sideways under the mask edge by ~0.8 × the junction depth, intolerable once gate lengths dropped below a micron. Fourth, the profile is always maximum at the surface; a **retrograde** profile (peak buried, low at the surface) is impossible by diffusion and is exactly what a well needs. Ion implantation solves all four. Diffusion survives today only as an unwanted side effect of annealing, and in a few specialised places: some power-device and analogue flows, and processes that deliberately grow an oxide during the anneal because oxidation injects silicon interstitials and speeds diffusion (**oxidation-enhanced diffusion**), used in some **BiCMOS** flows, which combine bipolar transistors with CMOS on one chip.

## 3. Inside an Ion Implanter

The problem an implanter solves is this: put exactly N atoms of one chosen species per cm² of wafer, at a chosen depth, with nothing else mixed in, and count them as you go. An implanter is a **mass spectrometer**, an instrument that sorts ions by mass using a magnet, with a wafer where the detector would be. A high-current tool is 6–10 m long, weighs ~15–25 tonnes, is wrapped in lead and interlocks, and costs roughly $4–8 M depending on class. Walking down the beamline, the beam passes seven stages in order:

1. **Ion source**: a small plasma turns dopant gas or vapour into ions of every kind the gas can make.
2. **Extraction**: a 30–80 kV gap pulls the ions out of the plasma as a ribbon-shaped beam; the physics of mutual repulsion here caps the current every later stage has to live with.
3. **Analyser magnet**: bends the beam so that only the wanted ion (say ¹¹B⁺) follows the curve of the pipe; everything else hits the wall.
4. **Acceleration or deceleration column**: sets the final energy, and with it the depth the ions will reach.
5. **Scanning**: sweeps a beam a few centimetres wide across a 300 mm wafer, or the wafer across the beam, so every point receives the same dose.
6. **End station**: holds the wafer at the chosen tilt and rotation, cools it, and handles it in and out of vacuum.
7. **Faraday cup**: catches the beam and integrates its current, so charge divided by area is the dose.

### Ion source

The source is an arc-discharge plasma in a small tungsten (or tungsten-lined graphite) **arc chamber**, roughly 5 × 5 × 10 cm, with a slot for extraction. A **plasma** is a gas in which some atoms have lost an electron, so it contains free ions and electrons and conducts electricity; an **arc** is the sustained electrical discharge that keeps it lit. Feed gas enters at a few cm³ per minute measured at atmospheric pressure (**sccm**, standard cubic centimetres per minute): **BF₃** for boron, **PH₃** (phosphine) for phosphorus, **AsH₃** (arsine) for arsenic, **GeH₄** or GeF₄ for germanium, plus SiF₄ for silicon ions (used, like germanium, to disorder the crystal on purpose; see "Channeling" below), N₂ for nitrogen (gate-dielectric implants and the n-type dopant of silicon carbide, Section 9), CO₂ for carbon (a co-implant that pins down unwanted diffusion, Section 6), and Xe or Ar as heavy inert ions used purely to damage or disorder a surface. Solid dopants (antimony, indium, gallium, aluminium) are evaporated from a **vaporiser** oven at a few hundred degrees and the vapour piped into the same arc chamber; Sb₂O₃ and Al are the common solids.

The two source architectures are the **Bernas** source and the **indirectly heated cathode (IHC)** source. In a Bernas source a tungsten filament sits inside the arc chamber; it is heated to ~2,500 K and boils off electrons (**thermionic emission**, the same process that lit a vacuum tube), and a ~50–120 V arc voltage between filament and chamber wall accelerates those electrons to ionise the gas. A **source magnet** of a few hundred gauss (tens of millitesla, a few hundred times Earth's field) along the chamber axis makes the electrons spiral, lengthening their path and improving ionisation efficiency, and a **repeller** electrode at the far end bounces them back. In an IHC source (the industry standard for high-current tools since the late 1990s) the filament sits *behind* a thick tungsten cathode button and heats it by electron bombardment; the cathode, not the filament, faces the plasma. This keeps the fragile filament out of the corrosive fluorine plasma and roughly doubles source life, to ~100–300 hours between rebuilds, which matters because source maintenance is the dominant downtime item on an implanter. BF₃ plasma etches tungsten as WF₆ and redeposits it elsewhere; arsenic and phosphorus form conductive coatings on the insulators, which then short-circuit in small arcs. Source lifetime and cleanliness is a whole sub-discipline; Axcelis and Applied both sell "extended life" sources, and co-flowing a little hydrogen or a fluorine-scavenging gas is one trick used.

The plasma contains everything: B⁺, BF⁺, BF₂⁺, BF₃⁺, F⁺, B₂⁺, and both isotopes (atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons) ¹⁰B (19.9 %) and ¹¹B (80.1 %). Typical arc currents are 1–5 A and arc power a few hundred watts. Sorting this zoo into a single species is the next two stages' job.

### Extraction and the space-charge limit

The arc chamber sits at the **extraction voltage** relative to the first electrode, typically **30–80 kV** for a high-current or medium-current tool. Between the plasma and the electrode is the **sheath**, the thin boundary layer between a plasma and a surface across which almost all of the extraction voltage drops; ions that wander to the plasma edge fall through it and leave.

> **Intuition: space charge.** Every ion in the beam carries the same positive charge, and like charges repel, so the beam pushes itself apart. The more current you try to pull through the gap, the more positive charge sits in the gap at once, and the stronger it pushes newly arriving ions back toward the source. This mutual repulsion is **space charge**, and it sets a ceiling on how much current any gap can pass, however strong the plasma. Every beam-current number in this module, and the split of the tool market into separate machines (Section 4), comes from this ceiling.

Extraction is where beam current is fundamentally limited: the current density that can be pulled through the sheath is bounded by the **Child-Langmuir** space-charge law, J ∝ V^(3/2) / (d²·√m), where J is the current density (A/cm²), V the extraction voltage, d the gap between plasma edge and electrode, and m the ion mass. In words: doubling the voltage gives 2.8 times the current; doubling the gap cuts it to a quarter; and heavier ions, which move more slowly at the same energy and so pile up more, extract less current. Ion mass is quoted in **atomic mass units (amu)**, one amu being roughly the mass of a proton or neutron: boron-11 is 11 amu, arsenic 75. So at the same voltage an As⁺ beam extracts at √(11/75) ≈ 38 % of the current of a B⁺ beam. Higher extraction voltage helps, and is why tools extract at high voltage even when the final energy is low, then decelerate; a smaller gap helps until it arcs. Downstream of the gap the beam makes its own thin plasma: the fast ions knock electrons off the residual gas molecules in the pipe, and those slow, freed electrons are held inside the beam by its positive charge and travel along with it, partly cancelling its space charge. This **beam neutralisation** is what lets a beam of tens of milliamps survive the metres of beamline after extraction at all. But the extraction gap would pull those same electrons back up its 30–80 kV, so a **suppression electrode** biased a few kV negative sits just outside the extraction slit to turn them back; electrons that got through would load the supply and, striking the source at full voltage, generate X-rays. Extraction slits are tall and narrow (e.g. 3 × 50 mm), so the extracted beam is a ribbon.

### Analyser magnet and mass resolution

The extracted beam enters a dipole **analyser magnet** (a two-pole electromagnet with a uniform field across the gap; typically a 60–90° bend, radius ~0.3–0.5 m, field up to ~1 tesla, about 20,000 times Earth's field). An ion of mass m, charge q and energy qV moving in a field B follows a circle of radius r = (1/B)·√(2mV/q). In words: heavier or faster ions bend less, and a stronger field bends everything more; the magnet is set so exactly the wanted ion follows the curve of the pipe. Only that species passes through the **resolving aperture** (a slit) at the magnet exit; everything else hits the walls. The magnet is set by a lookup table of B for the desired species and energy, and fine-tuned by peaking the beam current through the aperture. For a 49 amu BF₂⁺ ion at 30 keV in a 0.5 T field, r = (1/0.5)·√(2 × 49 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg × 30,000 V / 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) ≈ 0.35 m, which is exactly the scale of a real magnet.

**Mass resolution** m/Δm is typically 60–100. A resolution of 60 means two ions whose masses differ by one part in 60 land in different places at the slit, so ¹¹B⁺ (mass 11) and ¹⁰B⁺ (mass 10), which differ by one part in 11, are cleanly separated; only m/Δm > 11 would be strictly needed, but you want clean rejection of the tails. Separating ³¹P⁺ from ³⁰SiH⁺ (mass 31 also) is impossible by mass alone and is dealt with by not having silicon compounds in the source. Separating ⁷⁵As⁺ from ⁷⁴Ge⁺ when a source has been used for both matters for **cross-contamination** (a trace of the previous species riding along in the beam) and is why production tools are often dedicated to n-type or p-type species. The choice of ¹¹B⁺ versus ⁴⁹BF₂⁺ is about energy: a molecular ion shares its kinetic energy among its atoms in proportion to mass, so BF₂⁺ at 30 keV delivers its boron atom with only 30 × (11/49) ≈ 6.7 keV. Before ultra-low-energy tools existed, BF₂ was how shallow p⁺ junctions were made, with the fluorine coming along (and, it turns out, helping to disorder the surface and slightly retard boron diffusion). Doubly charged ions are the opposite trick: P⁺⁺ has lost two electrons, so it gains twice the energy per volt, and a 200 kV terminal delivers 400 keV phosphorus at the cost of a much smaller beam current, since doubly charged fractions are only a few percent of the plasma.

### Acceleration, deceleration and the column

After mass analysis the beam is at the extraction energy. A **post-analysis acceleration column** (a stack of ring electrodes with graded voltages) adds or subtracts energy to reach the final value. The source and magnet sit on the **terminal**, a high-voltage platform; the wafer is at ground, so the ions fall through the terminal voltage on their way down the column. A medium-current tool with a 200–300 kV terminal covers ~5 keV to 600+ keV with singly and doubly charged ions. Above about 1 MeV (a **mega-electron-volt, MeV**, is a million electron-volts, or 1,000 keV: the energy a singly charged ion gains falling through a million volts), DC voltages become impractical: the air around sharp high-voltage edges breaks down and glows (**corona**), stray electrons make X-rays, and the insulators grow enormous. **High-energy** implanters therefore use a **radio-frequency linear accelerator (linac)**: a row of hollow electrodes whose voltage flips sign millions of times a second, timed so that an ion always sees a push as it passes each gap; the energy adds up stage by stage without any single voltage being huge. Axcelis's Purion XE uses a 12-stage linac to reach 4.5 MeV, and the Purion XEmax extends to ~15 MeV with multiply charged ions. Older tools used tandem electrostatic accelerators (negative ions accelerated to a positive terminal, stripped to positive, and accelerated again) but these have largely gone.

**Deceleration** is the reverse, and it is how sub-keV implants are done: extract at, say, 5 keV where space charge permits a useful current, analyse, then slow the beam to 500 eV just before the wafer. The danger is **energy contamination**. The beamline is at ~10⁻⁶ torr (a **torr** is 1/760 of atmospheric pressure, so this is about a billionth of an atmosphere), but a few percent of ions still undergo **charge exchange** over a metre of path: a fast ion snatches an electron from a stray gas molecule and becomes a fast neutral atom, which electric fields can no longer slow down. Any ion neutralised before the decel stage arrives at the full 5 keV, ten times deeper than intended.

> **What can go wrong: energy contamination.** *Symptom*: the junction is deeper than the recipe predicts, and the depth varies with beamline pressure and source age. *Detection*: a SIMS depth profile (secondary ion mass spectrometry, the depth-profiling method of Section 10) on a monitor wafer (an unpatterned test wafer run alongside product) shows a tail extending to ~10 × the intended depth carrying ~1 % of the dose. That 1 % is enough to move the junction: the junction depth is where the dopant concentration falls to the background doping of the opposite type (Section 5 makes this precise), and 1 % of a 10¹⁵ cm⁻² dose is still 10¹³ cm⁻², which spread over a few tens of nanometres of tail is a concentration of ~10¹⁸ cm⁻³ or more, above any well doping. So wherever the tail ends is where the junction ends; the tail *is* the junction. Maps from the ThermaProbe optical dose monitor (Section 10) also drift. *Fix*: modern low-energy tools place a bend or electrostatic **energy filter** *after* deceleration, so only ions at the correct final energy reach the wafer, and they hold beamline pressure low to keep the neutral fraction down.

### Beam optics, the neutral trap and beam parallelism

Between magnet and wafer, **quadrupole lenses** (four-pole magnetic or electrostatic elements that focus a charged beam the way a glass lens focuses light) shape the beam, and a **neutral trap** removes neutrals: the beam is bent by a few degrees electrostatically or magnetically, neutrals continue straight into a beam dump, and only charged particles follow the bend to the wafer. This matters because neutrals are not counted by the dose measurement system, so a beam with 3 % neutrals would give 3 % overdose.

The last stage before the wafer is where the beam's *angle* is fixed, and it deserves more than a clause because the fin-shadow argument below turns on it. On a ribbon-beam tool the beam leaving the analyser is a ribbon a few centimetres tall, the height of the extraction slit. It is first fanned out magnetically, so that it diverges like light from a point source until it is wider than the wafer, more than 300 mm. Then a **collimator** (or parallelising) magnet, a bending magnet whose field strength varies across its gap, bends the outer rays of the fan back more strongly than the inner ones until all of them run parallel, exactly as a lens turns the diverging light from a point source into a parallel beam. On a spot-beam tool the same job is done by a corrector magnet placed after the scanner, which straightens each scanned ray as it passes. Either way, every ion strikes the wafer at the same angle to within ~±0.5°, at every point across the 300 mm.

Angular accuracy became important when the implanted structure stopped being a flat surface. In a modern transistor the channel is a **fin**: a vertical wall of silicon ~50 nm tall and ~5–6 nm wide standing on the wafer (Section 8). A beam tilted 7° from vertical casts a shadow behind a 50 nm fin of length 50 × tan 7° ≈ 6.1 nm; at 8° the shadow is 50 × tan 8° ≈ 7.0 nm, 15 % longer. So a 1° error on a 7° tilt changes the shadowed length of a fin by ~15 %, and the dopant reaches a different length of fin sidewall.

> **Why it matters downstream: implant angle.** The two sides of a fin are implanted at mirror-image angles in separate passes. If the angle drifts between passes, or across the wafer, the two sides receive different doses and the two transistors that share a fin no longer match. In an SRAM cell (static random-access memory, the on-chip cache), whose six transistors must be closely matched, that shows up as a spread in threshold voltage from cell to cell and, at the extreme, cells that cannot be read reliably (Modules 11 and 13). Angle control to a fraction of a degree is therefore a specification, not a nicety.

### Scanning

A spot beam is at most a few cm across, and a ribbon beam, even after fanning to wafer width, is only a few cm tall; the wafer is 300 mm in both directions. The beam must be swept over the wafer (or the wafer past the beam) with better than 1 % dose uniformity. Three schemes coexist:

- **Electrostatic two-axis scanning** (medium-current tools): deflection plates **raster** the spot beam (sweep it line by line, like an old television) at kHz rates in x and y over a stationary wafer. Simple, but a scanned spot arrives at varying angles across the wafer unless a corrector magnet re-parallelises it, and space charge limits it to a few mA.
- **Hybrid scanning** (most modern high-current and medium-current single-wafer tools, e.g. Applied's VIISta series and Axcelis's Purion): the beam is scanned electromagnetically in one axis (or is a fixed ribbon beam already fanned out wider than the wafer, as described above), and the wafer is mechanically translated slowly in the other axis on an end-station arm. The wafer velocity profile is **servoed** to the measured beam current (a feedback loop continuously adjusts the speed) so that dose stays uniform even as beam current drifts.
- **Mechanical spinning-disk batch** (classic high-current tools, e.g. the Varian E-series and Axcelis GSD/Optima HD): 13–17 wafers clamped on the rim of a ~1 m disk spinning at ~1,200 rpm while the disk is slowly translated radially through a fixed beam. Batch tools spread the beam's power (tens of mA × tens of kV = kW) over many wafers so no single wafer overheats. Almost all new tools are single-wafer, but batch disks remain in older fabs and in some high-dose applications.

### Channeling

Before the end station, one piece of physics that decides how the wafer must be held.

> **Intuition: rows in an orchard.** Walk past an orchard and the trees look like a random thicket, until you reach the spot where you are looking straight down a row and can see clear to the far side. A crystal is the same: along certain directions the atoms line up and leave open corridors between them. An ion travelling down a corridor makes only glancing collisions with the corridor walls, loses energy slowly, and goes several times deeper than one travelling at a random angle.

Silicon is a crystal, and along certain directions an ion can travel between rows of atoms. Crystallographers label these with triples of small integers that give a direction's components, or a plane's orientation, along the three cube edges of the crystal, and the brackets carry meaning: angle brackets such as ⟨110⟩ name a whole family of equivalent directions, curly brackets such as {111} a whole family of equivalent planes, and round brackets such as (100) one specific plane. All you need here is that the ⟨110⟩ direction is the most open corridor in silicon, then ⟨100⟩ and the {111} and {110} planes, and that a wafer's surface is a (100) plane, so a beam straight down the wafer normal is looking down a ⟨100⟩ corridor. A **channeled** ion goes several times deeper than the random-stopping prediction. Because a small change in angle or a little surface damage de-channels ions, channeling shows up as an irreproducible deep tail on the profile, and the entire practice of implantation is organised to suppress it:

1. **Tilt** the wafer by 7° (the classic number: enough that the beam is well away from ⟨100⟩, small enough that shadowing is modest) and **twist** it (rotate it about its own normal) by ~22° so that the beam also misses the major planes.
2. Implant through a thin **screen oxide** (5–20 nm of SiO₂) that randomises the incoming directions. The cost is **oxygen knock-on**: recoiled oxygen atoms end up in the silicon, and the screen absorbs a fraction of a low-energy implant, so sub-keV implants are often done bare.
3. **Pre-amorphisation implant (PAI)**: implant germanium (~10–30 keV, 5 × 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ cm⁻²) or silicon first so the top 20–40 nm is **amorphous** (its atoms knocked out of their regular arrangement, like glass compared with quartz) and there are no channels at all. This gives the sharpest, most reproducible boron profiles, and is also required for millisecond and solid-phase-regrowth anneals (Section 7). Ge PAI is standard before contact implants and many extension implants.
4. Use molecular ions (BF₂⁺ or larger) whose heavy fragments amorphise the surface as they go, so the implant is self-de-channeling after the first ~10¹⁴ cm⁻².

### End station, tilt and twist

The end station holds the wafer on an **electrostatic chuck**, a flat plate that grips the wafer by the attraction between charged electrodes buried in the plate and the opposite charge they induce in the wafer, with gas flowed behind the wafer for cooling. Wafer temperature during a high-dose implant is a real concern: 20 mA at 40 kV is 800 W into the wafer, and even with backside gas the thermal coupling from wafer to chuck is only ~5 W/K, so a steady 800 W would hold the wafer ~160 °C above the chuck. The wafer's own heat capacity (~90 J/K for a 300 mm wafer) buys only seconds: 800 W raises it by ~9 °C every second. Without cooling, and without the scanning that spreads the beam's dwell over the whole wafer so that no spot is under the beam for long, the wafer would rapidly exceed the ~100 °C above which photoresist **reticulates** (its surface wrinkles and cracks like dried paint) and outgasses. The chuck sets the **tilt** (angle between beam and wafer normal, commonly 0° or 7°, up to 45–60° for tilted implants that put dopant under the gate edge and for some fin implants) and the **twist** (rotation of the wafer about its normal, commonly ~22–23° off the alignment notch cut in the wafer edge, so that the beam is not aligned with a crystal plane), the two channeling answers from the previous subsection. Multi-rotation implants (**quad** implants at four twists 90° apart, each still offset from the crystal planes, e.g. 22°, 112°, 202° and 292°, each getting a quarter of the dose) are used whenever a tilted beam would otherwise shadow one side of a gate or fin; the ~22° offset and the 90° steps are two separate things, one for channeling and one for symmetry.

### Dose measurement

**Dose** is measured by charge integration. A **Faraday cup** (a deep, biased metal cup that captures the beam and suppresses the escape of **secondary electrons**, electrons knocked out of a surface by the beam) sits behind the wafer plane and samples the beam during the scan or between wafers; on ribbon-beam tools a travelling Faraday profiles the beam before each lot (a batch of wafers processed together). The dose is

Q [ions/cm²] = ∫I·dt / (n·q·A),

where ∫I·dt is the integrated beam current (total charge collected, in coulombs), n the charge state of the ion (1 for B⁺, 2 for P⁺⁺), q the elementary charge and A the scanned area. In words: count the charge, divide by the charge per ion and by the area. The end-station controller integrates the current and stops (or adjusts the mechanical scan speed) when the target dose is reached.

> **Worked example: Faraday-cup dose integration.** A 10¹³ cm⁻² well implant on a 300 mm wafer (area 707 cm²) needs 10¹³ × 707 = 7.07 × 10¹⁵ ions. At one charge each, that is 7.07 × 10¹⁵ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 1.13 × 10⁻³ C, or 1.13 mC. At a beam current of 1 mA the controller reaches that charge in 1.13 s; the tool's time on this wafer is dominated by handling and scanning, not beam. With doubly charged P⁺⁺ (n = 2), each ion carries two charges but delivers one atom, so for the same atom dose the controller must collect twice the charge, 2.26 mC: at a given current, a doubly charged beam delivers atoms at half the rate. That is why the equation divides by n.

Absolute accuracy is ~1–2 % and repeatability tool-to-tool is a few tenths of a percent, which is why implant is the reference process for dose. The sources of error are exactly the things listed above: neutrals (not counted), secondary electrons (if they escape the cup they count as extra positive current, so the cup is biased to keep them in), charge exchange, and beam scattered off apertures.

### Wafer charging and the flood gun

The beam is a current of positive charge landing on a surface that is largely insulating: photoresist and oxide do not conduct. Charge piles up like static on a rubbed balloon, and the surface potential of an isolated gate can rise to tens of volts. That is enough to force current through the **gate oxide** (the 1–2 nm insulator between gate and channel; in modern devices a hafnium-based **high-k** layer, meaning a material with a high dielectric constant that insulates like a thicker oxide, Module 11) and break it down, or damage the high-k stack. Beam currents of tens of mA make this severe. The fix is the **plasma flood gun** (PFG, also called an electron flood): a small xenon or argon plasma source near the wafer that delivers a bath of low-energy (< 5–10 eV) electrons, which are attracted to any positively charged region and neutralise it without having enough energy to charge it negatively. Dedicated charge monitors (CHARM test wafers, which carry arrays of EEPROM cells, electrically erasable non-volatile memory, that record the peak voltage they saw) are used to qualify tools for charging damage.

> **Why it matters downstream: charging damage.** Charging rarely kills a wafer outright. A gate oxide stressed to a few volts during implant carries latent damage: extra leakage through the oxide and a shorter time to breakdown at normal operating voltage. It shows up as elevated gate leakage at wafer test and as early failures in reliability stress (Modules 14 and 18), weeks after the implant, on wafers that passed every in-line check. Flood-gun setup and CHARM qualification are how the implant bay prevents that.

The market is divided by beam current and energy because of the space-charge ceiling of Section 3: the gap that passes 50 mA of arsenic at 40 keV cannot hold off 3 MV, and the column that holds off 3 MV cannot pass 50 mA. No one machine can do everything, so three classes exist, each built around the physics it needs.

**High-current** tools exist because high-dose steps need tens of milliamps, and tens of milliamps can only be extracted at modest voltage through a short gap; they run at low energy and represent ~half of all implanters sold. Applied's VIISta HCP, VIISta Trident and the older Quantum X dominate; Axcelis competes with Purion H and the newer Purion Dragon (the highest-current tool as of ~2025) and its 300 mm ribbon-beam architecture.

**Medium-current** tools exist because well and threshold implants need low dose but a wide energy span and, above all, precision: they have the tightest requirements on angle control (fin implants at multiple tilts) and dose repeatability (a 1 % change in V_T-adjust dose moves V_T by several mV). Their **I/O devices** are the thicker-oxide transistors that talk to the outside world at higher voltage and still need planar-style implants.

**High-energy** tools exist because MeV energies need a linac rather than a DC column (Section 3), which makes them the most expensive class (~$8 M and up). Axcelis has been the historic leader here.

**Ultra-low-energy (ULE)**: the same high-current machines run in deceleration mode to deliver B, BF₂, As or P at 0.2–2 keV for junctions of 5–15 nm. The projected range (mean depth, Section 5) of 500 eV boron is ~3 nm; you cannot make a junction shallower than a few nm this way because of straggle (the spread in depth), channeling tails and the energy-contamination issue discussed above, which is why in-situ doped epitaxy took over the shallowest layers.

**Molecular and cluster implants**: instead of a single boron atom, implant **octadecaborane B₁₈H₂₂** (the "ClusterBoron" technology developed by SemEquip, later part of Ceradyne and now 3M, which supplies the B₁₈H₂₂ source material to implanter vendors) or **carborane C₂B₁₀H₁₂**.

> **Worked example: energy sharing in a cluster ion.** A B₁₈H₂₂⁺ ion has mass 18 × 11 + 22 × 1 = 220 amu. Accelerated to 20 keV, it shares that energy among its atoms in proportion to mass, so each boron atom carries 20 keV × 11/220 = 1.0 keV: an ultra-low-energy boron implant delivered by a 20 keV beam, at a voltage where extraction is easy. And because each ion carries 18 boron atoms but only one charge, a 1 mA cluster beam delivers boron atoms at the rate of an 18 mA atomic beam, at an energy where atomic beams are limited to a few mA by space charge.

The cluster also self-amorphises the surface (no PAI needed), and the carbon in carborane co-implants a diffusion-suppressing species (Section 6). Clusters are used in some DRAM and logic S/D and contact steps but never became universal, partly because the solid sources are awkward and partly because epi took the same applications.

**Cryogenic and hot implants**: the wafer's temperature during the implant decides how much of the damage each ion leaves survives to the end of the implant, because defects are also healing while the beam is on. This healing is **dynamic annealing**: the knocked-out atoms and the empty sites they left finding each other and recombining during the implant itself. Cold freezes that healing out; hot speeds it up. At **−100 °C** (Applied VIISta Trident with a cryogenic chuck) the damage accumulates cleanly: the disordered layer that a heavy implant creates has a sharper bottom edge and fewer defects survive below it, which after the anneal means lower junction leakage. Cryo is used for contact and S/D pre-amorphisation in some logic and DRAM flows. At **+150 to +500 °C** (hot implant; the upper limit is vendor-dependent) the crystal heals as fast as it is damaged and never turns amorphous, which is essential for **FinFETs** (transistors whose channel is the vertical fin of Section 3) and **nanosheets** (whose channel is a stack of thin horizontal silicon sheets), and mandatory for SiC (Section 9). Why a cold implant leaks less, and why a fin must never be amorphised right through, needs the damage physics of Section 6 and the regrowth anneal of Section 7; the mechanism is spelled out at the end of Section 7, once those are in hand.

**Plasma doping (PLAD)**: no beamline at all. The wafer sits on a chuck pulsed to −0.5 to −10 kV (a vendor-dependent range) in a B₂H₆/H₂, PH₃/H₂ or AsH₃/H₂ plasma. Each negative pulse pushes the plasma's electrons away from the wafer and leaves a sheath (the same boundary layer as at the ion source, now wrapped around the whole wafer) across which the full pulse voltage drops; positive ions at the sheath edge fall through it into the wafer, so the pulse voltage sets the energy the way the column does in a beamline, and the number of pulses times the current per pulse sets the dose. The ions arrive with a spread of angles rather than as a parallel beam, because they scatter off gas molecules on the way through the sheath, and the plasma also deposits a thin dopant-rich film on every surface that the bombardment then knocks in. The result is **conformal** doping, following every surface, reaching the sidewalls of fins and the walls of the deep contact holes in DRAM (dynamic random-access memory, the main-memory chip) that a directional beam can only shadow. Because the whole wafer is implanted at once with no scanning, doses of 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻² take seconds and throughput is high. Applied's **VIISta PLAD** is the main production tool, adopted first in DRAM (polysilicon plug and buried-channel doping) and used for some FinFET extension and 3D NAND (the flash memory in solid-state drives) applications. Conformal doping by PLAD and by dopant layers deposited by **ALD** (atomic layer deposition, which lays down one atomic layer per gas pulse and so coats sidewalls perfectly, Module 6) followed by a drive-in are the competing answers to "how do you dope the sides of a 5 nm-wide fin?"

> **What can go wrong: plasma doping has no mass analysis.** There is no analyser magnet, so everything in the plasma goes into the wafer: the dopant, but also hydrogen from the carrier gas and fluorine or hydrogen from the source molecule, which alter the damage and diffusion behaviour. There is no Faraday cup either; dose is inferred from the chuck current, which includes secondary electrons and the current of every ion species, so the dopant dose is a calibrated estimate rather than a count. The engineer's defence is to calibrate PLAD against SIMS on monitor wafers for each recipe (Section 10) and to keep the plasma chemistry fixed; a drift in the hydrogen fraction shows up as a shift in sheet resistance (the resistance of the doped layer, Section 7) after anneal with no change in chuck current.

## 5. Where the Ions Stop: Range Statistics

> **Intuition: bowling ball or bullet.** An ion entering silicon can lose energy in two ways. A heavy, slow ion is a bowling ball rolling into pins: it bounces off silicon nuclei in big deflections, the pins fly (atoms are knocked off their sites), and it stops after a short, zigzag path. A light, fast ion is a bullet through syrup: it feels a smooth drag from the electron cloud, barely deflects, displaces almost nothing, and travels far. Every ion does some of both, and the mix decides both how deep it goes and how much damage it leaves.

An ion entering silicon loses energy by two mechanisms. **Nuclear stopping**: elastic collisions with silicon nuclei, which dominates for heavy ions and at low energies, scatters the ion through large angles and displaces silicon atoms, producing damage. **Electronic stopping**: inelastic drag against the electron cloud, which dominates for light ions and at high energies (above ~10 keV per amu), is nearly straight-line and produces no displacement damage. **LSS theory** (Lindhard, Scharff and Schiøtt, 1963) put both on a common footing and gives the **projected range** R_p (mean depth of the stopped ions), the **projected straggle** ΔR_p (standard deviation in depth), the **lateral straggle** ΔR_⊥ (the sideways spread) and the corrections that turn a symmetric bell curve into the skewed distributions used in process simulators.

The **dose Q** counts implanted ions per unit wafer area. A **concentration** counts them per unit volume. To get from one to the other, we must describe how that dose is distributed with depth: the same dose spread through a thicker region has a lower peak concentration. In the expression below, x is depth beneath the surface, N(x) is concentration at that depth, N_p is its peak, R_p is the mean stopping depth, and ΔR_p measures the spread. Use consistent length units when combining them with dose.

To first order the as-implanted profile is a Gaussian, a bell curve centred at R_p:

N(x) = N_p · exp[−(x − R_p)² / 2ΔR_p²], with peak N_p = Q / (√(2π)·ΔR_p) ≈ 0.4·Q/ΔR_p.

In words: the dose Q is spread over a depth of roughly ΔR_p, so the peak concentration is the dose divided by that width. Real profiles are skewed: boron, being light, backscatters and has a tail toward the surface; heavy ions have a tail into the bulk. Everybody uses **SRIM/TRIM** (Ziegler's free program, still the reference, which follows thousands of individual ions collision by collision with randomly chosen impact angles and then averages, a **Monte-Carlo** method) or the calibrated tables inside Synopsys's Sentaurus Process and Silvaco's Victory Process simulators for real numbers. Rules of thumb: boron goes ~3 nm per keV at low energy, arsenic ~1 nm per keV over the same range, and straggle is roughly 40 % of range for light ions and 35 % for heavy ones. Lateral straggle is comparable to ΔR_p, which is why implanted junctions spread under a mask edge by ~0.7 ΔR_p even before any diffusion.

The **metallurgical junction** is the depth x_j at which the implanted concentration equals the background doping of the opposite type, so that the silicon flips from one type to the other; it is what "junction depth" means throughout this module.

> **Worked example: from dose to peak concentration and junction depth.** A p⁺ extension is implanted with ¹¹B⁺ at 10 keV to a dose Q = 1 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻² into an n-well doped N_B = 1 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³. From the table R_p ≈ 37 nm and ΔR_p ≈ 17 nm = 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ cm. The as-implanted peak is N_p ≈ 0.4 × 10¹⁵ / 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 2.4 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³, right at boron's solubility limit, which is why 10¹⁵ cm⁻² is the canonical "high dose." The metallurgical junction is where N(x) = N_B: solve exp[−(x_j − R_p)²/2ΔR_p²] = N_B/N_p, giving x_j = R_p + ΔR_p·√(2·ln(N_p/N_B)) = 37 + 17 × √(2 × ln 240) = 37 + 17 × 3.31 ≈ 93 nm. The inverse problem is the same equation: if a device needs x_j = 15 nm at N_B = 10¹⁸ and the dose is 10¹⁵, you need ΔR_p ≈ 3 nm and R_p ≈ 5 nm, i.e. boron at well under 1 keV, or a molecular ion, or epi. Anneal then adds √(Dt)-scale broadening on top of this (strictly, the Gaussian's width grows by √(2Dt); the factor √2 is not worth carrying, and √(Dt) is the number to remember), and the TED effects of the next section can add tens of nanometres more.

> **What can go wrong: three ways a real profile is not the Gaussian.** First, a **channeling tail** (Section 3): if the tilt, twist or screen oxide is off, a few percent of ions run down a crystal corridor and land several times deeper than R_p, and the junction depth is set by that tail, not by the table. Second, **energy contamination** in decel mode (Section 3) adds a tail at the pre-decel energy for the same reason. Third, **simulator density mismatch**: SRIM assumes the target density you type in, and an error of a few percent in the density of a screen oxide or hard mask shifts R_p by the same few percent, which on a shallow junction is a visible fraction of the depth. The engineer catches all three the same way: a SIMS profile on a monitor wafer (Section 10) compared against the model before a recipe is released.

## 6. Damage, Amorphisation and Transient Enhanced Diffusion

Each 10 keV boron ion displaces on the order of a hundred silicon atoms; each 50 keV arsenic ion displaces around a thousand. The ion does not do this alone: the first silicon atom it strikes flies off with enough energy to knock out others, which knock out others in turn, so one ion sets off a branching tree of collisions called a **damage cascade**, a few tens of nanometres across, and most of the displacements are made by recoiling silicon atoms rather than by the ion itself. Every displaced atom leaves a vacancy (an empty lattice site) behind and becomes an interstitial (an atom in the aisle between sites), in the car-park picture of Section 1. The damage is characterised by **displacements per atom (dpa)**, the average number of times each atom in the implanted volume has been knocked off its site (0.1 dpa means one atom in ten has been displaced once), or by the deposited nuclear energy density, the energy per unit volume that went into collisions with nuclei rather than into electronic drag. When that density exceeds ~10²¹ keV/cm³, the "critical damage density", the silicon collapses into an **amorphous** layer, one in which the atoms have lost their regular arrangement altogether, like glass compared with quartz. The two measures connect like this: 10²¹ keV/cm³ shared among 5 × 10²² atoms is ~20 eV per atom, about the ~15 eV it takes to knock one silicon atom permanently off its site (the **displacement energy**); most of that energy ends up as heat and most displaced atoms fall straight back, so the net is roughly 10 % of the atoms displaced (~0.1 dpa) at the moment the lattice gives up its order. At room temperature the **amorphisation threshold dose** is ~1–3 × 10¹⁴ cm⁻² for Ge, As and Sb, ~5 × 10¹⁴–10¹⁵ for P and Si, and above 10¹⁶ for boron, which is so light that its cascades are sparse (a few widely spaced displacements rather than a dense tangle) and heal by dynamic annealing before neighbouring cascades overlap. A high-dose As or Ge implant leaves a continuous amorphous layer from the surface to roughly R_p + 2ΔR_p, with a damaged-but-crystalline region below it.

Below the amorphous/crystalline (a/c) interface lies the **end-of-range (EOR)** damage: an excess of silicon **interstitials** left because the amorphous layer above contains all the vacancies and the tail ions knocked interstitials deeper. On annealing these interstitials cluster first into small interstitial clusters, then into rod-like **{311} defects** (interstitials condensed into rods lying on one particular family of crystal planes, visible in a **transmission electron microscope (TEM)**, which images a thinned slice of the crystal at atomic scale, as lines ~10–100 nm long), and, if the dose was high enough, into **dislocation loops**: ring-shaped seams in the crystal where an extra disc of atoms has been inserted, which persist to 1,000 °C and above. EOR dislocation loops sitting inside a junction's depletion region are a source of leakage (the current that flows through a reverse-biased junction that is supposed to be off, Section 1) and a classic yield killer.

The interstitials are also the mechanism of **transient enhanced diffusion (TED)**, the most consequential piece of physics in implant annealing. Boron and phosphorus barely move on their own; a boron atom only hops when a silicon interstitial bumps it off its site, and the more interstitials there are, the more bumps. The equilibrium interstitial concentration at 900 °C is tiny, so equilibrium diffusion is slow. An implant changes that. Almost all of the vacancies and interstitials an ion creates find each other and recombine in the first moments of the anneal, but the implanted atom itself is one extra atom for which the lattice has no bay, so roughly one excess interstitial per implanted ion survives (the "+1 model"). These gather in {311} defects, and as those dissolve at 700–950 °C over seconds to minutes, they raise the interstitial **supersaturation** (the excess over the equilibrium concentration) by 10³–10⁴ and boron diffusivity with it. A 10¹⁴ cm⁻² boron implant annealed at 800 °C for 10 min can move ~50 nm, while equilibrium diffusion would predict less than 2 nm.

> **Worked example: how far TED moves boron.** Boron's equilibrium diffusivity at 800 °C is D ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁷ cm²/s (from the Arrhenius law of Section 2 with E_a ≈ 3.5 eV). For a 10-minute anneal, √(Dt) = √(4 × 10⁻¹⁷ × 600) = √(2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴) ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻⁷ cm = 1.6 nm: the boron should barely move. Now let the {311} defects raise the interstitial supersaturation, and with it D, by a factor of 10³. Since the wander distance goes as the square root of D, the distance grows by √1,000 ≈ 32, to ~50 nm. That is the observed number, and it is why a 10-minute furnace anneal at a "gentle" 800 °C can ruin a shallow junction that a 1,050 °C spike would have preserved.

The practical comparison is between complete heating schedules, not temperatures alone. Dopants spread while crystal defects assist their motion. A schedule that removes those defects quickly can leave less time for spreading, even if its peak temperature is higher. Keep both questions in mind: how fast can the dopant move, and how long does the defect population keep that motion enhanced?

The effect is *transient* because once the {311}s have emptied, diffusivity relaxes to equilibrium. And it is *worse at lower temperature*, which deserves the full argument, because it is the least intuitive result in this module and the one that shaped every anneal in Section 7. The +1 excess is the same number of interstitials whatever the anneal temperature, so if a hot anneal merely released them faster you might expect the same boron motion compressed into less time. That is not what happens, for two reasons. First, what drives boron is not the excess itself but the supersaturation, the excess *divided by* the equilibrium interstitial concentration, because boron's diffusivity scales with that ratio; and the equilibrium concentration is itself Arrhenius and rises steeply with temperature, by ~10²–10³ between 800 and 1,050 °C. The same excess is therefore a supersaturation of thousands at 800 °C but only tens at 1,050 °C: a much smaller enhancement factor. Second, the excess disappears faster when hot. Interstitials are removed at the wafer surface, which acts as a **sink** because an interstitial that reaches it simply becomes part of the top atomic layer and ceases to exist as a defect; the hotter the anneal, the faster interstitials leave the {311}s and reach the surface. At 800 °C the {311}s release their interstitials slowly over minutes and the surface drains them slowly, so boron sits in a strongly supersaturated bath for minutes; at 1,050 °C the {311}s dissolve and the interstitials reach the surface in a fraction of a second, and the enhancement is smaller the whole time. A smaller enhancement for a shorter time means less total motion, even though boron's equilibrium diffusivity is a thousand times higher hot. This counter-intuitive fact (a low-temperature anneal can broaden the junction more than a high-temperature one) is why the industry moved to ever hotter, ever shorter anneals: spike anneals get through the TED window in under a second at 1,050 °C, dissolving the {311}s and letting the interstitials reach the surface before boron moves far. You can see TED directly by comparing SIMS profiles (Section 10) before and after the anneal: the implanted peak stays where it was, while a low-concentration shoulder marches tens of nanometres deeper and then stops growing once the interstitials are used up.

Boron has a second problem: at concentrations above ~10¹⁹ cm⁻³ in an interstitial-rich environment it forms immobile, inactive **boron-interstitial clusters (BICs)**, which are why an as-implanted 2 × 10²⁰ boron peak might show only 5 × 10¹⁹ active after a low-temperature anneal. Co-implanting **carbon** or **fluorine**, which trap interstitials, and PAI (which puts the boron inside amorphous silicon that regrows with no interstitial excess in the boron layer) are the standard mitigations.

> **What can go wrong: end-of-range loops in the depletion region.** *Symptom*: a junction that leaks when reverse-biased, on a wafer whose sheet resistance and profile look fine. *Cause*: the a/c interface of the PAI or S/D implant was placed at a depth that the junction's depletion region later sweeps across, and the anneal was not hot enough to dissolve the dislocation loops that formed there; each loop is a line of broken bonds that generates carriers inside the high-field zone. *Detection*: cross-section TEM on monitor wafers shows the band of loops and its depth; reverse-bias leakage on test structures at wafer sort (the electrical test of every die, Module 13) shows the electrical consequence. *Fix*: either anneal hotter or longer than the loop-dissolution temperature (a spike anneal before any millisecond step), or change the PAI energy so that the a/c interface, and therefore the loop band, sits either inside the heavily doped region (where there is no field) or well below the depletion region. Cryo implant (Section 4; the mechanism is given at the end of Section 7) reduces the number of loops in the first place.

> **Why it matters downstream: loop leakage and standby power.** A chip with a few billion transistors has a few billion source/drain junctions in parallel, all reverse-biased while the chip idles. A leakage increase of a few picoamps per junction from EOR loops, invisible on a single device, multiplies into milliamps of standby current at the chip level (Modules 11 and 19), which is why junction leakage from implant damage is tracked as a yield and power parameter, not just a device curiosity.

## 7. The Annealing Zoo

The anneal has three jobs: repair the lattice, move the dopants onto substitutional sites, and do so while moving them as little as possible in space. The first two want heat; the third wants none. The way out is in the activation energies: the processes that put dopant onto lattice sites and dissolve damage clusters have activation energies of ~4–5 eV, higher than the ~3.5 eV of dopant diffusion, so raising the temperature speeds activation and repair *more* than it speeds diffusion, and a short time then caps how far the dopant can move. That is why every generation of anneal has been hotter and shorter than the one before: the history of annealing is the substitution of shorter times for lower temperatures.

**Furnace anneal** (800–1,000 °C, 10–60 min, N₂ or N₂/O₂ in a vertical batch furnace from TEL, Kokusai or ASM): the original method. Full repair, high activation, but √(Dt) of 20–100 nm. Still used for well drive-in, where you want the diffusion: a well is implanted as two or three separate Gaussian peaks at different energies (Section 8), and the device needs a single smooth tub of doping with no ripples, so tens of nanometres of diffusion to merge the peaks is the point of the anneal rather than its price, and a furnace is the cheapest way to buy it for a batch of 100+ wafers at once. It is also used for gate-oxide-related steps and for the low-temperature regrowth anneal (SPER, below).

**Rapid thermal anneal (RTA)** (1,000–1,100 °C for 1–30 s): a single wafer on quartz pins in a chamber with banks of tungsten-halogen lamps above it, ramped at 50–150 °C/s, with the temperature measured by a **pyrometer**, a thermometer that reads the wafer's infrared glow, corrected for **emissivity** (how brightly a given surface glows at a given temperature, which changes with the films on the wafer, so the tool must compensate or it misreads). Applied's **Vantage Radiance/RadiancePlus** and **Vantage Vulcan** (which heats the wafer from its unpatterned backside to avoid pattern-dependent lamp absorption) and Mattson's **Helios** (Mattson is now part of Beijing E-Town) are the tools; Screen's **LA-3100** is a flash-lamp annealer used for the millisecond regime described below. RTA dissolves {311} defects, activates dopants close to solid solubility and gives √(Dt) of a few nm, plus TED.

**Spike anneal** is RTA with the soak removed: ramp at 150–300 °C/s to a peak of 1,000–1,100 °C, no dwell (< 1 s within 50 °C of peak), and immediately cool at ~50–100 °C/s (limited by radiative cooling of a 300 mm wafer). This became the standard S/D anneal from the 130 nm to 45 nm nodes (a **node** is one generation of process technology, historically named by its smallest printed feature in nanometres; since ~2010 the number has been a marketing label rather than a measured dimension, but each node is still a distinct, roughly two-year generation); it produces the maximum activation for a given diffusion, for the activation-energy reason given above.

**Millisecond anneal (MSA)**: two flavours, both heating only the top few microns to 1,150–1,350 °C for 0.1–10 ms while the wafer bulk stays at a 400–800 °C preheat. How can a lamp shining on the whole wafer heat only its skin? The light is absorbed in the top few microns, and heat then spreads downward by conduction, which behaves exactly like diffusion: it travels a distance of order √(αt), where α is silicon's thermal diffusivity (~0.1–0.2 cm²/s at these temperatures), so in a 1 ms pulse heat penetrates only on the order of 100 µm and in a 0.1 ms pulse a few tens of microns, against a wafer thickness of ~775 µm. The surface reaches its peak while the back of the wafer never leaves the preheat, and when the pulse ends the hot skin dumps its heat into the cool bulk in the same ~ms, which is what makes the quench so fast. **Flash-lamp anneal** (Mattson **Millios**, Screen **LA-3100**): a xenon arc-lamp bank fires a ~1 ms pulse over the whole wafer. **Laser spike anneal (LSA)** / **dynamic surface anneal (DSA)**: a CO₂ laser (infrared, wavelength 10.6 µm; Veeco's **LSA101**, from the Ultratech acquisition) or a diode laser (Applied's **Vantage Astra DSA**) is focused to a line ~0.1 mm × 10 mm and scanned across the wafer at ~100–500 mm/s, so each point sees a ~0.5 ms triangle at up to ~1,300 °C, the same millisecond dwell as the flash but delivered one strip at a time. Because √(Dt) is well under a nanometre, dopant does not move, and because the peak temperature is close to the melting point (1,414 °C), the solubility limit is briefly very high; the quench (the rapid cooling) freezes in **metastable super-activation**, e.g. boron active above 2 × 10²⁰ and phosphorus above 5 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³. The drawback is that MSA does not fully dissolve EOR loops (the wafer bulk is too cold), so it is usually preceded by a spike anneal or used on PAI/SPER material; and the extreme temperature gradient (1,000 °C across microns) causes **slip** (planes of the crystal sliding past each other under thermal stress, leaving line defects that show up as bright lines near the wafer edge under inspection), pattern-dependent heating (dense metal and poly areas absorb differently), and wafer-to-wafer variation. Pattern effects are mitigated by absorber layers or by choosing a wavelength where silicon and dielectrics (insulators) absorb similarly. Since about 2015 MSA has been used mainly for contact-level activation rather than S/D: activating the top of the phosphorus-doped silicon (**Si:P**) and boron-doped silicon-germanium (**SiGe:B**) source/drain layers that are grown by epitaxy (Section 8) where the metal contact will land.

**Solid-phase epitaxial regrowth (SPER)** (500–650 °C, minutes): an amorphous layer on crystalline silicon recrystallises epitaxially from the a/c interface upward, each atomic layer copying the crystal below it, at a rate that is Arrhenius with E_a ≈ 2.7 eV: ~1 nm/min at 500 °C, ~10 nm/min at 550 °C and ~50 nm/min at 600 °C for (100) silicon, so a 30 nm PAI layer regrows in a few minutes at 550 °C or under a minute at 600 °C. Dopants in the amorphous layer are swept onto substitutional sites at the regrowth front at concentrations far above equilibrium solubility (boron > 10²⁰ at 600 °C), with essentially no diffusion because the temperature is so low. The catch is the EOR defects just below the original a/c interface, which SPER leaves untouched, and the fact that any part of the amorphous layer that lacks a crystalline template below it (a fully amorphised fin, or an amorphous region touching the oxide sidewall of an isolation trench) regrows **polycrystalline**, as a patchwork of small crystals in random orientations rather than one crystal. Low-temperature SPER is central to **3D-sequential** and monolithic-3D integration, in which a second layer of transistors is built on top of finished wiring that cannot survive above ~500 °C.

**Why implant temperature matters: cryo and hot implants, revisited.** With end-of-range damage (Section 6) and SPER in hand, the two temperature modes of Section 4 explain themselves. A cryogenic implant at −100 °C freezes out dynamic annealing, so the damage accumulates cleanly: the amorphous/crystalline interface is sharper and, crucially, the crystalline region just below it contains fewer residual defects, because at room temperature the partial healing during the implant leaves a broad, half-damaged transition zone stocked with interstitials. After SPER, fewer end-of-range interstitials remain below the regrown layer to condense into the dislocation loops of Section 6, so a junction whose depletion region sweeps that depth leaks less; that is why cryo was introduced for the contact and S/D pre-amorphisation implants, whose a/c interface sits closest to a junction. A hot implant at 150–500 °C does the opposite on purpose: the crystal heals as fast as it is damaged and never amorphises. That matters for a fin because SPER needs a crystalline template to copy. A fin 5–6 nm wide that is amorphised right through has no seed left except its base, and regrowth from a single narrow base comes back as a mess of **twins** (regrowth mistakes in which the lattice comes back mirror-imaged across a plane) and polycrystalline grains; hot implant keeps the fin crystalline throughout, so there is nothing to regrow. It is the same reason SiC is implanted at ~500 °C (Section 9): amorphised SiC does not regrow in the right crystal form at all.

**Microwave and other low-temperature anneals** are research-stage; **laser melt anneal** (nanosecond UV pulses such as Screen's **LT-3100** from its LASSE affiliate, melting the top ~20 nm and re-solidifying at ~1 m/s) gives box-like, fully active profiles and is used in some image-sensor backside and SiC contact processes but not in mainstream logic.

> **What can go wrong: anneal failure modes.** *Spike under-activation*: a peak temperature tens of degrees low (a pyrometer misled by an emissivity change, or a lamp bank ageing) leaves dopant clustered and inactive; the symptom is a high sheet resistance on the post-anneal monitor (Section 10), and the fix is emissivity recalibration and lamp maintenance. *MSA slip*: the 1,000 °C gradient across microns produces thermal stress (hot silicon wants to expand and the cool silicon beside it will not let it) that exceeds the crystal's **yield strength**, the stress beyond which a solid deforms permanently instead of springing back, near the wafer edge, and slip lines appear on inspection; the preheat temperature and pulse shape are tuned to stay under the threshold. *MSA pattern effects*: dense metal or poly regions absorb the pulse differently from open areas, so the same recipe gives different peak temperatures across a die (one chip's area on the wafer), and matched transistors come out mismatched; absorber layers or wavelength choice are the mitigations. *MSA leaving EOR loops*: because the bulk never gets hot, a flow that uses only MSA after a PAI keeps its dislocation loops and leaks (Section 6); the fix is a spike anneal first. *SPER without a seed*: if a fin was amorphised through its full width, or the amorphous layer touches an isolation sidewall, the regrowth has no crystal to copy and comes back polycrystalline, and the device is scrap; hot implant (Section 4) or a shallower PAI prevents it.

> **Worked example: sheet resistance from dose and mobility.** **Sheet resistance** is the resistance measured across any square patch of a thin layer, whatever the size of the square (a bigger square is wider, which lowers the resistance, but also longer, which raises it, and the two cancel); it is written Ω/sq (ohms per square). A strip that is 10 squares long, such as a 1 µm-long, 0.1 µm-wide extension, has 10 times the sheet resistance end to end, so a 140 Ω/sq layer contributes 140 × 10 = 1,400 Ω to that transistor. A 5 × 10¹⁴ cm⁻² arsenic S/D implant is spike-annealed and fully activated. Sheet resistance is R_s = 1/(q·Q_active·μ), where Q_active is the active dose per cm² and μ the average mobility over the layer. At ~10²⁰ cm⁻³ electron mobility is ~90 cm²/V·s, so R_s = 1/(1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 5 × 10¹⁴ × 90) = 1/(7.2 × 10⁻³) ≈ 140 Ω/sq. If a four-point probe (a four-needle probe, Section 10) measures 280 Ω/sq the activation is ~50 %, and the process engineer knows to look at clustering or a cold anneal. For a 10¹⁵ boron extension with μ_p ≈ 50 cm²/V·s, full activation gives ~125 Ω/sq; real as-annealed ULE boron extensions in the 32 nm era ran 400–800 Ω/sq at 15 nm junction depth, a direct measure of how far short of full activation they fell. The general figure of merit is R_s versus x_j: every junction-engineering paper of 2000–2015 plotted its results on that chart, with the goal of pushing toward the lower-left corner of < 500 Ω/sq at < 10 nm.

### Keeping junctions under 10 nm

Putting the pieces together, a sub-10 nm junction in silicon needs: an implant whose R_p + 2ΔR_p is under ~8 nm (sub-keV B or a cluster, or As/P at 1–3 keV); Ge PAI so there is no channeling tail and the boron ends up in amorphous material; carbon or fluorine co-implant to suppress TED; and an anneal that is either SPER-only (600 °C) or MSA-only (1,300 °C, ms) with √(Dt) under 1 nm. Even then, control of the *shape* of the profile is poor compared to epitaxy, in which every layer's thickness and concentration is set by gas flow at 500–700 °C with no damage. This is why the abrupt, highest-concentration layers in a modern transistor are grown rather than implanted.

**Wells and isolation.** Still 100 % implant. After **STI** (shallow trench isolation: oxide-filled trenches etched between devices to fence them apart), n-wells (phosphorus at ~100–500 keV in two or three energies, ~10¹³ cm⁻² each) and p-wells (boron at ~50–300 keV) are implanted through a photoresist mask 1–2 µm thick, producing retrograde profiles with the peak ~0.3–0.6 µm below the surface. Deep n-wells for isolated p-wells (a **triple well**, which lets an n-channel device sit in a p-well that is itself isolated from the p-type wafer by an n-type layer beneath) need phosphorus at 1–2 MeV. A high-energy or medium-current tool does these at 10¹²–10¹³ cm⁻² and 200–400 wph, so well implants are cheap per wafer but numerous: with several device flavours (core, I/O, SRAM, analogue, and the ESD protection diodes that shunt static discharges from a pin away from the core) a flow may have 10–15 well and well-related masks.

**Anti-punch-through / punch-through stopper.** In FinFETs, a heavily doped (~10¹⁸–10¹⁹ cm⁻³) layer at the base of the fin stops source-to-drain leakage under the channel. It is placed by a medium-energy implant before fin patterning (so the fin above is undoped) or after, with the fin's own top protected. GAA flows are moving toward a **bottom dielectric isolation** layer, an insulator inserted under the sheet stack that blocks the leakage path outright and removes this implant.

**Channel and V_T adjust: mostly gone.** A planar transistor set its threshold with a channel implant of ~10¹⁷–10¹⁸. In a fin 5–6 nm wide, or a nanosheet 5 nm thick, that concentration means a handful of atoms per device.

> **Worked example: random dopant fluctuation and fin shadowing.** A channel 20 × 20 × 20 nm doped at 10¹⁸ cm⁻³: the volume is (2 × 10⁻⁶ cm)³ = 8 × 10⁻¹⁸ cm³, so it contains 10¹⁸ × 8 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 8 dopant atoms. Atoms land at random, and a count of N random events has a statistical spread of √N, so 8 ± √8 ≈ 8 ± 2.8, or ±35 %: some transistors get 5 dopants, some 11, and their threshold voltages differ accordingly. This is **random dopant fluctuation (RDF)**, and no implanter, however precise its dose, can fix it; only removing the dopant does. Geometry adds a second problem for anything that is implanted into a fin: by the fin-shadow arithmetic of Section 3, a 1° tilt error changes the shadowed length of a fin sidewall by ~15 %, so the dose reaching the base of the sidewall depends on angle to a degree that a planar surface never did.

Modern channels are therefore undoped (< 10¹⁶), and V_T is set by the **work function** of the metal gate stack: the energy needed to pull an electron out of a metal, which differs from metal to metal, so choosing the metal shifts V_T without any dopant (TiN, TiAl, TaN thicknesses, and lanthanum or aluminium **dipole layers**, atomically thin films at the high-k interface that add a fixed voltage step; Module 11). This gives 3–4 V_T flavours per polarity with no implant. A few residual "V_T trim" implants survive in some flows, at very low dose.

**Halo / pocket: essentially gone.** The planar-era tilted (20–45°) quad implant that put a pocket of counter-doping around the extension tip to fight **short-channel effects** (the loss of gate control as source and drain get close enough to influence the channel themselves) has no place in a **fully depleted** fin or sheet, and RDF would kill it anyway. Fully depleted means the fin or sheet is so thin that the gate's field reaches all the way through it and sweeps out every mobile carrier when the transistor is off (the whole body becomes a depletion region, in the language of Section 1), so the body needs no dopant of its own to stay off; the gate does with geometry what the planar era did with a channel implant and a halo, and that is the reason the whole planar doping recipe could be abandoned.

**Extensions.** FinFETs still typically use a low-energy, tilted or PLAD extension implant (As/P at 1–5 keV, B at < 1 keV, ~10¹⁴–10¹⁵ cm⁻²), often at hot-implant conditions to keep the fin crystalline, to define the junction under the **spacer**, the insulating sidewall on the gate that sets how far from the gate edge the implant lands. In many GAA flows the extension is instead formed by out-diffusion from the in-situ doped S/D epi during a short anneal, and the implant disappears.

**Source/drain: mostly replaced by epi.** Intel led the change with embedded SiGe at its 90 nm node for PMOS (the p-channel transistor), and NMOS (n-channel) followed around the 22 nm-class generation; since then the deep S/D has been a recessed, **selectively** grown epitaxial layer: the silicon where the S/D will go is etched out to make a recess, and the growth chemistry is tuned so that crystal grows only on the exposed silicon in the recess and not on the surrounding oxide and nitride, which is what "selective" means. For PMOS it is SiGe:B (Section 7), silicon-germanium with 30–55 % Ge and boron at 10²⁰ to over 10²¹ cm⁻³ chemical; for NMOS Si:P (or Si:CP, with carbon added) with phosphorus at ~3–5 × 10²⁰ active and > 10²¹ chemical, grown at ~550–700 °C in Applied Centura or ASM Intrepid **reduced-pressure CVD** reactors (chemical vapour deposition below atmospheric pressure, Module 6). Epi gives higher active concentration than any implant-plus-anneal, no damage, and **strain**: the SiGe lattice is larger than silicon's, so it squeezes the channel (a **stressor**), and squeezing or stretching the channel crystal makes holes or electrons move faster. Implant still does S/D in I/O transistors, ESD devices and resistors, and some flows retain a light "S/D cap" implant.

**Contact implants.** The metal plug does not touch bare silicon: a thin layer of titanium is reacted with the silicon surface to form a **silicide**, a metal-silicon compound (here titanium silicide, TiSi_x) that bonds intimately to the crystal, and the plug lands on that. The interface between the S/D epi and that silicide is the biggest resistance in the transistor; **contact resistivity** targets are ~1–2 × 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² (resistance times contact area, so that the resistance of a given contact is this number divided by its area). Where a metal meets silicon an energy step forms, the **Schottky barrier**, which electrons must climb over; dope the silicon above ~10²⁰ cm⁻³ and the barrier becomes so thin that electrons **tunnel** straight through it, quantum-mechanically, instead of climbing. Standard practice is a Ge PAI plus a very high dose, very low energy dopant implant (P or As at 1–3 keV, B or Ga at < 1 keV, 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻²) into the contact hole, followed by MSA or SPER, so that a metastably super-activated layer sits right at the metal interface to thin the barrier. This is now the single most demanding implant in the flow, and cryo implant was introduced for it.

> **Why it matters downstream: contact resistance and clock speed.** A transistor's drive current is set by the voltage that actually reaches its channel, which is the supply voltage minus the drop across every series resistance in the path, and the contact is the largest of them. At 1–2 × 10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² a contact a few hundred square nanometres in area is already a few hundred ohms (10⁻⁹ Ω·cm² divided by 4 × 10⁻¹² cm²); let activation slip and it doubles. Lower drive current means slower gates, and a GPU or CPU built from a few billion of them loses clock frequency or must raise its voltage and burn more power (Module 19). The super-activated contact implant is therefore one of the few implants that shows up directly in a product's headline specification.

**Poly and dummy gate.** In a gate-last flow (RMG: a polysilicon placeholder gate is used through the hot steps and then removed and replaced by metal after the anneals, so that the metal stack never sees them) the polysilicon dummy gate is sometimes implanted to tune its etch and removal behaviour; in older gate-first and I/O-gate flows the poly gate is heavily doped (P or B, ~10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻²) to avoid poly depletion (Section 1).

**Everything else.** Pre-amorphisation before silicide formation (so the silicide grows uniformly on a disordered surface rather than following crystal facets), resistor implants (undoped-poly resistors trimmed with a light implant), ESD and analogue devices, and a growing list of "fix" implants such as nitrogen or fluorine into gate dielectrics.

> **What can go wrong: implanting a patterned wafer.** *Fin shadowing*: at 7° tilt, one side of every fin sits in the shadow of its neighbour for the first ~6 nm of height (the Section 3 number), so dense fin arrays and isolated fins receive different sidewall doses; the fix is quad rotation and, where possible, smaller tilt or PLAD. *Resist outgassing and reticulation*: at doses of 10¹⁵ cm⁻² and above the resist absorbs kilojoules, releases gas that raises beamline pressure (more charge exchange, more energy contamination) and, if the wafer passes ~100 °C, wrinkles and cracks, letting ions through where they should not go; the fixes are backside cooling, resist hard-bake, and splitting the dose over passes. *Well-mask misalignment*: a well mask offset by tens of nanometres puts well dopant under the edge of the wrong device, shifting its threshold and its leakage; it is caught by overlay metrology (Module 7) before the implant, and by electrical test of edge devices afterwards.

The pattern: the high-current, high-dose implants that defined the 1990s (S/D, poly) have moved to epi; the ones that remain are the wells (medium/high energy, low dose, many masks), the contacts (highest dose, lowest energy), and a scatter of device-specific tuning implants. Implanter *count* per fab has stayed roughly flat while the implanters have become more specialised.

## 9. Implants Beyond Logic

**CMOS image sensors (CIS)**, the chips behind every phone camera, rely on deep, high-energy implants more than anything else. Each pixel is a **photodiode**, a p-n junction that converts light into charge, and it must be deep to collect the charge from red light, which penetrates several microns into silicon. The photodiode and its isolation are formed by boron and phosphorus implants from ~1 to 8 MeV (and up to 15 MeV for the largest pixels), through photoresist masks 5–10 µm thick, to create p-wells and n-wells several microns deep for full-depth charge collection and pixel-to-pixel isolation. The depths follow from the range table of Section 5: phosphorus at 2 MeV stops at ~2 µm, and scaling up, 8 MeV phosphorus reaches roughly 5–6 µm and 8 MeV boron, being lighter and less easily stopped, roughly 10 µm, which is the whole thickness a photodiode needs to collect red light. The mask has to be thick for the same reason: to block an ion it must be thicker than the ion's range in resist, and resist, at about half the density of silicon, stops ions roughly 1.5× less effectively per micron, so a 5–6 µm silicon range calls for ~8–9 µm of resist. Resist that thick is hard to pattern and hard to keep standing, which is why these masks are among the few in a fab that are measured in microns. This is the reason the Axcelis Purion XEmax exists.

**DRAM** uses implants for the peripheral CMOS (the logic around the memory array, which sees the same well, extension and contact implants as a logic chip), for the array wells (medium- and high-energy implants at ~10¹³ cm⁻², like logic wells, that isolate the array and set the body of the cell transistors), and historically for the cell transistors themselves. The **buried-channel** array transistor (whose channel runs along the wall of a trench etched into the silicon, to save area) and the polysilicon **contact plugs** that connect each cell to its bit line (the wire that reads it) are doped by PLAD in several flows, because a directional beam cannot reach the walls of holes that are many times deeper than they are wide; PLAD delivers its 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻² plug doses in seconds (Section 4). DRAM is the second-largest implanter market after logic.

**3D NAND** needs relatively few implants: the memory strings are **charge-trap** devices, storing their bits as charge in an insulating nitride layer rather than in doped silicon, and the vertical channel is undoped polysilicon. But its CMOS periphery, deep triple wells and a few high-energy well implants through the thick layers of the finished stack still consume several MeV-class steps per wafer; the range table of Section 5 says what reaching through microns of material takes (phosphorus needs ~2 MeV to reach ~2 µm in silicon).

**Power devices in silicon carbide (SiC)** are the fastest-growing implant application of the early 2020s and the one with the strangest physics. SiC is a **polytype** material: it stacks its atomic layers in several different repeating orders, of which 4H-SiC is the one used for devices and 3C-SiC is a different order that forms if the crystal regrows wrongly. Dopant diffusion in SiC is negligible below 1,800 °C, so *every* doped region in a SiC MOSFET (p-wells, n⁺ sources, p⁺ contacts, and the **junction termination extension**, a lightly doped ring at the device edge that spreads the electric field so the edge does not break down first) must be implanted to its final shape. Aluminium (p-type) and nitrogen (n-type) are implanted at up to ~500 keV–1 MeV for the deep wells and at doses up to 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ cm⁻² for contacts, and the wafer is held at **~500 °C** during implant because amorphised SiC does not regrow epitaxially in the correct polytype and becomes a permanent 3C-SiC/defect region. Annealing is at **1,600–1,800 °C** in argon, where the bare SiC surface would sublime (turn straight from solid to vapour) and **step-bunch** (its atomic-height surface steps merge into tall terraces, roughening the surface the gate oxide must later be grown on), so it is capped with **carbon** (a photoresist layer baked into graphite at ~800 °C, later removed in an oxygen plasma). Axcelis reportedly holds ~70 % or more of the SiC implant market with its Purion Power series (Purion M SiC, Purion H200 SiC, Purion EXE SiC, Purion XE SiC); SiC demand drove the boom and the subsequent fall-back in Axcelis's revenue described in Section 12.

> **What can go wrong: SiC implant and anneal.** *Incomplete aluminium activation*: even at 1,700 °C only a fraction of the implanted Al is electrically active, and a cooler or shorter anneal leaves p-wells and contacts more resistive than designed; it is caught by sheet-resistance and contact-resistance test structures. *Cap failure*: if the carbon cap is too thin or cracks, the surface sublimes and step-bunches under it, and the roughened surface gives a poor gate oxide and low channel mobility; **atomic-force microscopy (AFM)**, which drags a nanometre-sharp tip across the surface and records its height, resolving single atomic steps, is the check after cap removal. *Wafer too cold during implant*: if the heated chuck under-reads, the high-dose contact regions amorphise, regrow as 3C-SiC inclusions and stay defective through any anneal; TEM on monitor wafers shows the inclusions, and the fix is chuck temperature calibration.

**Gallium nitride (GaN)**, the other wide-band-gap semiconductor used for power and radio-frequency devices, is by contrast barely implant-doped at all: activating implanted magnesium, the p-type dopant, needs > 1,300 °C under pressure, and implant-doped GaN devices are not in volume production as of 2025. That is why GaN devices are grown with all their doping in place.

**Silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers**, in which a thin single-crystal silicon layer sits on a buried oxide instead of on bulk silicon, are made by the **Smart Cut** process, an implant that has nothing to do with doping: a hydrogen (or H + He co-) implant of ~5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻² at 30–200 keV into an oxidised donor wafer, which is then bonded to a handle wafer (a second wafer that becomes the mechanical support) and heated to 400–600 °C, whereupon the hydrogen gathers into **platelets**, flat, disc-shaped hydrogen-filled bubbles a few nanometres across lying parallel to the surface at the implant depth, which grow as more hydrogen diffuses into them, link up into micro-cracks and finally into one continuous crack, and the wafer splits along it, leaving a thin silicon layer on the buried oxide (Module 3). At 5 × 10¹⁶ this is one of the highest doses in the industry (the worked example in Section 12 shows it takes over 20 minutes of beam per wafer), and dedicated high-current hydrogen implanters (Soitec buys from both Applied and Axcelis) run continuously.

**Other**: MEMS (micro-electromechanical systems such as accelerometers) and photonics (germanium implant for waveguide photodetectors), oxygen implant for **SIMOX** (separation by implanted oxygen: an oxygen dose of ~10¹⁸ cm⁻² followed by a high-temperature anneal that turns the buried oxygen-rich layer into a continuous oxide in place, an obsolete way to make SOI that Smart Cut displaced), nitrogen implant into gate oxide, and xenon or argon implants used purely as damage tools.

## 10. Metrology

Because dose and depth are invisible optically, implant metrology is a mix of destructive profiling and fast indirect monitors. Most of it is done on **monitor wafers**: unpatterned wafers run through the tool alongside product just to be measured.

In production the three main monitors form a chain. The ThermaProbe optical monitor reads every implant lot's monitor wafer before the anneal, within minutes of the implant, and catches a wrong dose, a wrong energy or a drifting beam angle while the implanter can still be stopped. The four-point probe reads the same monitor after the anneal and catches an anneal that under-activated. SIMS is run when a recipe is set up, and again whenever the two fast monitors disagree, because it is the only one of the three that shows the depth profile itself.

**Secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS)** is the reference for depth profiles. A Cs⁺ or O₂⁺ primary beam at 0.25–5 keV **sputters** (knocks surface atoms off with fast ions, digging downward) a crater a few hundred µm across while a mass spectrometer counts the sputtered dopant ions; depth is calibrated by measuring the crater with a **profilometer**, a fine stylus dragged across the surface that records its depth. Detection limits are ~10¹⁴–10¹⁶ cm⁻³ depending on species and matrix (the host material), depth resolution ~1–2 nm at low primary energy, and a profile takes minutes to hours. Cameca (Ametek) IMS Wf/SC Ultra and Physical Electronics ADEPT-1010 are the fab tools; SIMS is done on monitor wafers, never product, and the first few nm are unreliable because of the sputtering transient (the sputter rate takes a few nanometres to settle). SIMS measures *chemical* concentration, not active concentration.

**Four-point probe (4PP)** measures sheet resistance: four in-line tungsten-carbide probes on a monitor wafer, current through the outer pair, voltage across the inner pair, R_s = (π/ln 2)·(V/I) = 4.532·V/I for a thin layer, mapped at 49–121 sites in a minute. The factor π/ln 2 is pure geometry: current injected at a point into a thin sheet spreads out radially, so the voltage falls off logarithmically with distance from each outer probe, and integrating that between the two inner probes gives ln 2 over π. The formula holds when the probe spacing (~1 mm) is much larger than the layer thickness, so the current is confined to the sheet, and much smaller than the wafer, so the edges do not matter; near a wafer edge a correction is applied. It reads active dose times mobility, so it is the standard post-anneal monitor. Its limitation is that probes punch through ultra-shallow junctions; the non-contact **junction photovoltage (RsL)** technique (a light spot generates carriers in the doped layer, which set up a small voltage across the junction beneath it; that voltage spreads sideways through the layer and is sensed without contact by capacitive electrodes at two distances from the spot, and how fast it falls off with distance depends on the layer's sheet resistance) and micro-4PP (developed by CAPRES, a Danish company acquired by KLA in 2019) were developed for junctions under 20 nm.

> **Worked example: from a probe reading to activation.** A 4PP on the monitor wafer from the arsenic S/D anneal of Section 7 reads V/I = 30.9 Ω. R_s = 4.532 × 30.9 = 140 Ω/sq, which is exactly the fully activated value calculated there for 5 × 10¹⁴ cm⁻² of arsenic at μ ≈ 90 cm²/V·s, so the anneal did its job. On the next lot the reading is 61.8 Ω, so R_s = 280 Ω/sq: twice the resistance means half the active dose times mobility, i.e. ~50 % activation, and the engineer looks for a cold anneal (a pyrometer or lamp fault) before the lot moves on. Since the anneal, not the implanter, is the usual suspect when ThermaProbe on the same lot was normal, the two monitors together point at the culprit.

**Modulated optical reflectance ("Thermawave")**: KLA's **ThermaProbe** (from the Therma-Wave acquisition) measures the as-implanted, un-annealed wafer. A pump laser heats a tiny spot on and off about a million times a second; the heat and the excited electrons spread outward from the spot as ripples, and lattice damage slows both, which changes how much of a second, probe laser is reflected. The change is only parts per million, but it grows with damage, and damage grows with dose, so a calibrated reading maps to dose with ~0.5 % repeatability for 10¹¹–10¹⁵ cm⁻² implants. It is non-destructive, takes a minute, and is the tool used to catch a bad implanter *before* the anneal, which is why every implant bay has one. It reads damage, not dopant, so it is calibrated per species and energy, and it cannot read hot implants well, because a hot implant heals its damage as it goes and leaves little to measure.

**Capacitance-voltage (C-V)** on MOS capacitors (a gate electrode over an oxide over the doped silicon) or by **mercury probe** (a drop of mercury pressed against the wafer as a temporary, non-damaging electrode) gives the active doping versus depth in the 10¹⁴–10¹⁸ range (wells), where 4PP is insensitive. It works because a reverse-biased junction, or a MOS capacitor biased into depletion, is a capacitor whose insulating gap is the depletion region of Section 1: raising the reverse voltage widens the depletion region, which lowers the capacitance, and how much the capacitance falls per volt depends on how much fixed dopant charge each extra nanometre of width contributes. So the slope of 1/C² against V gives the active doping at the depletion edge, and stepping the voltage walks that edge deeper, tracing out doping versus depth over the micron or so that a well occupies. Since wells are about a third of the implant steps in a logic flow, this is the workhorse check on their active profile. **Spreading resistance profiling (SRP)** on bevelled samples is a legacy active-profile method; **scanning spreading resistance microscopy (SSRM)** and **atom probe tomography** are the research-grade successors. Ultimately, the metrology that matters is electrical test of the transistors after the whole flow.

> **What can go wrong: measuring the wrong thing.** *4PP punch-through*: on a junction under ~20 nm the probe tips pierce the layer and read the substrate in parallel with it, so R_s comes out low and activation looks better than it is; the check is a second measurement by RsL or micro-4PP. *SIMS surface transient*: the first few nanometres of a SIMS profile are unreliable because the sputter rate has not settled, and on a 10 nm junction that is a third of the profile; low primary-beam energy and a sacrificial cap layer reduce the error. *ThermaProbe calibration drift*: because it reads damage, the same dose of a different species, energy or wafer temperature gives a different signal, and a recipe change without recalibration produces false alarms or, worse, silence; every species/energy combination gets its own calibration curve against SIMS or 4PP.

## 11. Safety

Implant bays are among the most hazardous places in a fab. **Arsine** (AsH₃) has an ACGIH (the American industrial-hygiene body that sets exposure limits) **threshold limit value (TLV)**, the concentration a worker may breathe for an 8-hour day, of 0.005 ppm and an **IDLH** (immediately dangerous to life or health) level, at which a few minutes of exposure can kill, of 3 ppm; it is odourless at dangerous concentrations and causes **haemolysis** (red blood cells burst) with symptoms delayed by hours. **Phosphine** (PH₃, TLV 0.3 ppm) is comparably toxic and **pyrophoric** (it ignites on contact with air). **BF₃** is corrosive and hydrolyses to HF (reacts with moisture to form hydrofluoric acid). The industry's answer, since the 1990s, is the **sub-atmospheric gas source**: Entegris's **SDS** (Safe Delivery Source; the gas is adsorbed on activated carbon inside the cylinder at below atmospheric pressure, so a leak lets air *in* rather than gas out) and the mechanically regulated **VAC** cylinders from Linde. Every source housing has toxic-gas monitors with alarm points near the TLV; an arsine alarm empties the bay and brings in a response team in self-contained breathing apparatus. Source maintenance is done in glove boxes with full respiratory protection because the arc chamber walls are coated in arsenic and phosphorus compounds.

**High voltage**: terminals at 100–300 kV DC, with stored energy in the capacitance of the terminal that is lethal; the whole terminal is inside an enclosure filled with air or **SF₆** (sulphur hexafluoride, an insulating gas that suppresses sparks) with interlocked grounding sticks. **X-rays**: electrons backstreaming up the acceleration column and striking the source at 100+ kV generate **bremsstrahlung**, the X-rays emitted when fast electrons are stopped by a heavy metal, and the machines carry lead shielding and are surveyed periodically; high-energy tools are more heavily shielded still. Add cryogenic pumps, magnets, mechanical scan arms moving at m/s, and it is clear why implanters have the longest safety training list in the fab.

## 12. Market, Cost and Throughput

The implant equipment market is ~$3.5–4 B per year (analyst estimates, as of ~2025), roughly 3–4 % of **wafer fab equipment (WFE)**, the ~$100 B-plus per year market for all the machines inside a fab. **Applied Materials** (which acquired Varian Semiconductor Equipment in 2011 for $4.9 B, and with it the VIISta line and ~60–70 % share of the silicon implant market) is the leader across high-current and medium-current; **Axcelis** (Massachusetts, spun out of Eaton in 2000) holds ~25 % overall, is the leader in high-energy and dominates SiC power (reportedly ~70 % or more). **SMIT** (Sumitomo Heavy Industries Ion Technology, Japan) and **Nissin Ion Equipment** (Japan) supply medium-current and, in SMIT's case, high-energy tools mainly to Japanese and some Chinese fabs. Chinese suppliers include **CETC** (the 48th Research Institute) and **Kingstone**. Kingstone’s public catalogue covers low-energy high-current, medium-current, high-energy, hydrogen and SiC/GaN implantation systems. These categories correspond to different beam energy, current and material requirements; a catalogue listing establishes an offered product family, not qualification for every process or customer. Applied and Axcelis together are effectively a duopoly at the leading edge. Axcelis's revenue more than doubled from 2020 (~$475 M) to 2023 (~$1.13 B), mainly on SiC implant demand (Section 9), and then fell back in ~2024–2025 as SiC, electric-vehicle and mature-node demand softened, so that growth was a cycle, not a trend, and the doubling should not be extrapolated.

Tool prices run ~$4–5 M for medium-current, ~$5–7 M for high-current, and ~$8 M and up for high-energy. Throughput depends on dose: a well implant at 10¹³ cm⁻² is limited by wafer handling and mechanical scan to ~200–400 wph (vendors quote up to 500 wph), while a high-dose implant is beam-current limited.

> **Worked example: implant time at high dose.** A 300 mm wafer has an area of 707 cm². A 1 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻² As⁺ implant needs 7.07 × 10¹⁷ ions, i.e. a charge of 7.07 × 10¹⁷ × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 0.113 C. At 20 mA beam current that is 5.7 s of beam time; add overscan (the beam must scan past the wafer edge, ~30 % overhead) and ~8 s of handling, and the tool does ~200 wph. At 10¹⁶ cm⁻² (a contact or Smart Cut implant) the beam time alone is 57 s, so throughput falls to ~50 wph and the tool's cost per wafer rises fivefold; a single 5 × 10¹⁶ H⁺ Smart Cut implant at a typical 5 mA is over 20 minutes per wafer. This arithmetic is the entire reason the high-current, medium-current and high-energy classes exist: you spend a $7 M tool on 20 mA beams only for the steps that need it.

And the 20 mA itself is the space-charge ceiling of Section 3: a machine that could pull 200 mA through its extraction gap would make the contact implant a 6-second step, and no one has built one, because the Child-Langmuir law says the gap cannot pass it. A leading-edge logic fab of 100 k **wafer starts per month** (the number of new wafers it begins processing each month, the standard measure of a fab's capacity) with ~30 implant steps per wafer runs on the order of 30–50 implanters; the implant bay is small relative to litho, etch or deposition, but a single down high-energy tool can idle every well-implant step in the fab, so most fabs keep at least two of each class.

## Summary

- Doping means placing group-III or group-V atoms on silicon lattice sites. The working range of 10¹⁵–10²⁰ cm⁻³ (20 ppb to 0.2 %) spans only four orders of magnitude of resistivity because mobility falls as doping rises.
- Dopants only work when they are substitutional and ionised; **activation** is the fraction that are. A donor's fifth electron is bound by ~45 meV, under two kT at room temperature, so every donor is ionised.
- Diffusion from the surface lost to implantation because implant sets dose with an ammeter, allows buried (retrograde) profiles and spreads far less sideways.
- An implanter is a source, an extraction gap, an analyser magnet, an energy column, a scanner and a Faraday cup. The final energy sets depth (boron goes ~3 nm/keV at low energy); current × time sets dose.
- Space charge, the beam's self-repulsion, caps beam current at the extraction gap (J ∝ V^(3/2)/(d²·√m)), which is why high-current, medium-current and high-energy tools are separate machines.
- Ions stop by nuclear collisions (which damage) and electronic drag (which does not); the profile is roughly Gaussian with mean R_p and width ΔR_p, and 10¹⁵ cm⁻² of 10 keV boron peaks at ~2 × 10²⁰ cm⁻³.
- Channeling down crystal corridors gives deep, irreproducible tails and is suppressed by 7° tilt, 22° twist, a screen oxide or a germanium pre-amorphisation implant.
- Heavy ions amorphise silicon at ~10¹⁴ cm⁻²; the interstitials left below the amorphous layer drive transient enhanced diffusion, which can move boron ten times further in a 10-minute 800 °C anneal than in a 1,050 °C spike.
- Anneals traded temperature for time: furnace, RTA, spike, millisecond flash and laser, and low-temperature SPER, each with a characteristic √(Dt) (60 nm, a few nm, ~2.4 nm, ~0.6 nm, negligible).
- A 2026 transistor implants its wells, isolation and contacts, leaves its channel undoped because eight atoms cannot be controlled, and grows its source/drain by epitaxy.
- SiC power devices must be implanted hot (~500 °C) and annealed at ~1,700 °C under a carbon cap; Smart Cut SOI (5 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻² hydrogen) and MeV image-sensor wells are the other big non-logic uses.
- ThermaProbe checks dose before the anneal, four-point probe checks activation after it, and SIMS is the reference profile; arsine and phosphine make the implant bay the most hazardous in the fab; Applied and Axcelis are effectively a duopoly.

## Common Misconceptions

- "Implantation puts dopants where you want them and you are done." → The as-implanted dopant is mostly inactive and the crystal is damaged; the anneal determines the final profile, activation and leakage, and TED during the anneal can move dopant ten times further than the implant range.
- "A lower-temperature anneal always means less diffusion." → In the 700–900 °C window, transient enhanced diffusion from slowly dissolving {311} defects can move boron *more* than a 1,050 °C spike anneal that gets through the interstitial transient in under a second.
- "Modern transistors are heavily implanted." → The channel of a FinFET or nanosheet is undoped and V_T is set by gate metal work function; the source/drain is in-situ doped epitaxy. Implant now does wells, isolation, contacts and tuning, but still ~20–40 steps per wafer.
- "The ion beam energy sets the depth, so lower energy means arbitrarily shallow junctions." → Straggle, channeling tails and energy contamination from charge-exchanged neutrals in decel mode all set a floor of a few nm, which is why epi took over the shallowest layers.
- "A higher extraction voltage means a deeper implant." → Extraction voltage sets how much current the source can deliver (space charge); depth is set by the *final* energy after the column, and low-energy tools extract high and decelerate.
- "Dose uniformity across the wafer is set by how good the beam is." → A spot beam is a few centimetres across, a ribbon a few centimetres tall, and either drifts; uniformity comes from the scan servo, which adjusts wafer speed to the measured current, and from Faraday-cup integration that stops the implant at the target charge.
- "Boron and arsenic behave the same in silicon." → Boron is light, does not amorphise silicon, diffuses via interstitials and suffers TED and clustering; arsenic is heavy, amorphises at ~10¹⁴ cm⁻², diffuses slowly and is limited by vacancy clustering. Their process windows are completely different.
- "Implant is a dead technology." → SiC power devices, MeV image-sensor wells, Smart Cut SOI and contact engineering have all grown the implant market; it is ~$3.5–4 B/year, and SiC alone drove a boom-and-bust cycle in Axcelis's revenue over 2020–2025 (Section 12).

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Implant and anneal sit in the front-end loop between lithography (Modules 7 and 8, which define the resist masks the ions are shot through) and etch (Module 9, which removes the resist and later opens the contact holes that receive the highest-dose implants). The inputs are the patterned wafer, dopant gases from the specialty-gas suppliers of Module 4 (arsine, phosphine and BF₃ in sub-atmospheric cylinders from Entegris and Linde), solid sources, and screen oxides from Module 6; the process consumes implanter beam-hours on Applied and Axcelis tools and anneal time in Applied, Mattson, Veeco and TEL/Kokusai tools. The outputs are the electrically defined regions (wells, isolation, extensions, contact layers) that Module 11 assembles into a working FinFET or nanosheet transistor, and, in the special cases of Smart Cut and SiC, the SOI wafers of Module 3 and the power devices that live alongside the GPU in every server rack. Every well implant is checked by ThermaProbe and 4PP before the wafer moves on, feeding the metrology and yield systems of Module 13.
