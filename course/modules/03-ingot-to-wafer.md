# Module 03: From Ingot to Polished Wafer

The crystal grower delivers a solid cylinder; the fab needs thin discs that stay in focus when a pattern is projected onto them. Slicing creates the discs, but sawing alone cannot make the smooth, undamaged surface the fab needs.

A finished Czochralski **ingot**, the cylinder grown from molten silicon, is a 300 mm diameter, roughly 2 m long, 300+ kg cylinder of single-crystal silicon with a domed crown on one end and a conical tail on the other. What a fab wants is about 1,500 to 2,000 discs cut from it, each 775 µm thick, with a front surface whose height varies by less than ~20 nm across any 26 mm × 8 mm **lithography site** (the strip of wafer that a lithography scanner images in one sweep of its light slit; Modules 07–08). Scale the wafer up to a football field 100 m across and the allowed bump height within a site is ~7 µm, a tenth of a human hair.

Flatness and roughness describe different problems. Flatness asks whether a patch of wafer rises or falls across the area being printed. Roughness asks how bumpy the surface is at much smaller scales. A surface can be smoothly curved, like a polished lens, yet fail the wafer's flatness requirement. The surface must also have roughness below 0.1 nm RMS (**root-mean-square (RMS) roughness** is the typical height of the bumps on a surface; 0.1 nm is less than one atomic layer of silicon, which is 0.136 nm high), fewer than a handful of particles larger than 26 nm across the whole surface, metal contamination below ~10^10 atoms/cm² (a (100) silicon surface holds ~6.8 × 10^14 atoms/cm², so that is one foreign atom per ~70,000 surface silicon atoms), and edges that will not chip during a thousand process steps. Each of these is a specification you will be able to explain by the end of this module.

The difficulty is that silicon is hard (Mohs ~7, close to quartz; the **Mohs scale** is the 1–10 scratch-hardness ladder in which talc is 1, window glass ~5.5, quartz 7 and diamond 10), brittle, and every mechanical operation that shapes it leaves a layer of microcracks and dislocations beneath the surface. A **dislocation** is a line in the crystal where a half-plane of atoms ends; it moves under stress, and inside a finished transistor it leaks current. A brittle solid cannot dent, it can only crack, so every abrasive grain that presses into silicon leaves a crack roughly as deep as the grain is large. The entire wafering sequence is therefore a controlled descent: each step removes the damage layer left by the previous, coarser step, while adding a thinner damage layer of its own, until chemistry and **chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP)**, polishing with a slurry that softens the surface chemically while a pad wipes the softened layer away, take over and the last few nanometers are removed without any mechanical damage at all. Along the way, ~25% of the ingot's silicon becomes sawdust and sludge.

By the end of this module you will be able to explain the 14-step sequence from ingot to boxed wafer and why each step removes the damage the previous one left; why a chemically polished surface has no damage layer at all; the flatness vocabulary (thickness variation, warp, site flatness, nanotopography) and what a lithography scanner can and cannot correct; how the RCA clean, the standard wet-chemical cleaning sequence named for the laboratory that invented it, lifts particles and metals without roughening the surface; what epitaxial, annealed and silicon-on-insulator wafers add to a polished one; and who makes the wafers, the saws and the polishers, and what a wafer costs.

## Before you start

- **Single-crystal silicon and the Czochralski (CZ) pull (Module 02).** A seed crystal is dipped into molten silicon and drawn out slowly, and one continuous crystal ~300 mm across, ~2 m long and ~300 kg grows below it. The **Dash neck** is the thin section pulled first to shake dislocations out; the **crown** (shoulder) is where the diameter was widened, and the **tail** is the tapered end.
- **Crystal directions and Miller indices (Module 02).** Think of silicon as stacked cubes 0.543 nm on a side. (100) is a cube face, (111) is a plane that cuts off a corner, and <110> is the direction of a face diagonal. A "(100) wafer" has a cube face as its surface.
- **Dislocations and slip (Module 02).** Dislocations are line defects in the lattice that move under stress ("slip"); they leak current and short transistors, and heat plus stress creates them. The whole point of Module 02 was to grow a crystal with none, and this module must not put any back.
- **Doping and resistivity (Module 02).** Boron makes **p-type** silicon (conduction by missing electrons, "holes"); phosphorus or arsenic make **n-type** (conduction by extra electrons). **Resistivity**, in Ω·cm, is the resistance of a 1 cm cube of the material; more dopant means lower resistivity. Dopants distribute unevenly along the ingot (segregation).
- **Point defects, vacancies and COPs (Module 02).** A **vacancy** is a missing atom. Too many of them cluster into ~100 nm voids that reach the surface as pits (crystal-originated particles, COPs). Oxygen from the quartz crucible dissolves in the crystal and can be made to precipitate deliberately to trap stray metals (**gettering**).
- **Hardness and brittleness.** Silicon is hard (Mohs ~7) and brittle, so it cannot dent, only crack; every abrasive grain leaves a crack about as deep as the grain is large.
- **School chemistry.** Acids, bases and pH; oxidation as taking electrons (peroxide or nitric acid turns silicon into silicon dioxide, SiO2); hydrofluoric acid (HF) is the one common acid that dissolves SiO2; strong bases such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) attack silicon directly; surface tension is the "skin" on a liquid surface.
- **Lithography in one sentence (Modules 07–08).** A scanner projects the chip pattern onto a 26 mm × 33 mm field by sweeping a 26 mm × 8 mm slit of light across it, and like a camera it has a limited depth of focus, ~100 nm for extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) tools.
- **SEMI.** The semiconductor industry's standards body. **SEMI M1** is the specification every polished silicon wafer is sold against, and **prime** is the top grade.
- **Units and scales.** 1 mm = 1,000 µm = 1,000,000 nm; a human hair is ~70 µm across; an atomic step on a Si(100) surface is 0.136 nm high; a (100) surface holds ~6.8 × 10^14 atoms/cm².

## The Process Flow at a Glance

Four words are needed to read the map below. **Kerf** is the width of crystal that a saw turns into dust, the slot the wire cuts. The **subsurface damage layer** is the zone of cracks and dislocations beneath any abraded surface. **Total thickness variation (TTV)** is the thickest point of a wafer minus the thinnest. A **prime wafer** is the top grade, one that meets the customer's full device specification; wafers that miss it on a non-critical parameter are sold as test grade. SEMI is the industry standards body and SEMI M1 is its master specification for polished wafers; every other acronym in the table is expanded in the step list below or defined in its own section.

The fourteen steps, in order, each with what physically happens and why it comes next:

1. **Crop and section.** A diamond band saw removes the crown and tail and cuts the 2 m body into ~300–400 mm blocks, because that is the longest block a wire saw can hold; thin test slugs from each cut face are measured to qualify the block.
2. **Cylindrical grind.** The block is turned against a diamond wheel like a lathe to bring the diameter from ~302–306 mm to exactly 300.00 mm; it is done first because every later step assumes a true cylinder.
3. **X-ray orient and notch grind.** X-ray diffraction (XRD) finds the <110> crystal direction and a small wheel grinds a 1 mm notch along the whole block, so that every slice carries the notch in the same place.
4. **Multi-wire saw.** A web of hundreds of parallel diamond-coated wire strands cuts the block into ~900 µm slices in one pass; it comes after notching so that no slice has to be marked individually.
5. **Double-disk grind (or lap).** Two opposed diamond wheels grind both faces at once, removing the saw marks and the deep saw damage and setting TTV below 1 µm.
6. **Edge profile.** A formed diamond wheel rounds the razor-sharp as-cut edge so it cannot chip; this happens before etching so the etch also removes the profiling damage.
7. **Alkaline or acid etch.** A hot chemical bath dissolves 20–30 µm of silicon, everything the grinders left, because only chemistry removes damage without creating new damage.
8. **Laser mark.** A laser drills an identity code into the wafer; it is done before polishing so polishing smooths the rim of each pit.
9. **Edge polish.** The rounded bevel is mirror-polished with a soft pad and colloidal silica to remove the microcracks the profile wheel left, so the edge cannot shed particles in the fab; its position in the flow varies by maker (after the etch, and either before or after the double-side polish).
10. **Double-side polish (DSP).** Both faces are polished at once between two pads with colloidal silica slurry, erasing the matte etched surface and setting the final flatness.
11. **Final front-side polish.** A gentle single-side polish takes the front face to below 0.1 nm RMS roughness with no haze.
12. **RCA clean and dry.** A sequence of wet chemistries removes particles, metals and organics, and the wafer is dried without leaving a droplet behind.
13. **Metrology and inspection.** Every wafer is measured for flatness, nanotopography, particles and contamination against the spec before it is graded.
14. **Pack.** Twenty-five wafers are sealed in a shipping box under nitrogen.

The table below is the numeric companion. Numbers are typical for 300 mm prime wafers; every wafer maker has its own recipe.

| Step | Purpose | Typical removal (300 mm) | Damage left behind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crop and section | Remove crown/tail, cut test slugs, cut ingot into ~300–400 mm blocks | (ends of ingot) | Saw damage on block ends only |
| Cylindrical grind | Bring diameter to 300.0 mm, remove growth ripples | ~1–3 mm on radius | ~10–20 µm subsurface on periphery |
| XRD orient and notch grind | Locate <110>, cut the 1 mm notch | (notch only) | Local |
| Multi-wire saw | Slice block into ~900 µm slices | ~150–170 µm kerf per slice (diamond wire; 200+ µm slurry) | 5–30 µm subsurface cracks, saw marks |

| Alkaline/acid etch | Dissolve all remaining mechanical damage | ~20–30 µm total | None (chemically etched surface) |

Total: a raw slice of ~890–950 µm becomes a 775 µm wafer, and roughly 1.05 mm of ingot length is consumed per wafer.

> **Intuition: the controlled descent.** Read the right-hand column of the table from top to bottom. The saw leaves cracks 5–30 µm deep. The grinder removes 60–80 µm, far more than the deepest saw crack, and leaves cracks of only 2–5 µm. The etch removes 20–30 µm, again more than the deepest grinding crack, and being chemical it leaves no cracks at all, only a rough faceted surface. The polish removes 15–25 µm of that roughness and leaves a surface flat to a fraction of an atomic step. At every stage the rule is the same: remove more than the previous step damaged, using a gentler tool than the previous step used. Only the last two stages, which are chemical, can remove material without leaving a crack behind.

## Why 775 µm: Wafer Thickness and Mechanics

SEMI M1 specifies 775 ± 25 µm for 300 mm, 725 ± 20 µm for 200 mm, 675 µm for 150 mm, and 625 µm for 125 mm. The proposed 450 mm standard was 925 µm. Why does the thickness grow with diameter, and why not exactly proportionally?

A wafer must survive being lifted on three pins or a ring by a robot without cracking, and it must not sag so much on a lithography **chuck** (the flat plate that holds a wafer by vacuum or electrostatic force while a tool works on it) or in a **furnace boat** (the quartz rack that holds 100 or more wafers in a furnace) that it distorts or touches its neighbor.

> **Intuition: the ruler.** Try to bend a ruler flat-wise and it flexes easily; try it edge-wise and it will not budge. Stiffness grows as the cube of the thickness in the bending direction, while the weight of the ruler only grows in proportion to its thickness. So doubling a plate's thickness makes it eight times stiffer but only twice as heavy, and its sag under its own weight falls by a factor of four. Diameter works the other way: a plate twice as wide has four times the weight and a much longer lever arm, and its sag grows sixteen-fold.

The gravitational sag of a plate supported at its edge scales as D^4 / t^2 (it scales with load, proportional to t, and inversely with **flexural rigidity**, the plate's resistance to bending, which is proportional to t^3). Keeping sag constant across diameters would require t proportional to D^2, i.e. a 300 mm wafer would need ~1.6 mm. The industry chose a compromise: t roughly proportional to D, accepting more sag at 300 mm, because thicker wafers cost more silicon, take longer to saw, and are harder to thin at the back end (every wafer is ground down to 50–100 µm before packaging anyway; see Module 16).

Two material constants appear in the calculation below. **Young's modulus E** is the stiffness of the material itself, the stress needed per unit of stretch; **Poisson's ratio ν** is how much the material thins sideways when it is stretched lengthwise. A plate is **simply supported** when it rests on its rim, free to tilt there but not to move.

> **Worked example: gravitational sag of a 300 mm wafer.** Model the wafer as a thin circular plate of radius a = 150 mm, thickness t = 775 µm, simply supported at its rim under its own weight. Uniform load q = ρ g t, where ρ is silicon density and g is gravitational acceleration: q = 2,330 kg/m³ × 9.81 m/s² × 775 × 10^-6 m ≈ 17.7 Pa. The resistance to bending is the **flexural rigidity** D = E t³ / [12 (1 − ν²)]. Here E is Young's modulus, the material's stiffness under stretching, and ν is Poisson's ratio, which describes its sideways contraction when stretched; with E ≈ 150 GPa (a representative in-plane average for (100) silicon) and ν ≈ 0.28, D ≈ 150 × 10^9 × (775 × 10^-6)³ / 11.06 ≈ 6.3 N·m. The center deflection of a simply supported plate is w = [(5 + ν)/(1 + ν)] × q a^4 / (64 D) = 4.13 × 17.7 × (0.15)^4 / (64 × 6.3) ≈ 9 × 10^-5 m, i.e. ~90 µm. Repeating for a 200 mm, 725 µm wafer gives ~20 µm. So a 300 mm wafer sags roughly 4–5 times more than a 200 mm wafer despite being thicker, which is why 300 mm tools support wafers on a full-face vacuum chuck or a wide-ring edge grip, and why furnace boats hold 300 mm wafers on multiple support points close to the edge. It is also why flatness must be specified in a "free" or "chucked" state: 90 µm of sag dwarfs a 20 nm **SFQR** spec (site flatness, the within-site height range a scanner must focus through; defined under Flatness Metrology) by more than three orders of magnitude, and metrology tools measure the wafer held vertically or on a flat chuck to remove gravity.

The ± 25 µm SEMI tolerance covers robot **end-effectors** (the blade or paddle on which a robot carries a wafer) and the **slot pitch** of the **FOUP** (front-opening unified pod, the fab's sealed 25-wafer carrier, Module 05; the slot pitch is the 10 mm spacing between its wafer slots); fabs actually specify ± 10–15 µm within a lot so the fab's own CMP steps and lithography focus do not drift wafer to wafer.

> **Why it matters downstream:** A free 300 mm wafer sags ~90 µm, yet the fab's flatness spec within a lithography site (SFQR) is ~20 nm, 4,500 times smaller. The two numbers are only compatible because every flatness figure in this module is defined for a wafer whose back has been pulled flat against a chuck, and because a scanner refocuses on every site. That is why 300 mm lithography tools use full-face vacuum chucks rather than edge grips, why the chuck itself must be flat to nanometres, and why the wafer's own contribution to focus error is measured only after gravity and global shape have been removed.

## Cropping, Sectioning, and Ingot QC

### Removing crown and tail

The ingot arrives from the puller (Module 02) with a shoulder/crown where the diameter was expanded from the Dash neck, and a conical tail where the diameter was reduced to detach from the melt without dislocation slip-back. Two refreshers: the **Dash neck** is the ~3 mm-thin section pulled first, whose purpose is to let any dislocations in the seed grow out to the surface before the crystal is widened; **slip-back** is what happens if the crystal is pulled off the melt abruptly, because the thermal shock nucleates dislocations at the detachment point that run back up into good crystal, so the diameter is tapered down first to make the final separation gentle. Both crown and tail are cropped off. Historically this was done with an **inner-diameter (ID) saw**: a thin stainless-steel annulus, tensioned like a drumhead, with diamond grit electroplated on its inner edge. ID saws are still used for small diameters and for single cuts, but for 300 mm ingots the standard crop tool is a **diamond band saw** (a continuous steel band carrying diamond grit, like a woodworker's band saw) or a large diamond wire saw, because a 300 mm ID blade would need an impractically large, thick blade and would waste a wide kerf (~0.5 mm or more). The band saw's mechanism is the same two-body abrasion as the diamond wire saw described later, with the band in place of the wire: fixed diamond grains, run past the silicon under coolant flood, scratch out chips of crystal, and the crack layer they leave (comparable to the wire saw's, ~5–30 µm) is confined to the two cut faces, which become scrap ends or test slugs anyway. The numbers that matter are the kerf, ~1 mm (1.2 mm on Arnold Gruppe's Model 72/375, a German band saw built for 300 mm CZ ingots that takes segments up to 500 mm long and 310 mm across), and the cut time, which for a 300 mm cross-section is of the order of tens of minutes per cut (makers do not publish it) rather than the hours a wire-saw pass takes, because a band saw makes one cut at a time and can run a coarser, faster-cutting grit; a fresh band cuts fastest, and the band is changed when its cut time drifts up. A single diamond wire, run in a cropping saw at the same ~10–25 m/s as the multi-wire saw's wire, is the alternative for a slightly thinner kerf. Arnold Gruppe (Germany) is one of the few makers that publishes specifications for this duty; the rest of the cropping-saw market is a handful of Japanese and European machine-tool builders, and wafer makers do not publish their tool lists.

The cropped crown and tail are etched clean and returned to the **puller** (the Czochralski growth furnace) as remelt **charge** (the polysilicon loaded into the crucible for the next pull).

### Sectioning into blocks

A 2 m body is too long for a wire saw's work envelope, so it is cut into **blocks** (also called sections or ingot segments) of ~300–400 mm length. From each cut face a thin **test slug** (a few mm thick) is taken. These slugs are the primary **quality-control (QC)** samples for the whole block, because the crystal's properties vary along its length in a predictable way due to dopant segregation and because oxygen incorporation from the quartz crucible falls as the melt is depleted. The **segregation coefficient** of a dopant is the ratio of its concentration in the freezing solid to its concentration in the liquid; anything below 1 is partly rejected into the melt, so the melt, and therefore the crystal grown later, gets steadily richer. The segregation coefficient of boron in silicon is ~0.8 and phosphorus ~0.35, so dopant concentration rises toward the tail. That is why the tail slug and the crown slug differ and why each wafer's resistivity is interpolated from its position.

> **Worked example: what each sectioning cut costs.** Every cut through the body consumes one band-saw kerf (~1 mm) plus the two test slugs taken from the new faces (~3 mm each), about 7 mm of crystal. At the wire saw's pitch of ~1.06 mm per wafer (derived under Multi-Wire Slicing), that is ~6–7 wafers lost per cut, or at ~$100–150 per polished wafer roughly $700–1,000 of finished product each time the band saw passes through the ingot. Cutting a 2 m body into six ~330 mm blocks costs five such cuts, ~35 mm and ~33 wafers, under 2% of the body. Cutting it into twelve ~165 mm blocks would double that loss, which is why blocks are made as long as the wire saw's work envelope allows and why a longer envelope is a selling point for a saw.

### What is measured on the slugs

- **Resistivity.** Why the fab cares: the substrate doping sets the starting point for every well and the threshold of every transistor built on it, and a wafer outside the window shifts the whole chip's electrical behaviour. How: a **four-point probe** (SEMI MF84) presses four spring-loaded needles in a line onto the slug, passes a current through the outer two and reads the voltage across the inner two; because no current flows in the voltage-sensing needles, the resistance of the needle-to-silicon contacts drops out of the measurement. For a slug thick compared with the needle spacing, the current spreads out into a half-space and the resistivity is ρ = 2π s × V/I, where s is the needle spacing, I the current through the outer pair and V the voltage across the inner pair; with s = 1 mm (0.1 cm), 1 mA and 16 mV, ρ = 2π × 0.1 cm × 16 Ω ≈ 10 Ω·cm (a thin wafer needs a geometric correction factor, tabulated in the standard). Non-contact **eddy-current gauges** do the same job by inducing a circulating current with a coil and measuring the energy it absorbs. Numbers: a typical p-type logic substrate is 1–20 Ω·cm (boron ~10^15 cm^-3); a p+ substrate is 0.005–0.02 Ω·cm, where **p+** means heavily boron-doped, and such substrates are used under an **epitaxial layer**, a thin fresh crystal layer grown on top (covered at the end of this module). Out of window: fabs specify a resistivity window per product, and a block outside the window is diverted to another product or to test-wafer grade.
- **Interstitial oxygen** (oxygen atoms sitting in the gaps between silicon lattice sites, not on them; an **interstitial** is any atom squeezed between lattice sites, the opposite defect to a vacancy). Why: oxygen dissolved from the quartz crucible is wanted, in a window, because during the fab's thermal cycles it precipitates into tiny SiO2 clusters deep in the wafer that trap stray metals, a mechanism called **intrinsic gettering** (gettering is any deliberate trapping of contaminants away from the transistors); too much oxygen, though, precipitates so heavily that the wafer warps and slips. How: **Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy** shines infrared light through the slug; the Si–O bond vibrates at a frequency that absorbs light at a **wavenumber** of 1107 cm^-1 (wavenumber is 1/wavelength, so this is ~9 µm light), and the depth of that absorption is proportional to the oxygen concentration (SEMI MF1188 / ASTM F1188). Numbers: typical CZ oxygen is 10–18 **ppma** (parts per million atoms, the older units of ASTM, the American standards body whose wafer test methods SEMI later absorbed as the "MF" standards) or ~5–9 × 10^17 atoms/cm^3; fabs specify a narrow window (e.g. 12 ± 1.5 ppma) and a target precipitate density. Out of window: the block goes to a product with a different window or to test grade.
- **Substitutional carbon.** Why: carbon sitting on silicon lattice sites nucleates unwanted oxygen precipitation, so the precipitate density the fab designed for comes out wrong. How: FTIR again, at 605 cm^-1. Number: must be below ~0.1–0.5 ppma. Out of window: downgrade.
- **Crystal defects and lifetime.** Why: a single dislocation, or a ring of **stacking faults** (planar defects in which one atomic layer is laid down out of sequence) in the wrong place, means the crystal grower's process drifted and the block may not be uniform. How: an etched slug is checked for dislocation etch pits using a **Secco or Wright etch**, chromium-based acid mixtures that etch faster where the lattice is strained, leaving a countable pit at each dislocation, and for the position of the **OSF ring** (oxidation-induced stacking-fault ring), the ring of stacking faults that appears after a test oxidation at the boundary where vacancy-rich crystal meets interstitial-rich crystal (here the interstitials are silicon atoms between lattice sites, the same kind of defect as the interstitial oxygen above; Module 02); its radius tells the grower how well centred the pull was. **Lifetime** is how long an excited electron survives in the crystal before a metal atom traps it, so a short lifetime means metal contamination; **microwave photoconductive decay (µ-PCD)** measures it by flashing the slug with light and watching, with a microwave beam, how fast the extra conductivity decays, which flags metal contamination at parts-per-trillion levels. Out of window: dislocated or metal-contaminated blocks are scrapped or sent to non-electronic uses, and the contamination is traced back to the puller.

A block that passes gets a **lot** number (a lot is the batch of wafers, usually 25, that travels and is processed together) that follows every wafer cut from it, with end-slug resistivity and oxygen interpolated to each wafer's position.

> **Worked example: interpolating resistivity along a block.** Module 02's Scheil picture says the concentration in the freezing solid rises as (1 − f)^(k − 1), where f is the fraction of the melt already solidified and k is the segregation coefficient. For boron, k ≈ 0.8, so the exponent is −0.2. A ~330 mm block from a ~2 m body spans about 16% of the ingot; take one running from f ≈ 0.30 to f ≈ 0.46. The concentration factor (1 − f)^(−0.2) is ~1.07 at the head and ~1.13 at the tail, a rise of ~5%, so if the head slug reads 10.0 Ω·cm the tail slug reads ~9.5 Ω·cm and a wafer from the middle of the block is assigned ~9.75 Ω·cm by linear interpolation (resistivity is close to inversely proportional to boron concentration at these levels). The curve steepens toward the tail: the last block, from f ≈ 0.8 to 0.9, drops ~15% across its length, which is why tail blocks are the ones most likely to fall outside a tight window. For phosphorus, k ≈ 0.35 makes the exponent −0.65, and resistivity falls almost threefold between the crown and f = 0.8; that is why n-type ingots yield far fewer wafers inside a narrow specification.

> **What can go wrong:** A block whose resistivity or oxygen falls outside the customer's window is not scrapped but diverted to a product with a looser window or to test grade, at a lower price. An OSF ring at the wrong radius shows that the puller's v/G (pull speed over temperature gradient) drifted, and such a block is downgraded for products sensitive to **gate-oxide integrity** (how reliably the few-nanometre insulating layer under each transistor's gate survives voltage stress; Module 11). High carbon means precipitation will not follow the fab's model, so the block goes to a less demanding customer. A short µ-PCD lifetime means a metal (typically iron or copper) got into the melt from the **hot zone** (the graphite heaters and insulation that surround the crucible) or from the polysilicon, and the block is held while the puller is investigated.

## Cylindrical Grinding to Diameter

The as-grown ingot has a diameter of ~302–306 mm with millimeter-scale ripples from melt level and pull-rate fluctuations. The ripples are the reason 1–3 mm has to come off the radius: the grind must reach below the deepest valley in the surface and below the worst out-of-roundness, everywhere along the block, or the finished cylinder would have a flat spot where the original surface was already low. Grinding is also the only way to make the block round to a hundredth of a millimetre; the crystal puller controls diameter to a millimetre at best.

The block is mounted between centers (or on a chuck) and turned against a diamond grinding wheel, exactly like a lathe, until the diameter is 300.00 mm; an in-process laser gauge, which measures the shadow of the rotating block in a beam of light, reads the diameter continuously so the operator does not stop to measure. SEMI M1, the master specification for polished silicon wafers, sets the 300 mm diameter tolerance at ± 0.2 mm, and most wafer makers work to tighter internal limits (± 0.1 mm) because every downstream tool's edge grip, pre-aligner, and edge-exclusion metrology assumes a precise diameter.

The grind uses a resin- or metal-bonded diamond wheel in two passes (coarse ~#200–#400 grit, then fine ~#800–#1500) under coolant flood, removing 1–3 mm on the radius. A **grit number** is the mesh count of the sieve that sorted the grains, so higher is finer: #400 grains are ~35–40 µm across, #1500 ~10 µm, and #2000 ~5–7 µm (approximate; the standards differ slightly). The **bond** is the matrix that holds the diamonds: a **resin bond** is softer and wears away to expose fresh diamond, giving a gentler cut; a **metal bond** lasts longer but runs hotter. The grind leaves a damage layer ~10–20 µm deep on the cylinder surface, which becomes the wafer edge, so the edge is later profiled and polished. The ground-off silicon, unlike the crown and tail, is not remelted: it is a slurry of micrometre-sized silicon grains mixed with coolant and worn diamond and bond, contaminated with the wheel's metals, and it goes to the same low-value outlets as the wire-saw kerf.

Ingot grinders are heavy cylindrical grinders adapted to a brittle workpiece: a stiff bed so that the wheel does not chatter, coolant flood, an in-process laser gauge, and often an X-ray orienter on the same machine so that the block need not be re-mounted for the notch. STUDER (Switzerland, part of the UNITED GRINDING group) builds cylindrical grinders with an integrated X-ray orienter for exactly this duty; Arnold Gruppe (Germany) offers round grinding alongside its cropping and notch-grinding machines; and Japanese machine-tool builders, several of which also make the grinders used later in the flow, supply the rest. It is a low-volume, custom-built tool class, a handful of machines per wafer plant against the dozens of wire saws and polishers computed in the worked examples below, so no maker publishes a market share and wafer makers do not publish their tool lists.

> **Worked example: how much comes off, and how little misalignment it takes to fail the tolerance.** Take a 330 mm block ground from an as-grown 304 mm to 300 mm, i.e. 2 mm off the radius. The volume removed is π × (152² − 150²) mm² × 330 mm = π × 604 × 330 ≈ 626,000 mm³, or 0.63 litres of silicon, which at 2.33 g/cm³ is ~1.5 kg per block and ~9 kg per six-block ingot, on top of the 329 kg body computed at 300 mm in the slicing example below; that is ~3% of the crystal turned into grinding sludge before a single wafer is cut. Now the tolerance: the maker's internal limit of ± 0.1 mm on diameter is ± 0.05 mm on radius. If the block's axis is tilted by only 0.01° relative to the grinder's traverse, the radius changes by 330 mm × tan(0.01°) = 330 × 1.75 × 10^-4 ≈ 0.058 mm from one end of the block to the other, a 0.115 mm change in diameter, which already uses the whole tolerance band. A hundredth of a degree is the mounting accuracy the operator must hold, and it is why the block is indicated (measured for run-out) on the machine before the first pass rather than simply clamped.

> **What can go wrong:** If the block is mounted slightly off-axis the ground cylinder tapers, so slices from one end are a fraction of a millimetre larger than from the other and the fab's pre-aligner (the small stage that centres each wafer before a robot hands it on) throws alignment faults. Wheel vibration leaves **chatter marks**, periodic ripples that the edge profiler must remove. Out-of-roundness beyond ± 0.1 mm confuses edge-grip robots. And the 10–20 µm of subsurface cracks left in the periphery become edge chips in the fab unless the edge is later profiled and polished, which is why those two steps exist.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The fab places **dies** (the individual chips, still joined together on the wafer) to within 1.5 mm of the wafer edge (the edge exclusion, defined later), so a diameter error of 0.1 mm is a noticeable fraction of the usable margin, and every edge-grip end-effector and pre-aligner in the fab is built around a wafer that is 300.00 mm across. A block ground 0.2 mm undersize would still meet SEMI M1 but would misplace the outer dies of every wafer cut from it.

## Orientation and the Notch

> **Intuition: Miller indices.** Picture the crystal as stacked cubes 0.543 nm on a side. The label (100) is a cube face; (110) is the plane through two opposite edges, the diagonal slice; (111) is the plane that cuts off a corner. Angle brackets give directions rather than planes: <110> is the direction of a face diagonal. Curly brackets, {110}, mean the whole family of planes equivalent by symmetry. A (100) wafer's face is a cube face, and its notch points along a face diagonal, the <110> direction. The picture breaks only in one respect: silicon's real repeating unit contains eight atoms in a diamond-shaped arrangement, not one atom per cube corner, which matters for X-ray diffraction below.

### Why the wafer needs a fiducial

Silicon's mechanical, electrical, and etching properties are anisotropic (different in different directions). Transistor channels, and the rectangular grid of chips, are conventionally laid out along <110> on a (100) wafer. What fixes that convention is mechanics, not electronics: a (100) wafer cracks cleanly along <110> directions, where its **cleavage planes** (the planes along which silicon prefers to crack, {111}) meet the surface, so laying the **dicing streets** (the gaps between chips where the wafer is sawn apart) along <110> means the saw follows the lines the crystal already wants to break on, and KOH etch facets line up with the same directions; decades of process recipes and reliability data were then built on that layout. Electronics enters as a trade-off on top of it. The channel direction affects **mobility**, the speed at which charge carriers drift per volt of electric field, and on (100) silicon the two carriers disagree: holes drift faster along <110>, which suited the early CMOS era when the p-type transistor was the weak one, while electrons drift somewhat faster along <100>. Some processes therefore deliberately rotate the layout 45° so that channels run along <100>, gaining electron mobility for the n-type transistors at the cost of some hole mobility and of dicing off the cleavage lines. Either way, every wafer must carry a mark that tells every tool where the crystal axes are. On 150 mm and smaller wafers this is the **primary flat**: a chord ground on the ingot along a {110} plane (57.5 mm long in the flatted 200 mm option of SEMI M1), plus a smaller secondary flat that encoded doping type and orientation. SEMI M1 offers 200 mm wafers with either a flat or a notch, and most 200 mm lines adopted the notch. On 300 mm wafers only the **notch** exists: a small V-shaped groove, 1.00 mm deep (+0.25/−0 mm), with a 90° (+5°/−1°) included angle and rounded bottom, per SEMI M1, centered on the <110> direction.

### Why a notch and not a flat on 300 mm

Three reasons. Area: a 57.5 mm flat on a 200 mm wafer removes a circular segment of ~160 mm² (about 0.5% of the wafer) plus a wider effective loss where the edge-exclusion band follows the flat; scaled to 300 mm a flat would be 80+ mm long and cost several dies, while a notch removes < 1 mm². Mechanics: a flat is a stress concentrator that breaks the axisymmetric stiffness of a wafer already sagging tens of micrometers under its own weight. Process uniformity: spin coating (applying a liquid film by spinning the wafer), spin rinsing, and **edge-bead removal** (rinsing off the ridge of **photoresist**, the light-sensitive coating that lithography patterns (Module 07), that spin coating leaves at the rim) assume a circular edge, and a flat produces a coating anomaly downstream of it. The notch was introduced as an option in the 200 mm generation (early 1990s) and made the only fiducial in the 300 mm standard (~1999–2001) to eliminate all three.

### How orientation is found

The <110> direction is located by **X-ray diffraction (XRD)**. The block is rotated on a precision rotating stage while a **collimated** (made parallel) X-ray beam hits a cut face. The beam is typically **Cu Kα**, the sharp X-ray line a copper target emits when electrons fired at it knock out copper's innermost electrons and outer electrons drop into the holes; its wavelength is 1.54 Å (0.154 nm; the **ångström**, Å, is 0.1 nm, the traditional unit for atomic spacings), close to the spacing of the atomic planes, which is what makes it useful. The planes of atoms act like a stack of half-silvered mirrors: each reflects a little of the beam, and the reflections add up only when the extra path travelled between neighbouring planes is a whole number of wavelengths. That is **Bragg's law**, 2d sin θ = λ, where d is the spacing between the planes, θ is the glancing angle between beam and planes, and λ is the wavelength. The detector sees the Bragg reflection from {220} planes only at the **azimuth** (rotation angle about the block axis) where those planes are set at the Bragg angle. The peak locates <110> to better than ± 0.1°.

> **Worked example: the Bragg angle for silicon {220}.** For a cubic lattice the spacing of the (hkl) planes is d = a / √(h² + k² + l²). With a = 0.543 nm, d(220) = 0.543 / √8 = 0.543 / 2.83 = 0.192 nm. Then sin θ = λ / 2d = 0.154 / (2 × 0.192) = 0.401, so θ = 23.6° and the detector must sit at 2θ = 47.3° from the incoming beam. Rotate the block until the detector at 47.3° lights up; that azimuth is <110>. Why {220} rather than {110}? In the diamond lattice the reflections from the two interleaved sets of atoms in a {110} spacing cancel each other exactly, so the first usable reflection along that direction is the second order, {220}, with half the spacing.

SEMI M1 requires the notch to be within ± 1° of <110>. The same measurement checks the surface orientation: the (100) face must be within ± 0.5° (or tighter for some epi products). An **off-cut** is a deliberate tilt of a few degrees between the wafer surface and the exact crystal plane; it is not used for standard 300 mm **CMOS** wafers (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor, the transistor technology of essentially all logic and memory chips, Module 11), though it is used for some epi products and for silicon carbide (SiC) wafers (Module 04). A block whose growth axis has wandered off (100) by more than the tolerance is sawn at a corrected angle, which is why the wire saw's block holder has tilt adjustment.

After XRD, the notch is ground along the full length of the block with a small formed diamond wheel, so every slice from the block carries an identically placed notch. The wheel is a resin-bond diamond wheel (the softer bond, so that the groove's surface is left with shallow damage rather than deep cracks), shaped to the 90° V with its rounded bottom, and it is traversed along the block at a slow feed under coolant while the block is held at the azimuth the XRD peak found; the notch's 1.00 mm depth is held to +0.25/−0 mm, its centre to ± 1° of <110>, and both are re-checked on a sample slice from every block. The station handles one block at a time and its cycle, an XRD scan plus a full-length traverse, is minutes rather than hours, so one orient-and-notch station keeps up with several wire saws. Makers: STUDER (Switzerland) integrates the X-ray orienter into its ingot cylindrical grinder so that diameter grinding and orientation happen in one set-up; Arnold Gruppe (Germany) builds dedicated notch-grinding machines; and a few Japanese specialists supply combined orient-and-notch stations. As with the ingot grinders, this is a handful-per-plant tool class and no maker publishes a market share.

> **What can go wrong:** A notch ground at the wrong azimuth is the expensive failure. SEMI M1 allows a single notch, and grinding a misplaced one out would take the block below 300 mm, so a block notched more than ± 1° off <110> cannot be corrected: every wafer from it would have its dicing streets and transistor channels rotated off the crystal axes, and the fab's pre-aligner, which finds the notch optically and trusts it, would never know. That is why the XRD azimuth is verified on a sample slice before the block is released and why a block that fails is scrapped or downgraded rather than shipped. A block whose surface orientation is off (100) by more than the tolerance is not scrapped; it is sawn at a compensating tilt, as described above.

## Multi-Wire Slicing

### The machine

A **multi-wire saw** does the single most consequential operation in wafering: it turns a block into slices, and it wastes the most silicon while doing so. The principle is simple. A single steel wire, 100–140 µm in diameter and tens to hundreds of kilometers long, is wound in a helix around three or four grooved rollers (wire guides) so that between two rollers it forms a **wire web** of several hundred to over a thousand parallel, equally spaced strands. The block, glued with epoxy or wax onto a sacrificial glass or graphite beam, is fed downward through the web. The wire runs continuously from a supply spool through the web to a take-up spool at 10–25 m/s, and all strands cut simultaneously. The block is fed through its own 300 mm diameter at ~0.5–1 mm/min, so one pass takes ~5–10 hours (worked below) and yields 300–400 wafers.

The pitch of the grooves on the wire guides sets the wafer pitch: pitch = as-cut wafer thickness + kerf, where **kerf** is the width of the slot the wire cuts, the crystal that becomes dust. For 300 mm prime wafers with a ~900 µm as-cut slice and ~150–170 µm kerf, the pitch is ~1.05–1.10 mm.

> **Intuition: the bowstring.** The wire is a bowstring. As the block is pushed against it, each strand bends sideways by a few millimetres, and it is the wire's tension acting through that bow that presses the diamond into the silicon: the sideways force on the block is roughly 2T sin α, where T is the tension and α the bow angle. With a tension of a few tens of newtons and a bow of a few millimetres across a span of a few hundred millimetres (a bow angle of ~0.01–0.02 radians), that is of the order of a newton per strand: ~30 N × 2 × 0.02 ≈ 1 N. Now change the tension, or the feed rate, or let the block warm up and expand during a multi-hour cut: the bow changes, the plane in which the wire cuts moves, and the slice thickness changes along the cut. That is the origin of as-cut thickness variation and warp, and it is why the tension is held to a fraction of a newton.

Wire tension is of the order of tens of newtons, controlled to within a fraction of a newton, since tension variation produces wire bow variation and thus wafer thickness and warp variation. The wire runs in **pilgrim mode**: forward several hundred meters, then back slightly less, then forward again, so that fresh wire is continuously introduced while each length of wire makes many passes. The reason is cost. The wire wears where it cuts and must be renewed, but a cut that used every metre of wire only once would consume tens of kilometres of expensive diamond wire per block. Reversing lets each length of wire cut many times in both directions, and the small net advance per cycle brings in fresh wire at roughly the rate it actually wears, of the order of a kilometre or a few kilometres of net new wire per block. The reversals are also the reason the cut leaves periodic marks: every time the wire stops and changes direction the cutting conditions change for a moment.

Komatsu NTC (Japan) dominates 300 mm semiconductor wire saws, with Takatori and the former Meyer Burger line, sold to Precision Surfacing Solutions in 2019, as alternatives. Diamond wire comes from Japanese suppliers such as Asahi Diamond Industrial, Nakamura Choko, and A.L.M.T., and increasingly from Chinese producers that grew up serving the solar industry.

### Slurry sawing versus diamond wire

Until roughly 2010–2015, wafers were cut by **loose-abrasive slurry sawing**: a bare steel wire (~140–160 µm) dragged silicon-carbide grit (F400–F600, ~10–17 µm; the F-numbers are the European FEPA grit-size grades, higher being finer) suspended in **polyethylene glycol (PEG)**, a viscous, water-miscible liquid that keeps the grit suspended and carries heat away, through the cut. This is **three-body abrasion**: free-rolling SiC grains pressed by the wire indent the silicon. Press a sharp grain into glass and a crack shoots straight down beneath it, the **median crack**; when the load is released, cracks running parallel to the surface, **lateral cracks**, open up and lift out a flake. The lateral cracks are the material removal; the median cracks stay behind as the damage layer. The surface is a mosaic of craters and the subsurface is a crack network 15–30 µm deep.

**Diamond wire sawing (DWS)** replaced this for **photovoltaic (PV)**, i.e. solar-cell, wafers and then for semiconductor wafers between ~2014 and 2018. Diamond grit of 8–25 µm is fixed to a 100–140 µm steel core by electroplated nickel, so the wire is the abrasive tool itself (**two-body abrasion**) and runs in water-based coolant. Diamond wire cuts 2–3 times faster, with thinner kerf (150–170 µm versus 200+ µm), a shallower damage layer (5–15 µm), no PEG/SiC waste, and better consistency because fixed grit wears slowly. Its signature is a set of parallel **saw marks** and a thin amorphous or phase-transformed layer. Under the extreme pressure beneath a diamond point, ~10 GPa, about 100,000 atmospheres, the open diamond-cubic lattice of silicon collapses into a denser arrangement, the same one white tin has (the metallic **Si-II** or **β-tin phase**), which flows like a metal instead of cracking; when the point passes and the pressure drops, the silicon cannot re-form the perfect crystal and freezes into disordered, **amorphous** silicon on unloading (or, if the pressure comes off slowly, into Si-III and Si-XII, two other metastable crystal forms of silicon that neither the diamond-cubic lattice nor a transistor has any use for). This is what makes **ductile-mode** removal possible: the silicon is smeared away rather than fractured, which is why fine fixed diamond leaves a thinner crack layer than loose grit. The picture has limits: the smeared skin is only tens of nanometres thick, and cracks still form beneath it wherever a grain is large or the load is high. The marks must be ground away downstream.

### Kerf loss and yield

Kerf is the width of the slot the wire cuts, roughly the wire diameter plus twice the protruding grit height plus wire vibration: a 120 µm core with 15 µm diamond gives a kerf of ~155–165 µm. For every 1.05 mm of ingot consumed, ~0.16 mm turns into silicon sludge. The kerf material is not recoverable for semiconductor use (contaminated with nickel, iron, and coolant), though it is sometimes sold for metallurgical or battery-anode applications.

> **Worked example: wafers from a 2 m ingot.** Take a 300 mm CZ ingot with a 2.0 m body (after crown and tail removal). Volume of the body = π × (0.15 m)² × 2.0 m = 0.141 m³; at 2,330 kg/m³ that is ~329 kg of silicon. Sectioning into six ~330 mm blocks costs one ~1 mm band-saw kerf per cut plus one ~3 mm test slug per block face: ~5 cuts × (1 + 6) mm ≈ 35 mm, leaving ~1,965 mm of sliceable length. With a diamond-wire pitch of 1.06 mm (900 µm as-cut + 160 µm kerf), the ingot yields 1,965 / 1.06 ≈ 1,850 slices. Discounting ~3% for broken or out-of-spec slices, ~1,800 wafers. Each finished 775 µm wafer has volume π × 15² × 0.0775 cm³ = 54.8 cm³ and mass ~128 g, so the 1,800 wafers weigh ~230 kg. Of the 329 kg body, ~70% ships as product, ~15% is kerf sludge, ~12% is ground and etched off the faces, and the rest is test slugs and breakage. Counting the crown, tail, and neck (typically another 15–25% of the pull), the total ingot-to-wafer mass yield is on the order of 55–60%.

> **Worked example: cut time and saw count.** Cut time is diameter divided by feed rate. At ~0.5 mm/min the wire takes 300 mm / 0.5 mm/min = 600 min = ~10 h to pass through the block; at ~1 mm/min it takes ~5 h. A ~330 mm block at 1.06 mm pitch gives ~310 slices. Allow ~8 h of cutting plus ~2 h to mount the block, thread and tension the web and unload the slices, and one saw finishes ~2.4 blocks per day, or ~740 wafers per saw-day. A plant shipping ~1 million wafers a month (~33,000 a day) therefore needs ~45 wire saws running around the clock, before allowing for maintenance and wire changes. All of these figures are illustrative; each maker's feed rate, block length and changeover time differ.

### What the saw leaves behind

Three things must be fixed downstream: (1) the **subsurface damage layer**, 5–30 µm of cracks, dislocations, and amorphous silicon that would nucleate slip in the fab; (2) **thickness variation and warp** from wire bow (the wire deflects several mm under cutting force) and thermal expansion of the block during a multi-hour cut, giving as-cut total thickness variation (TTV) of 10–20 µm and warp of 20–40 µm; (3) **saw marks** from wire vibration and pilgrim-mode reversals, tens of nanometers to a micrometer high. Grinding removes the saw marks and the thickness variation; the etch removes the damage layer; edge profiling, which sits between them in the flow, fixes a separate problem, the sharp rim.

> **What can go wrong:** Every slice is measured on a **capacitive thickness gauge** (two electrodes, one facing each face of the slice, whose capacitance to the silicon gives the two gap widths and hence the thickness without touching it) as it leaves the saw, and the thickness profile along the cut direction shows immediately whether tension or feed drifted; a batch with TTV above the grinder's correction budget is scrapped or downgraded. Warp is measured on a sample and traced to block heating or glue failure. Saw marks deeper than the grinder's removal show up later as **nanotopography** (millimetre-scale waviness, defined under Flatness Metrology) and are traced back to a specific saw and wire lot. The worst event is a wire break mid-cut: the web goes slack, the partly cut block can no longer be re-registered to the old cut planes to a micrometre, and the block is usually scrapped or re-cut at a new pitch with the partial slices lost, which is why wire tension and wire wear are monitored continuously and why saws stop automatically when tension falls.

## Lapping and Double-Disk Grinding

### Lapping

Classic wafering used **double-side lapping**: wafers in geared plastic carriers sit between two cast-iron plates rotating in opposite directions while an alumina (Al2O3, ~9–12 µm, "#1200") slurry is pumped between them. The carriers move in **planetary motion**: each spins on its own axis while orbiting the centre of the plates, like a planet, so every point of the wafer sees every point of the plate and no local plate error is copied into the wafer. Both faces are abraded by loose grit against two parallel reference plates, removing 40–60 µm per side, bringing TTV below ~1 µm, and erasing warp. It leaves a new damage layer 10–15 µm deep and is slow, messy, and hard to automate, but remains standard on 150 mm and many 200 mm lines.

### Double-disk grinding

For 300 mm the industry moved to **double-disk grinding (DDG)**, also called simultaneous double-side grinding. The wafer is held vertically by **hydrostatic water pads**, thin films of pressurised water on both faces so that nothing solid touches them, and rotated slowly while two opposed **cup wheels**, rings of diamond abrasive on the rim of a cup so that only a narrow annulus contacts the wafer, grind it from both sides. The wheel axes are offset from the wafer center, so the wheel's working face sweeps through the wafer center as it rotates, so that every point of the face, including the centre, is ground; a ring-shaped wheel spinning on the wafer's own axis would leave an unground island in the middle. Because the wafer is not clamped to a chuck, chuck-flatness errors do not **print through** (any bump on a chuck reappears as a dip in the ground face, because the wafer bends over the bump while it is ground and springs back afterwards), and because both sides are ground at once with a fixed-abrasive wheel, TTV of < 0.5 µm and near-zero induced warp are achievable. **Vitrified-bond** wheels, in which the diamonds are held in a glass matrix that is stiff and self-sharpening, of #2000 grit (~5 µm diamond) leave a damage layer of only ~2–5 µm. The rule of thumb behind that number: crack depth scales with grain size, roughly 0.5–1 times the grain diameter, so the sequence saw (8–25 µm grit) → #2000 (~5 µm) → #8000 (~1 µm) is a sequence of ever-shallower damage. Koyo Machinery (JTEKT, whose DXSG series pioneered 300 mm DDG) leads the tools, with other Japanese grinder makers as alternatives; removal is ~60–80 µm total at ~20–30 wafers per hour. Some lines add a fine single-side grind (#8000, ~1 µm resin-bond wheel, the softer bond that wears to expose fresh diamond) that takes front-face damage below 1 µm so the later etch can be shallower.

> **Worked example: how many grinders a plant needs.** At ~25 wafers per hour and 720 hours in a month, one DDG tool grinds ~18,000 wafers a month. A plant making ~1 million wafers a month needs 1,000,000 / 18,000 ≈ 55 grinders, before allowing for wheel changes and maintenance. The same arithmetic, with the polishing times derived later, is why a wafer plant is a hall of dozens of identical tools rather than one big machine.

> **What can go wrong:** As the wheel wears, its cutting face recedes and the wafer comes out thick or tapered; in-line thickness gauges on every wafer feed a correction back to the wheel position, and the wheel is periodically **dressed** (its surface re-opened with a hard block) to keep it cutting freely. The grinding marks form a radial or spiral pattern that, if not fully removed by etching and polishing, becomes nanotopography in the finished wafer. If the two water pads are not balanced the wafer is bent while it is ground and comes out bowed when released. Grinding too hard, or with too little coolant, heats the surface and opens thermal cracks that scrap the wafer.

> **Why it matters downstream:** DDG is where the wafer's flatness is actually made. The etch that follows is chosen to preserve flatness rather than create it, and polishing can only improve TTV by a fraction of a micrometre. So a DDG TTV below 1 µm is the precondition for the final TTV of < 0.5 µm and the ~20 nm site flatness the fab specifies; a wafer that leaves the grinder at 3 µm TTV will never be a leading-edge prime wafer.

## Edge Profiling

The edge of an as-cut wafer is square and razor-sharp, which is fatal. A sharp edge chips; a chip is a crack starter; and in any high-temperature step (a 1,000 °C oxidation, an epi deposition) thermal stress concentrates at the crack and either generates slip dislocations that propagate inward or fractures the wafer outright, filling a $10–30 million tool with shards. Edge chips also shed particles onto neighboring wafers in a **FOUP** (front-opening unified pod, the sealed 25-wafer carrier used inside the fab; Module 05), and a square edge makes photoresist bead up during spin coating and copper plate thicker at the sharp corner during copper plating (Module 12).

> **Intuition: why a tiny chip matters.** A crack in a brittle plate concentrates stress at its tip, the way a small nick in a plastic bag lets you tear it with almost no effort. At 1,000 °C silicon's **yield stress** (the stress beyond which it deforms permanently instead of springing back) is low, the stress at the tip of a chip at the rim exceeds it, and dislocations run inward from the edge along the crystal's slip planes. A chip a tenth of a millimetre across at the rim ends up as a slip line running across a die 20 mm away, and that die fails. Rounding the edge removes the sharp corners where chips start; polishing the rounded edge removes the microcracks that the rounding itself left.

So the edge is ground with a formed diamond wheel whose profile is the negative of the desired shape. SEMI M1 defines the envelope, nominally a 22.5° **bevel** (the sloped face between the wafer's face and its rim) with rounded transitions fitting a template; modern makers use a round or elliptical profile of ~0.2–0.3 mm radius. The notch is profiled at the same time with a small pin-shaped wheel. The tool is a single-wafer machine: the wafer is held on a vacuum chuck, rotated, and its rim fed against the spinning formed wheel in a coarse pass and a fine pass, with an optical profile check on the machine; cycle times are of the order of ~1 minute per wafer (makers quote them per model and wafer makers do not publish theirs), so at ~1,400 wafers per tool per day the ~33,000-wafer-a-day plant of the slicing examples needs ~25–30 edge grinders running around the clock. Edge grinders come from Japanese specialists such as Daitron and SpeedFam (which also builds the edge polishers used later); Disco, better known for the dicing saws and back-grinders of Module 16, is sometimes listed as well.

> **Worked example: how little the profile removes, and why that is the point.** Rounding each of the two square corners of the rim to a radius r ≈ 0.25 mm removes, in cross-section, the corner area outside a quarter circle: r² − πr²/4 = r² (1 − π/4) = 0.0625 × 0.215 ≈ 0.013 mm² per corner, ~0.027 mm² for both. Multiply by the circumference, π × 300 mm ≈ 942 mm, and the whole profile grind removes ~25 mm³ of silicon, about 60 mg, from a 128 g wafer. Compare the 60–80 µm the double-disk grinder takes off the faces: 707 cm² × 0.006–0.008 cm × 2.33 g/cm³ ≈ 10–13 g. Edge profiling is not a removal step at all; it is a shape step, and what it costs is not silicon but a fresh damage layer on the bevel, a few micrometres deep like any fine grind, which is why the bevel is later etched with the faces and then mirror-polished. The 1.5 mm edge exclusion is set by that bevel and the polishing roll-off beside it, not by the profile's width: the rounded zone itself occupies only ~0.3 mm of radius.

The edge also carries the **edge exclusion**: the outer ring of the wafer, now 1.5 mm wide, in which no flatness or defect specification applies and on which the fab places no die. Two edge-flatness metrics live in this region and are defined with the flatness vocabulary later: **ZDD**, the curvature (second derivative) of the height profile near the edge, and **ESFQR**, the edge-site version of the site-flatness metric SFQR. Edge roll-off and the mirror polishing of the edge are consequences of polishing, and are covered after the polishing sections.

> **What can go wrong:** Edge chips are the classic failure. Every wafer passes an edge inspection station in which a camera, or a laser scatter tool, scans the bevel all the way round and flags chips, cracks and profile deviations; a chipped wafer is scrapped by the wafer maker before it can break in a furnace and take a hundred neighbours with it. A profile wheel that has worn changes the bevel angle, which the same inspection catches as a template mismatch.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Pushing the edge exclusion from 3 mm to 1.5 mm moves the usable boundary from a radius of 147 mm to 148.5 mm. The ring between them has an area of π × (148.5² − 147²) ≈ 1,400 mm², the area of ~14 dies at a typical 100 mm² (10 mm × 10 mm) logic die. A die only counts if it fits entirely inside the boundary, so the real gain is the partial dies along the rim that used to straddle the 3 mm line and now fit inside the 1.5 mm line: a standard gross-die estimate (π R² / A minus a perimeter term of π × 2R / √(2A)) gives ~615 whole 100 mm² dies at 3 mm exclusion and ~625–630 at 1.5 mm, roughly 13 more dies, or ~2%, from the same wafer. Not all of them yield: the outermost ring is where the wafer's edge roll-off and the fab's own edge non-uniformities concentrate, and edge dies yield worse than centre dies, so perhaps half to two-thirds of those 13 become good dies (fabs do not publish edge-die yields). Even so, on a leading-edge wafer each good die is worth hundreds of dollars, so a 1.5 mm edge exclusion is worth of the order of a thousand dollars per wafer, ten times the price of the wafer itself. That is why the wafer maker's edge profile, edge polish and edge flatness are now specified as tightly as the centre of the wafer.

## Etching to Remove Damage

After grinding, the wafer carries ~2–15 µm of mechanical damage on each face and edge. Grinding it away with an even finer wheel would just leave finer damage; only chemistry removes damage without creating it. The wafer is therefore etched to remove 20–30 µm total, well past the deepest crack.

> **Intuition: diffusion-limited versus reaction-limited.** Every wet etch involves two speeds: how fast fresh reagent can reach the surface, and how fast the surface chemistry can consume it. In a **diffusion-limited** etch the chemistry is faster than supply, so the rate is set by how the liquid moves; edges and corners, which stick out into fresh liquid, etch faster than the middle of a face, and the wafer thins at the rim and loses the flatness the grinder gave it. In a **reaction-limited** etch fresh reagent is always in excess and every point etches at the rate the surface chemistry allows, regardless of flow, so the shape set by grinding is preserved. That one distinction is why alkaline etching wins on flatness and acid etching wins on smoothness.

Two chemistries are used, and the choice is a trade-off between flatness and surface quality.

**Acid etching** uses HF/HNO3 mixtures, often with acetic or phosphoric acid as a diluent and wetting agent (a classic ratio is HF : HNO3 : CH3COOH = 1 : 3 : 1 to 1 : 5 : 2, by volume). The mechanism is a two-step redox: nitric acid oxidizes the silicon surface to SiO2 (releasing colourless nitric oxide, NO, which oxidises in air to brown nitrogen dioxide, NO2, hence the brown fumes over an acid-etch bath), and HF dissolves the oxide to hexafluorosilicic acid, H2SiF6, which is water-soluble. Overall: 3 Si + 4 HNO3 + 18 HF → 3 H2SiF6 + 4 NO + 8 H2O. The etch is **isotropic** (the same rate in every direction) and the surface is smooth and glossy. But the reaction is fast and **exothermic** (it releases heat, so the bath warms itself and the rate runs away unless it is cooled), and it is diffusion-limited, so the etch rate depends on local flow and on the layer of depleted acid at the surface; wafers come out with degraded flatness (TTV can grow by several µm) and edge thinning. Acid etch also produces large volumes of **NOx** (nitrogen oxides, the brown fumes) and spent fluoride waste.

**Alkaline etching** uses concentrated KOH or NaOH (45–55 **wt%**, percent by weight) at 80–90 °C. Hydroxide attacks silicon directly: Si + 2 OH^- + 2 H2O → SiO2(OH)2^2- + 2 H2. The reaction is reaction-rate-limited rather than diffusion-limited, so removal is uniform and flatness from grinding is preserved to within a fraction of a micrometer. It is also strongly **anisotropic** (direction-dependent), etching (100) planes tens to a few hundred times faster than (111). The reason is atomic packing: the (111) plane is the most densely packed, and each atom on it exposes only one bond to the liquid instead of two, so hydroxide attacks it slowest and the surface ends up tiled with facets of that slow plane. The etch therefore leaves a faceted, matte surface of tiny (111)-bounded pyramids and pits with roughness of a few hundred nm. The surface must then be polished off, which is fine since polishing follows anyway. Potassium is a **mobile ion**: K+ ions drift through a transistor's gate oxide under voltage and shift its switching threshold over time, so every trace must be removed, and alkaline-etched wafers are followed by an acid clean. Many wafer makers run a two-stage sequence: an alkaline etch for most of the removal followed by a light acid etch for smoothness.

The tool is a batch wet bench: a row of temperature-controlled, recirculated and filtered tanks into which a **cassette** (an open rack that holds 25 wafers on edge) or two of wafers, 25–50 at a time, is lowered by a robot, followed by rinse tanks and a dryer. The wet-bench makers named under Cleaning supply these, alongside smaller specialists.

> **Worked example: etch time.** Concentrated KOH (~50 wt%) at ~85 °C etches (100) silicon at roughly ~1 µm/min. Removing ~12 µm per face, with both faces etched at once in the tank, therefore takes ~12 minutes for a cassette of 25–50 wafers. An HF/HNO3 acid etch runs several times faster, but the wafers come out with several micrometres more TTV, which no later step can fully recover.

> **What can go wrong:** The KOH reaction releases hydrogen, and a bubble that clings to the surface masks the etch beneath it, leaving a pit; agitation and **surfactants** (soap-like additives that lower the liquid's surface tension) keep the bubbles moving. If the acid ratio drifts, a **stain film** (a brown porous silicon layer) forms instead of a clean surface. An acid etch that runs too hot gives an **orange-peel** texture, and its flatness loss is measured directly as TTV growth between the grinder and the polisher. Potassium left behind by an inadequate post-etch acid clean is caught by the TXRF (total-reflection X-ray fluorescence) surface-metal analysis described under inspection, and the whole batch is recleaned.

For 300 mm prime wafers, the alkaline-first approach is the norm because flatness is the dearer commodity. Some flows, with DDG damage of only 2–3 µm, have shrunk the etch to under 10 µm. After etching, the wafer is at ~790–800 µm and is, for the first time, free of mechanical damage.

## Laser Marking

This is a short side step. Every 300 mm wafer carries a laser-scribed identity so that fabs can track lots and individual wafers through hundreds of steps and, when a yield problem appears, trace it back to a crystal position, an ingot, a block, and a polishing tool. SEMI standards govern the mark. **SEMI M12** (12 characters) and its sibling **M13** (18 characters) specify an alphanumeric code in an **OCR**-readable font (optical character recognition: readable by both people and cameras, the "SEMI font"), written as a field of laser-drilled dots, placed within a few millimeters of the edge, 180° or adjacent to the notch, on the front surface. **SEMI T7** specifies a 2D **data-matrix** code (a square barcode of dots) written on the **back** surface of double-side-polished 300 mm wafers, which is what most leading-edge fabs actually read; the T7 mark is in a defined location and encodes the same wafer ID.

The marks are written with a **Q-switched** laser, one that stores energy and releases it in nanosecond pulses that vaporise a dot without heating its surroundings, either an **Nd:YAG** (a common solid-state laser crystal, neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet) or a fiber laser at 1,064 or 532 nm, as fields of dots ~50–100 µm across; a **soft mark** is ~1–2 µm deep, a **hard mark** several µm deep and survives the entire fab flow including CMP. Marking is done before polishing so that polishing smooths the **recast rim**, the lip of re-solidified silicon around each laser pit; a mark written afterward would shed laser-ejected silicon droplets as particles. Wafer-marking lasers come from a few specialist suppliers, mostly Japanese and American.

## Polishing I: Double-Side Polishing (DSP)

### The mechanism of chemical-mechanical polishing

Polishing silicon to an atomically smooth surface is not fine grinding. It is a chemical-mechanical process in which an alkaline liquid hydrates and softens the top **monolayers** (single atomic layers) of silicon into a hydrated silica-like film, and nanometer-sized silica particles carried by a compliant polyurethane pad shear that film away, exposing fresh silicon to be hydrated again. The chemistry is the KOH etch you just met, throttled back: hydroxide ions and water attack Si–Si bonds and convert the outermost atoms to Si–OH groups, but at pH 10–11 rather than in 50 wt% KOH the attack is roughly a thousand times slower, so only the outermost layers are softened, and the pad and particles wipe that soft layer away as fast as it forms. The abrasive, **colloidal** amorphous SiO2 (colloidal means particles so small, tens of nanometres, that they never settle out of the liquid) with a Mohs hardness of ~6, is softer than silicon (~7), so it cannot scratch the crystal; it only removes the chemically weakened surface. This is the reason polished silicon has no damage layer at all.

The slurry is **colloidal silica**, 30–80 nm spherical particles at 1–10 wt% in water, stabilized at pH 10–11.5 with KOH, NH4OH, or an **amine** (an organic base derived from ammonia; amines are preferred for the final polish because they leave no alkali metal such as potassium or sodium behind); Fujimi (Glanzox) and Nitta DuPont are the dominant suppliers. The pad is polyurethane, either a **napped poromeric** (a fibrous, sponge-like surface, Suba-type, softer, for finishing) or a cast microporous sheet (IC1000-type, harder, for stock removal and flatness), from Qnity (the former DuPont electronics business, spun off as an independent company in November 2025, which inherited the ex-Rodel pad line), Fujibo, and Nitta DuPont (the Japanese joint venture, which kept its name through the spin-off).

Removal rate follows **Preston's equation**: RR = k_p × P × v, where RR is thickness removed per unit time, P is the pressure (~100–300 g/cm², i.e. 10–30 kPa), v is the relative pad-wafer velocity (~0.5–1.5 m/s), and k_p is a constant capturing chemistry and pad state. In words: within a calibrated polishing process, pressing harder or moving the pad faster raises the removal rate. The coefficient k_p packages the effects of the slurry chemistry and the condition of the pad. It is not a universal constant: changing either can change the rate even if pressure and speed stay the same. For silicon stock polishing the rate is ~0.5–1 µm/min; for final polishing ~0.05–0.2 µm/min.

> **Worked example: Preston's constant and polishing time.** Take a stock-removal rate RR = 0.7 µm/min at P = 20 kPa and v = 1 m/s. Converting the rate to SI, 0.7 × 10^-6 m / 60 s = 1.17 × 10^-8 m/s, so k_p = 1.17 × 10^-8 / (20,000 Pa × 1 m/s) ≈ 6 × 10^-13 Pa^-1. Removing ~9 µm per face, the DSP allocation in the capstone below, takes 9 / 0.7 ≈ 13 min. Halving the pressure to protect flatness and reduce edge roll-off halves the rate and doubles the time to ~26 min, and a plant polishing ~1 million wafers a month, several wafers per load, needs dozens of DSP tools to keep up. That is the trade a polishing engineer makes every day: pressure buys throughput and costs flatness.

### The DSP tool

In **double-side polishing**, wafers are loaded into thin (slightly thinner than the wafer) glass-epoxy or stainless **carriers**, plates with gear teeth and cut-outs that hold several wafers per carrier, several carriers per load. The carriers sit between two large polishing **platens** (the rotating plates that carry the pads), each faced with pad, and are driven planetary-style by a **sun gear and ring gear** (a central gear and an outer toothed ring that between them spin each carrier on its own axis while sweeping it around the platen), exactly the geometry of lapping. Slurry is fed through holes in the upper platen, and the pads are **conditioned** between loads by a diamond-grit disk that re-opens their pores. Because the wafer floats between two pads with no chuck, there is no chuck-induced print-through, and because both sides are polished at once, the wafer comes out parallel: DSP achieves TTV < 0.3 µm and the global flatness that no single-side process can match. DSP removes ~15–25 µm total (both faces), erasing the alkaline-etch facets, and leaves both surfaces as mirrors. Both faces end up **specular** (mirror-like), which is why a modern 300 mm wafer has a mirror back side (200 mm wafers usually had an etched, matte back). Precision Surfacing Solutions (the former Lapmaster Wolters group, whose Peter Wolters AC2000 series is built in Germany), Fujikoshi, and SpeedFam supply DSP tools; a wafer fab runs dozens of them.

> **What can go wrong:** The gear-toothed carrier and the grooves in the pad both press a faint pattern into the wafer, which appears as nanotopography and is traced back to a specific carrier by its pattern. A single agglomerate of slurry particles, or a fragment of pad debris, drags a scratch across the mirror; point-of-use filtration and the Surfscan inspection described later catch it. A pad whose pores have closed up (**glazing**) loses removal rate until it is conditioned. A slurry whose pH has drifted down, from absorbed carbon dioxide or from consumed alkali, hydrates the surface more slowly and the rate falls, which the in-line thickness measurement reveals as under-polished wafers. And the pad wrapping over the rim thins the outermost millimetres, the edge roll-off discussed next.

## Polishing II: Final Front-Side Mirror Polish and the Edge

### The haze-free touch polish

The DSP surface is a mirror, but it has a residual **haze**, a faint milky scatter, visible under a bright light, from roughness of ~0.2–0.5 nm RMS, plus a low density of micro-scratches from the harder stock-removal pad. The final polish, sometimes called "haze-free" or "touch" polishing, uses a soft napped pad, dilute fine colloidal silica (20–40 nm) with an amine base, low pressure, and removes only ~0.5–2 µm from the front side. The recipe, in the terms of Preston's equation from the DSP section: pressure ~10 kPa (~100 g/cm², the bottom of the 10–30 kPa stock-polishing range) so that the soft pad barely deforms the surface; pad speed ~0.5–1 m/s; slurry at ~1 wt% solids or below, against several wt% for stock removal, and buffered with an amine rather than KOH so that no potassium is left behind; a removal rate of ~0.05–0.2 µm/min; and the pad flushed with DI water at the end of the cycle so that no slurry dries on the wafer. It is a single-side process: the wafer is held on a template or a soft backing film by surface tension or vacuum (some lines use a ceramic chuck with wax mounting, which gives the best flatness but leaves wax to clean off). Multi-head tools polish four or five wafers on a single large platen, each under its own pressure-controlled head. Okamoto (whose PNX332B is an automatic 300 mm final polisher), SpeedFam (SPAW series single-side polishers) and Fujikoshi are the Japanese single-side polisher makers, alongside the DSP makers above. The result is a surface with roughness below 0.1 nm RMS on a 1 µm × 1 µm **atomic-force-microscope (AFM)** scan (an AFM drags an atomically sharp needle across the surface and maps its height at atomic scale), which is to say the surface is flat to within a single atomic step (a silicon (100) monatomic step is 0.136 nm).

> **Worked example: the touch polish by Preston's equation.** Take the Preston constant found under DSP, k_p ≈ 6 × 10^-13 Pa^-1, and halve both the pressure and the speed: P = 10 kPa, v = 0.5 m/s gives RR = 6 × 10^-13 × 10,000 × 0.5 = 3 × 10^-9 m/s ≈ 0.18 µm/min, the top of the observed 0.05–0.2 µm/min range; the diluted slurry and softer pad lower k_p and bring real rates toward the middle of it. At ~0.1 µm/min, the ~1.5 µm the capstone below allocates to the final polish takes ~15 min. With four or five wafers on a platen and a few minutes to load and unload, one tool finishes ~13–18 wafers per hour, ~10,000–13,000 a month, so the ~1 million-wafer-a-month plant of the earlier examples needs ~75–100 final polishers, more than it needs grinders. Dropping the pressure further to protect the edge roll-off would lengthen every cycle in proportion, which is why the touch polish is kept as thin as the haze allows.

Why does the surface need to be that smooth? Three reasons. First, gate dielectrics on modern transistors are ~1–2 nm of interfacial oxide plus **high-k** (the hafnium-oxide gate insulator, Module 11); roughness comparable to the oxide thickness modulates the electric field, causes **tunneling** leakage (electrons passing straight through an insulator that is too thin), and scatters carriers, degrading mobility. Second, inspection: a laser-scattering particle counter looking for 26 nm defects sees haze as background noise; a hazy wafer cannot be inspected to spec. Third, bonding: silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers, made by bonding two wafers together as described at the end of this module, and **hybrid bonding** (joining two finished wafers face to face by direct atomic contact, Module 17) require surfaces with < 0.2–0.5 nm RMS roughness to bond at all.

### The edge after polishing: roll-off and mirror edge

Two subtler edge parameters have become critical for leading-edge lithography. **Edge roll-off (ERO)** is the tendency of polishing to thin the last 1–3 mm of the wafer because the pad wraps around the edge. It is measured as ZDD (the second derivative, i.e. the curvature, of the height profile) or as ESFQR (edge-site flatness), and ERO of more than a few tens of nanometers throws the scanner out of focus on the outermost dies. Because the fab's yield near the edge is worth hundreds of dollars per wafer, edge exclusion has been pushed from 3 mm (early 300 mm) to 2 mm and now to 1.5 mm, which means the polished flat zone must extend to a 297 mm diameter, i.e. to within 1.5 mm of the rim on every side (a radius of 148.5 mm). Second, the edge itself is polished to a mirror (**mirror edge polish**, step 9 of the flow at the top of the module) with a soft pad and colloidal silica, to eliminate the microcracks left by the profile grind and the resulting particle shedding; the wafer is spun against a polishing drum or a pad-covered wheel whose face is shaped to the bevel, one wafer at a time, on tools from the edge-machine makers named under Edge Profiling. Where edge polishing sits in the flow varies by maker: after the etch, and either before or after DSP.

> **What can go wrong:** Micro-scratches from pad debris and residual wax from mounting are the classic final-polish defects, both caught as scratches and clusters of light-point defects on the Surfscan. Chemistry that is too aggressive for the soft pad leaves haze, caught as an elevated haze reading. Edge roll-off beyond spec is caught by the edge-flatness metrics (ESFQR, ZDD) on the flatness tool, and a wafer that fails them is downgraded to a product with a wider edge exclusion.

## Capstone: Where the 115 µm Went

> **Worked example: the removal stack-up from raw slice to 775 µm.** Start with a diamond-wire as-cut slice of 890 ± 15 µm (TTV ~15 µm). Double-disk grinding removes ~35 µm per face, 70 µm total, to ~820 µm (TTV now < 1 µm, damage ~3 µm per face). Alkaline etch removes ~12 µm per face, 25 µm total, to ~795 µm (damage 0, but faceted, and TTV degrades slightly to ~0.5–1 µm). Edge profiling, laser marking and edge polishing remove nothing from the faces. DSP removes ~9 µm per face, 18 µm total, to ~777 µm (TTV < 0.3 µm, both faces mirror). Final front-side CMP removes ~1.5 µm to ~775.5 µm. RCA cleaning removes < 1 nm. Total face removal: ~115 µm, or 13% of the raw slice, and 11% of the 1.05 mm of ingot consumed per wafer; add the 160 µm kerf and the finished wafer represents ~74% of the crystal length it came from. The intermediate thickness targets are set backward from 775 µm, and the as-cut target (890 µm here) is the one number a wafer maker adjusts to compensate for changes in any downstream removal.

A wafer maker tunes the flow from the end: the final thickness is fixed at 775 µm by SEMI M1, the polishing removals are fixed by the roughness and flatness the polishers need to achieve, the etch removal is fixed by the depth of grinding damage, and the grinding removal is fixed by the depth of saw damage plus the as-cut TTV. Whatever those add up to, plus a safety margin, is the as-cut target, and when a new saw or wire cuts with shallower damage the target is lowered and one more wafer is won from every few hundred millimetres of ingot.

## Cleaning: The RCA Sequence and Drying

Every mechanical and chemical step above leaves residues: slurry silica, polishing-pad debris, organic surfactants, wax, and metals from the slurry, the KOH, the stainless-steel tooling, and the etch baths. The final clean has to remove all of them without roughening the polished surface, and it has to leave the surface in a state that survives weeks of shipping and storage. The industry's answer, still, is a descendant of the sequence Werner Kern developed at RCA in 1965 and published in 1970, the **RCA clean**.

The tool comes in two forms. A **batch wet bench** lowers a cassette of wafers through a row of tanks. For a shipped prime wafer one canonical bench sequence is: SC-1, rinse, SC-2, rinse, dilute HF, rinse, a final dilute SC-1 (or ozonated water), rinse, Marangoni dry; the chemistries are defined in the subsections below. The HF dip strips the oxide the two standard cleans grew, and the final SC-1 or ozone step regrows a thin, clean one, because a wafer must not be shipped with the bare, hydrophobic surface HF leaves. Makers differ in the details: some omit the HF dip and ship the SC-2 oxide, some swap the order of SC-1 and SC-2, and all differ in dilution and temperature. The bench is the workhorse of wafer makers because bare wafers can be cleaned aggressively and cheaply 25 or 50 at a time. A **single-wafer spin chamber** sprays each chemistry in turn onto one wafer spinning on a chuck, gives every wafer an identical, fresh chemistry, and is what fabs use on patterned wafers. Megasonic transducers sit in the SC-1 tank of a bench (or in a nozzle on a spin tool), and the Marangoni dryer is the last station of a bench; both are explained below. All the ratios in this section are by volume.

### SC-1: particles and organics

**Standard Clean 1 (SC-1, also APM, ammonia-peroxide mixture)** is NH4OH : H2O2 : H2O, originally 1 : 1 : 5 at 75–80 °C, now usually much more dilute (1 : 1 : 50 to 1 : 2 : 100) at 40–65 °C. Its mechanism is what makes it effective against particles. Hydrogen peroxide oxidizes the silicon surface, growing a ~0.6–1 nm chemical oxide; simultaneously the ammonium hydroxide etches that oxide slowly (silicon consumption ~0.1–0.5 nm/min in dilute SC-1). The surface is therefore continuously regrown and re-etched, and any particle sitting on it is undercut and released. Once released, it does not redeposit: at pH ~10 both the silica-covered wafer surface and most particles (silica, most organics, silicon) carry a negative **zeta potential** (the effective electric charge a surface carries when immersed in water, which depends on the pH), so they repel electrostatically. Peroxide also oxidizes organic residues to CO2 and water. Too aggressive an SC-1 (high NH4OH, high temperature) etches the silicon non-uniformly and raises roughness from 0.1 to 0.3+ nm, so the modern trend is dilute, cool SC-1 with megasonic assist.

> **Worked example: how much silicon a clean consumes.** Dilute SC-1 consumes ~0.2 nm/min of silicon. A 10 min bath removes ~2 nm, which at 0.136 nm per atomic layer is ~15 atomic layers, comfortably enough to undercut a 30 nm particle. Run a comparable clean ten times over a wafer's life in the fab and 20 nm of silicon is gone from every exposed surface, which is one reason fabs moved from the original 1 : 1 : 5 recipe to dilute, cool SC-1: the original consumed several times more.

### SC-2: metals

SC-1's weakness is that at high pH, metals such as Fe, Al, Zn, and Ca precipitate as hydroxides onto the surface (**hydroxide precipitation**: at high pH dissolved metal ions turn into insoluble solids such as Fe(OH)3 that settle on the wafer), and the peroxide can carry trace metals from its own manufacture. **Standard Clean 2 (SC-2, HPM, hydrochloric-peroxide mixture)** is HCl : H2O2 : H2O, 1 : 1 : 6 at 70–80 °C (or dilute, 1 : 1 : 50). The acid dissolves the metal hydroxides and holds metals in solution as soluble chlorides and **chloro-complexes** (metal ions with several chloride ions bound around them, a form that stays dissolved rather than settling on the wafer); the peroxide keeps the metals in their higher oxidation states so they do not plate out onto silicon by **galvanic displacement**, in which a metal ion steals electrons from the silicon and deposits as metal, the way copper plates onto an iron nail dropped in copper sulphate (copper is the notorious case: Cu2+ in HF solutions plates onto bare silicon within seconds). SC-2 does not attack silicon and does not remove particles; it is purely a metal-removal step.

### HF dip

A dilute HF dip (DHF, 0.5–1% HF for ~30–60 s) removes the chemical oxide and with it any metals incorporated in the oxide. It leaves a **hydrogen-terminated** silicon surface (every surface silicon atom capped with a hydrogen atom), which is **hydrophobic** (water beads up on it rather than wetting it) and remarkably stable in air for minutes to hours, but attracts particles strongly and re-oxidizes unevenly. The particle problem is electrostatic again: a **hydrophilic** (water-wetting) oxide surface in water carries a **hydration layer**, a bound film of water molecules and charge that particles must push through, whereas the hydrophobic surface has none. For shipped wafers, the last step is therefore usually a final dilute SC-1 or **ozonated water** (DIO3, 5–50 ppm, parts per million by weight, of ozone (O3) dissolved in ultrapure water, increasingly used as a substitute for peroxide because it is cleaner and cheaper) to regrow a uniform hydrophilic chemical oxide. That 0.6–1 nm oxide is what the fab receives, and the fab's own pre-gate clean will strip it.

### Megasonics

Cleaning tools drive the liquid with **megasonic** transducers at 0.8–1 MHz (versus 20–100 kHz for ultrasonics). Sound in a liquid creates **cavitation**: microscopic bubbles that form in the low-pressure half of each cycle and collapse in the high-pressure half. At ~1 MHz the cavitation bubbles are only a few µm in size, so their collapse and the **acoustic streaming** they drive (a steady flow set up by the sound wave itself, as the momentum of the absorbed wave is handed to the liquid) in the **boundary layer** (the thin, nearly stagnant film of liquid next to the wafer that ordinary flow cannot stir) dislodge particles down to ~30–50 nm without the damage that violent kilohertz cavitation causes; bare polished wafers can be cleaned aggressively, patterned wafers in the fab cannot.

### Drying and Marangoni

Drying is where a clean wafer is most easily ruined. If a droplet evaporates on silicon, dissolved **silicic acid** (silicon dioxide dissolved in water) and contaminants stay behind as a **watermark**, a ring of silica a few nm thick that is a real defect. Spin drying leaves droplets at the edge and in the notch. The solution is the **Marangoni** dryer, also called the IPA-vapor dryer after **isopropyl alcohol (IPA)**, the common rubbing alcohol: the wafer is drawn slowly (a few mm/s) out of a bath of **deionised (DI) water** (ultrapure water with every dissolved ion removed) while nitrogen carrying IPA vapor is directed at the **meniscus**, the curved water surface where it meets the wafer. IPA dissolving into the meniscus lowers its surface tension (pure IPA ~21 mN/m versus water ~72 mN/m; surface tension is measured in millinewtons per metre of contact line), and the resulting surface-tension gradient, low at the wafer contact line and high in the bulk, pulls the liquid off the wafer and back into the bath. Liquid is always dragged toward the region of higher surface tension, because the stronger "skin" there wins the tug-of-war with the weaker skin next to it (touch a soapy finger to a still water surface and the water rushes away from it), so the thin film clinging to the wafer, where the IPA has weakened the skin, is pulled toward the bulk bath and off the silicon. The wafer emerges with no liquid film, so nothing evaporates and nothing is left behind. Single-wafer tools use the equivalent "Rotagoni" process (a term coined at imec around 2000 and licensed to spin-processor makers: an IPA/N2 nozzle following the receding water film on a spinning wafer). SCREEN Semiconductor Solutions leads wet cleaning tools, with TEL, SEMES, Lam, and ACM Research also in the market.

> **What can go wrong:** A watermark shows up on the Surfscan (described next) as a ring-shaped cluster of light-point defects, usually near the edge or the notch, and points to a drying fault. Roughening from an over-aggressive SC-1 shows up as a rise in the wafer's haze reading across the whole surface. Copper plated onto silicon by galvanic displacement in a contaminated HF bath is invisible to the eye but is caught by TXRF, the surface-metal analysis described next, as a copper count far above 10^10 atoms/cm². In each case the batch is recleaned once, and repeated failures are downgraded while the bath chemistry is replaced.

## Flatness Metrology

Flatness is the wafer property the fab cares about most, because every lithography exposure focuses a pattern onto the surface, and the depth of focus of the scanner is on the order of 100 nm.

> **Intuition: a camera with a razor-thin depth of field.** A scanner is a camera with a razor-thin depth of field. Its **numerical aperture (NA)** is a measure of how wide a cone of light the lens gathers; a higher NA gives finer resolution but a thinner slab of sharp focus, and for a 0.33 NA EUV scanner that slab is on the order of 100–150 nm total. Everything that sits between the lens and the transistor competes for that slab: the scanner's own focus error, the flatness of the chuck, the topography of the films already on the wafer, and the flatness of the wafer itself. The wafer maker is allowed only a small slice of it.

The scanner does not need the whole wafer to be flat. It exposes one 26 mm × 33 mm field at a time by sweeping a 26 mm × 8 mm slit of light across it, and before each field it measures the local surface, tilts and refocuses. So only the height variation the slit sees within a site, after the best tilt has been removed, reaches the focus budget; that residual is SFQR, defined below.

The measurement is done with a **Fizeau interferometer**: light reflected from the wafer surface is combined with light reflected from a reference flat, and where the two path lengths differ by half a wavelength the light cancels, so the wafer's height map appears as a set of bright and dark fringes that are contour lines a half-wavelength (~0.3 µm) apart. Shifting the reference by fractions of a wavelength and recording several images ("phase stepping") resolves the height between fringes to nanometres. Two interferometers facing each other, one on each side of a vertically held wafer, give both surfaces at once, and from them thickness (front minus back) and shape (their average) fall out directly; the tool measures the wafer's free shape and computes what the front surface would look like with the back pulled flat against a chuck.

SEMI M1 defines the vocabulary below, and a family of SEMI MF test-method standards (the former ASTM F-series, one method per quantity) says how each term is measured: MF533 for thickness and TTV, MF534 for bow, MF657 and MF1390 for warp (and TTV) by non-contact scanning, MF1451 for **sori** (a warp-like shape metric referenced to a best-fit plane rather than a three-point plane, used mainly in Japanese specifications), and SEMI M43 for reporting nanotopography. The terms:

- **TTV (total thickness variation)**: max minus min thickness over the whole wafer, excluding the edge exclusion. Prime 300 mm: < 1 µm; leading-edge specs often < 0.5 µm.
- **Bow**: deviation of the center of the **median surface** (the mid-plane halfway between the front and back surfaces) from the **reference plane** defined by three points near the edge, measured on a free (unclamped) wafer. Sign indicates concave or convex.
- **Warp**: max minus min deviation of the median surface from the reference plane on a free wafer. Prime 300 mm: < 30 µm, with leading-edge specs of < 10–15 µm. Bow and warp are properties of the wafer's shape independent of thickness, while TTV is independent of shape. A wafer can be perfectly parallel (TTV 0) and still warped like a potato chip.
- **GBIR (global back-surface-referenced ideal-plane range)**: global front-surface flatness with the back chucked flat; essentially equals TTV.
- **SFQR (site front-surface-referenced least-squares range)**: for each lithography site (standardized as 26 mm × 8 mm, a scanner slit, though fabs also request 26 × 33 mm full fields), fit a **least-squares plane** (the flat plane that best fits the surface, minimising the squared deviations) to the front surface with the back chucked flat and report the peak-to-valley range around it; the spec is the maximum over all sites or the percent of usable sites. Early 300 mm: < 130 nm. Leading-edge EUV nodes: ~20 nm, with edge sites (ESFQR) specified separately. SFQR models what a scanner's leveling cannot correct: it tilts and refocuses per site, so only the residual within a site matters.
- **Nanotopography (NT)**: front-surface height variation over lateral scales of 0.2–20 mm on a free wafer with long-wavelength shape filtered out, reported as peak-to-valley in 2 mm and 10 mm windows; specs ~< 8–16 nm in a 2 mm window. NT was defined in the late 1990s when **shallow-trench-isolation (STI) CMP** (the polish that planarises the oxide-filled trenches separating transistors, Modules 06 and 12) was found to thin oxide over millimeter-scale bumps too small for SFQR to catch; wire-saw marks and DSP carrier print-through are the sources.
- **Edge exclusion (EE)**: the annulus where nothing above is specified: 3 mm at 300 mm's introduction, 2 mm through the 2010s, 1.5 mm now for leading-edge, with 1 mm under discussion.

> **Worked example: an illustrative focus budget.** Suppose a 0.33 NA EUV scanner has ~120 nm of usable depth of focus. Assign, illustratively, ~30 nm to the scanner's own focus and leveling error, ~20 nm to chuck flatness, ~40 nm to the topography of the films already on the wafer after earlier process steps, and ~20 nm to the wafer's SFQR, and the sum is ~110 nm with ~10 nm to spare. Real budgets combine the terms statistically rather than adding them, which buys a little room, but the arithmetic shows why a 20 nm SFQR spec is not conservative: the wafer's share is about a sixth of the whole budget, and a wafer at 40 nm SFQR would consume the margin on its own.

KLA's WaferSight series (from the 2006 acquisition of ADE) holds the wafer vertically between two Fizeau interferometers that measure both surfaces at once in seconds, yielding thickness, flatness, shape, and nanotopography; Corning Tropel's FlatMaster is the usual alternative. Every prime wafer is measured, and one that fails SFQR on a few sites is downgraded rather than scrapped.

> **What can go wrong:** A wafer that fails SFQR on a handful of sites is sold to a product with a looser spec, so flatness failures cost margin rather than wafers. Nanotopography failures are the more instructive kind: a periodic pattern in the NT map is a fingerprint, and the wafer maker matches its spacing and orientation to a wire saw's marks or a DSP carrier's gear pattern, then pulls that tool or carrier for maintenance.

## Particle and Defect Inspection

### Laser scattering

After cleaning, every prime wafer goes through an unpatterned-wafer inspection system, and the market here belongs to KLA's **Surfscan** family (SP5, SP7, and the SP7XP introduced in late 2020, which KLA rates at 12.5 nm sensitivity), with Hitachi High-Tech as a distant second. The wafer spins while a focused laser (**DUV**, deep ultraviolet, ~266 nm, in the current generation; earlier tools used 488 or 355 nm) scans a spiral across it. Collectors at several angles capture light scattered from any discontinuity: a particle, a pit, a scratch, a crystal defect, or the residual roughness. Scattering from a particle much smaller than the wavelength scales as the sixth power of its diameter, d^6: this is **Rayleigh scattering**, the regime worked out by Lord Rayleigh in which a scatterer far smaller than the wavelength re-radiates in proportion to the square of its volume (the same law that makes the sky blue, since it also rises steeply as the wavelength shortens). So a 26 nm particle scatters ~1/60 as much as a 52 nm one; separating that from the haze background is the entire engineering challenge, and it is why surface roughness below 0.1 nm RMS is a precondition for meeting the particle spec.

The tool reports **LPDs (light-point defects, or localized light scatterers)** binned by size, calibrated with **polystyrene latex (PSL)** spheres of known diameter (hence sizes are "PSL-equivalent": a real silicon pit or a flake that scatters as much light as a 26 nm sphere may be a different physical size). A leading-edge prime wafer spec might be < 10–20 LPDs at ≥ 26 nm (and even at ≥ 19 nm for some products), over the whole wafer inside a 1.5 mm edge exclusion; total **haze** is specified in ppm, the scattered intensity as parts per million of the incident light. Wafers that fail are recleaned once; repeated failures are downgraded.

> **Worked example: what the LPD spec means.** A 300 mm wafer has an area of π × 15² ≈ 707 cm². A spec of < 10 LPDs at ≥ 26 nm is therefore about one detectable defect per ~70 cm², a circle ~9 cm across, or roughly one per every few dozen dies of a typical logic chip, and that is before the fab adds its own particles over a thousand steps.

The Surfscan also reveals **COPs (crystal-originated particles)**, which are not particles at all but the octahedral voids (~100 nm) formed by vacancy agglomeration during crystal growth (Module 02). They intersect the polished surface as pits and scatter like particles. COPs in the gate region cause gate-oxide breakdown, so the fab wants none; this is the origin of epitaxial, annealed, and "perfect silicon" wafers described in the next section.

> **What can go wrong:** Haze is the enemy of sensitivity: a wafer with 0.3 nm RMS roughness scatters enough background that 26 nm LPDs disappear into it, so a hazy wafer is reported as clean when it is not, which is why haze is a spec of its own. PSL-equivalent sizes are calibration sizes, not physical ones, so a count of "10 at 26 nm" says nothing about the smaller defects the tool cannot see. A wafer that fails the count is recleaned once, and if it fails again it is downgraded rather than cleaned repeatedly, because every clean consumes silicon and roughens the surface a little.

### Contamination and electrical metrology

- **Surface metals**: **total-reflection X-ray fluorescence (TXRF)** fires X-rays at the surface at a grazing angle so shallow that they reflect off it and penetrate only the top few nanometres; each metal atom in that skin emits X-rays of its own characteristic energy, so the spectrum identifies and counts Fe, Ni, Cu, Zn, Ca to ~10^9–10^10 atoms/cm². **Vapor-phase decomposition plus ICP-MS (VPD-ICP-MS)** dissolves the surface oxide in HF vapour, rolls a single droplet of acid over the whole wafer to collect everything into one sample, and feeds it to an **inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP-MS)**, an instrument that ionises the droplet in a plasma flame and sorts the ions by mass so that each metal can be counted; it reaches sub-10^9 atoms/cm² sensitivity. Specs are typically < 1 × 10^10 atoms/cm² per element.
- **Bulk metals**: µ-PCD or **surface photovoltage (SPV)** mapping measures the **diffusion length**, how far an excited electron travels before a trap catches it; iron is identified because a brief heating breaks the iron–boron pairs that form in p-type silicon and changes the diffusion length by a known amount, so the before-and-after difference gives the iron concentration down to ~10^10 atoms/cm³.
- **Resistivity and epi**: four-point probe per lot; epi resistivity by **mercury-probe CV** (capacitance–voltage measurement, Hg-CV), which presses a drop of liquid mercury onto the epi as a temporary contact and reads the doping from how the capacitance of the junction beneath it changes with applied voltage; epi thickness by FTIR, in which infrared light reflected from the boundary where the doping (free-carrier density) changes interferes with light from the surface, accurate to ~1%.
- **Oxygen precipitation**: sample wafers get a precipitation anneal, then are etched and **bulk micro-defects (BMDs)**, the oxygen precipitates that do the gettering, counted to verify the wafer will getter as promised.

## Beyond Polished: Epitaxial, Annealed, and SOI Wafers

A polished CZ wafer is the baseline product, but the majority of 300 mm wafers going into advanced logic are epitaxial, **DRAM** makers (dynamic random-access memory, the main-memory chips of Module 15) use polished and annealed wafers, and a substantial specialty segment uses SOI. These are where the wafer makers' margins are.

### Epitaxial wafers

An **epitaxial (epi) wafer** has a single-crystal silicon layer grown by **chemical vapor deposition (CVD)**, the growth of a solid film from gases that react on a hot surface (Module 06), on top of the polished substrate, continuing the substrate's crystal lattice but with its own, independently chosen doping. The classic structure is **p/p+**: a lightly doped **p-** layer (5–20 Ω·cm; p- means lightly boron-doped, the opposite end of the scale from p+) on a heavily boron-doped p+ substrate (0.005–0.02 Ω·cm). The heavily doped substrate provides a low-resistance path that suppresses **latch-up** (a stray current path through the substrate that switches part of a CMOS chip fully on and burns it out; formally it is the turn-on of a parasitic **thyristor**, a four-layer p-n-p-n structure that, once triggered, stays on until the power is removed), and boron-rich silicon getters metals. Modern logic more often uses **p/p-** (lightly doped epi on a lightly doped substrate) since latch-up is handled by design and **wells** (the doped regions in which the transistors sit), and heavy boron substrates cause **autodoping** problems (boron evaporating from the hot p+ substrate and contaminating the growing layer); here the epi's job is purely to provide a defect-free, precisely doped top layer.

The reactor is a single-wafer, lamp-heated, **cold-wall** system (only the wafer is hot, so silicon does not deposit on the chamber walls); Applied Materials' Centura Epi leads, with ASM's Epsilon as the alternative. The wafer sits on a SiC-coated graphite **susceptor** (the plate that holds the wafer and absorbs the lamp heat) heated by halogen lamps to 1,050–1,150 °C inside a quartz chamber with **laminar** gas flow (smooth, non-turbulent flow, so every point on the wafer sees the same gas). The sequence: (1) a hydrogen bake at 1,100–1,150 °C for ~1–2 min removes the native oxide (SiO2 + H2 → SiO↑ + H2O) and leaves a clean, **reconstructed** surface (one whose outermost atoms have rearranged into a regular pattern of their own, ready to be built on); (2) an optional brief HCl etch removes a few nm of silicon, to strip any residual surface damage or contamination left after the bake so that the new crystal starts on a perfect template; (3) growth from **trichlorosilane (TCS, SiHCl3)** in hydrogen at 1,100–1,150 °C, or **dichlorosilane (DCS, SiH2Cl2)** at 1,000–1,100 °C, or silane at 900–1,000 °C for low-temperature work. The TCS reaction, SiHCl3 + H2 ⇌ Si + 3 HCl, is reversible, so the process is a near-equilibrium balance between deposition and HCl back-etch; growth is 2–4 µm/min, so a 3 µm layer takes about a minute. Dopant is added as diborane (B2H6), phosphine (PH3), or arsine (AsH3) at ppm levels in the hydrogen stream. Logic epi is 2–5 µm; power-device epi for **IGBTs** (insulated-gate bipolar transistors, the high-current switches in motor drives and trains) and **superjunction MOSFETs** (**MOSFET** is the metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor, the standard transistor type of Module 11; a superjunction version is a high-voltage variant built from alternating p- and n-doped columns) is 50–120 µm grown over hours. Thickness uniformity of ± 1–2% and resistivity uniformity of ± 3–5% are standard.

> **Intuition: step flow.** A crystal surface is never perfectly flat; it is a staircase of atomic terraces. An atom arriving from the gas skates across a terrace until it meets the edge of a step, where it has more neighbours to bond to, and attaches there. So the crystal grows not by piling up atoms randomly but by steps sweeping sideways across the surface like a tide coming in across a beach, each step laying down one atomic layer as it passes. A 100 nm pit in the surface is simply filled in by the steps that flow over it, and the layers above are continuous crystal. The analogy breaks if atoms arrive faster than they can reach a step: then islands nucleate on the terraces and the surface roughens, which is why growth rate and temperature are balanced.

Why epi eliminates COPs: the epitaxial layer is grown atom by atom from the gas phase at a temperature and rate that do not create vacancy **supersaturation** (more vacancies than the crystal can hold in equilibrium, the condition that lets them cluster into voids), so it contains no voids. Substrate COPs intersecting the surface are overgrown: lateral step-flow growth fills the ~100 nm pit within the first few hundred nanometers of growth and the crystal above is perfect. (Very large or clustered voids can nucleate an **epi stacking fault**, a planar mistake in the stacking of atomic layers that runs up through the epi, so wafer makers still grow substrates with low COP density.) The hydrogen bake also partially fills pits by surface diffusion. The result is a top layer with essentially zero grown-in defects and a **gate-oxide-integrity** yield (the fraction of test capacitors whose thin oxide survives a voltage stress) of ~100%, which is why leading-edge logic uses epi wafers almost universally and why SEMI reported that advanced epi for logic was the main growth driver in 2025 (as of SEMI's February 2026 release).

> **Worked example: epi chamber throughput.** One wafer's cycle is a hydrogen bake of ~1–2 min, growth of a 3 µm logic layer at ~3 µm/min, so ~1 min, plus loading, temperature ramps, purges and unloading of ~3–7 min, for a total of ~5–10 min. A single chamber therefore processes ~6–12 wafers per hour, and a reactor with several chambers a few dozen. Against a plant shipping hundreds of thousands of epi wafers a month, that is why epi lines are halls of reactors and why the epi step roughly doubles the wafer's price.

Cost: an epi step adds one reactor pass (~5–10 min per wafer on a $5–8 million tool, plus TCS and hydrogen), so an epi wafer sells for ~$200 or more versus ~$100–150 for a polished 300 mm prime wafer (as of 2025), with thick power-epi wafers and specialty structures costing considerably more.

> **What can go wrong:** At 1,100 °C a temperature difference of a few degrees across the wafer, from lamp non-uniformity or a warped susceptor, creates enough thermal stress to nucleate slip dislocations that run in from the edge; they are seen as lines on the Surfscan and by **X-ray topography** (an X-ray image of the wafer in which strained lattice shows up as contrast). Autodoping from a p+ substrate raises the epi's doping near the interface, seen as a resistivity profile that does not match the recipe on the mercury-probe CV. A dirty bake leaves haze. Particles on the surface before growth become epi stacking faults, counted on the Surfscan as characteristic bright defects. Thickness and resistivity drift between wafers is caught by the FTIR and Hg-CV measurements on monitor wafers, and the reactor is re-tuned.

### Annealed and "perfect silicon" wafers

There are two cheaper routes to a COP-free surface. Why they exist: DRAM makers want a COP-free surface for their gate oxides, and strong gettering below it, but at a price nearer a polished wafer than an epi wafer. **Argon- or hydrogen-annealed wafers** are polished wafers annealed at ~1,200 °C for ~1 hour in a vertical batch furnace under argon or hydrogen. The mechanism: at 1,200 °C silicon atoms hop between lattice sites fast enough (**self-diffusion**) that the thin oxide lining each COP dissolves and the void surfaces fill in, collapsing the voids in the top ~5–10 µm; at the same time oxygen near the surface diffuses out and escapes, leaving a precipitate-free **denuded zone** over a bulk that still precipitates and getters. This is the traditional DRAM choice. Furnaces for this duty come from the vertical-furnace makers named in Module 06. What goes wrong: at 1,200 °C the thermal gradients between wafers in a boat are large enough to cause slip unless the ramp rates are slow and the boat supports are carefully designed; hydrogen etches the surface slightly and can roughen it; and boron near the surface diffuses out, changing the near-surface resistivity, which is measured and allowed for.

**Perfect silicon** (SUMCO's term; the generic term is defect-free CZ) is the second route, and unlike annealing it fixes the problem in the puller rather than after the fact. Why it exists: an annealed wafer is COP-free only in its top ~5–10 µm, and the anneal is an extra 1,200 °C furnace pass with its own slip risk; a crystal that never formed voids in the first place needs neither. The mechanism is the **v/G ratio** (pull speed divided by the temperature gradient at the growth front, the Voronkov parameter of Module 02). Pull fast relative to the gradient and the crystal traps an excess of vacancies, which cluster into the ~100 nm voids that become COPs; pull slowly and it traps an excess of silicon interstitials, which cluster into dislocation loops (large, sparse defects that are just as fatal to a transistor). Between the two lies a narrow band of v/G around the critical value, ~0.13–0.2 mm² per minute per kelvin (Module 02), in which the two point defects annihilate each other as the crystal cools and neither kind of cluster forms. Perfect silicon is a crystal grown inside that band everywhere: along the whole ingot and, harder, across the whole radius.

The recipe follows from that. The gradient G is set by the hot zone (the graphite heaters, insulation, heat shields and cooling rings around the crucible and above the melt, met under ingot QC), and in a standard hot zone G is markedly higher at the crystal's rim, where heat radiates away, than at its centre, so a single pull speed cannot put the whole cross-section inside the band: the centre goes vacancy-rich and grows COPs while the rim goes interstitial-rich, and the boundary between them shows up as the OSF ring met under ingot QC. A perfect-silicon hot zone therefore uses an engineered heat shield and reflector above the melt to make G as uniform as possible across the radius, and the pull speed is then held to the band's centre and adjusted continuously as the melt is depleted and the crystal lengthens, because G drifts throughout the pull. With G at a 300 mm growth front of ~3 K/mm, the band sits at a pull speed of ~0.4–0.6 mm/min, at or below the bottom of the ~0.5–1.0 mm/min that a conventional vacancy-rich 300 mm pull uses (Module 02), and the speed must be held there rather than pushed up for throughput, so each puller grows fewer kilograms of crystal per day and the wafer carries a price premium over standard polished, though well below the epi premium; the exact premium is not published. The result, verified with the same tests as any other block, is a wafer with no Surfscan-detectable COPs and no dislocation loops across the whole diameter and through the whole thickness, with the bulk oxygen still available for gettering, which is why it became the mainstream DRAM substrate; SEH and the other majors ship equivalent defect-free CZ under their own names.

> **What can go wrong:** The band is narrow, so the failure mode is simply drifting out of it. Drift along the ingot (the pull speed not tracking G as the melt falls) puts part of the crystal back into the vacancy regime, and that part of the ingot grows COPs; drift across the radius (a hot zone that has aged, a heat shield out of position) reproduces the classic pattern of a COP-bearing core inside an OSF ring. Both are caught on the block's test slugs, by the Secco or Wright etch for dislocation loops and by the OSF test oxidation for the ring, and increasingly by a full-wafer Surfscan COP map on a sample slice, and the affected blocks are downgraded to standard polished grade. The cost of the slow pull is the other risk: a puller held to the bottom of its speed range is tied up longer per ingot, so any puller-side loss, a dislocation slip-back or a melt contamination, costs correspondingly more crystal time.

### SOI wafers and Smart Cut

A **silicon-on-insulator (SOI)** wafer has a thin single-crystal silicon device layer sitting on a **buried oxide (BOX)**, a layer of SiO2 beneath the device silicon, on a handle substrate. Transistors built in the thin layer have no **junction-to-substrate capacitance** (the capacitance between the transistor's doped regions and the bulk silicon beneath them, which slows switching) and, in **fully depleted (FD-SOI)** form (a layer so thin that the gate controls all of it, with no undepleted silicon underneath), near-ideal electrostatic control, meaning the gate alone decides whether the channel conducts; **partially depleted (PD-SOI)** uses a thicker film in which some silicon lies beyond the gate's reach. The dominant manufacturing process is **Smart Cut**, invented by Michel Bruel at CEA-Leti (the French national electronics research institute in Grenoble; patent filed 1991) and commercialized by Soitec (Bernin, France), which licenses it to Shin-Etsu Handotai and others and holds the majority of the SOI wafer market.

> **Intuition: the perforated sheet.** A hydrogen implant lays a plane of microscopic hydrogen-filled bubbles at a chosen depth beneath the surface, like the perforation on a sheet of stamps. Bond the surface to another wafer, heat the pair, and the bubbles inflate and join up until the sheet tears along the perforated line, leaving a thin film of silicon attached to the other wafer. The depth of the perforation, and therefore the thickness of the film, is set by the implant energy, and an implanter's depth control is far better than any saw's or grinder's.

The Smart Cut sequence:

1. **Oxidize the donor wafer.** A thermal oxide of the target BOX thickness is grown on a polished CZ or epi donor: ~20–25 nm for FD-SOI, 145 nm for PD-SOI, 200 nm to 1 µm for **radio-frequency (RF)** SOI (wafers for the radio circuits of phones) and photonics SOI (wafers for chips that guide light).
2. **Implant hydrogen.** H+ ions at a dose of ~5 × 10^16 cm^-2 (sometimes co-implanted with helium, whose ions are better at opening cavities while the hydrogen is better at lining them, which lets the total dose, and so the time on an expensive high-current implanter, be cut by roughly half) at an energy chosen so the **projected range** (the average depth at which the ions stop) sits at the intended split depth below the oxide, e.g. tens of **keV** (kilo-electron-volts, the energy an ion gains crossing a thousand volts) for a few hundred nm. The implant creates a buried layer of hydrogen-decorated vacancies and **platelets** (nanometer-scale, plate-shaped cavities on {100} planes).
3. **Clean and bond.** Both the donor and a polished handle wafer are RCA-cleaned, sometimes plasma-activated (a brief N2 or O2 plasma that raises the density of **hydroxyl** groups, –OH, on the oxide surface, which is what the bond forms between), and brought into contact at room temperature. Hydrophilic surfaces bond spontaneously by hydrogen bonding between surface **silanol** groups (Si–OH, the hydroxyl-capped silicon atoms of a wet oxide surface); a wave of contact propagates from one point across the wafer pair in seconds. This requires < 0.5 nm RMS roughness and essentially zero particles (a 1 µm particle produces a ~1 cm unbonded void).
4. **Split.** The pair is annealed at 400–600 °C. The hydrogen platelets grow, coalesce, pressurize, and the donor fractures along the implant plane, exactly like the cleave in a mica sheet, transferring the oxide plus a thin silicon film onto the handle. The film thickness as-split is typically 200–400 nm with ± few nm uniformity (set by the implant, which is extremely uniform).
5. **Strengthen and finish.** A high-temperature anneal (~1,100 °C) converts the hydrogen bonds into covalent Si-O-Si bonds across the interface. The split surface has ~5 nm roughness and a damaged layer, removed by a combination of touch CMP, thermal smoothing in H2/Ar, and thermal oxidation-and-strip thinning to reach the final thickness: ~12 nm ± 0.5 nm device silicon on 20–25 nm BOX for FD-SOI, or 50–200 nm for PD-SOI/RF.
6. **Reuse the donor.** The donor wafer, minus ~0.5 µm, is repolished and reused, typically ~10 times, which is what makes Smart Cut economical.

The bonding tools come from a few specialist makers (EV Group and SUSS MicroTec are the usual names), and the hydrogen implanters from the ion-implanter makers of Module 10, in high-current versions built for this dose.

> **Why it matters downstream:** In a fully depleted transistor the **threshold voltage** (the gate voltage at which the transistor turns on) depends directly on the thickness of the silicon film under the gate. A 12 ± 0.5 nm device layer means every transistor on every FD-SOI wafer sees the same film to within 4%, which is what allows the designer to set thresholds by **back-biasing**, applying a voltage to the handle wafer through the ultrathin BOX to shift the threshold up or down at will. A film that varied by ± 2 nm would scatter thresholds so widely that the design could not be closed.

Products: **FD-SOI** for GlobalFoundries' 22FDX and STMicroelectronics/Samsung 28FD-SOI (low-power **Internet-of-Things (IoT)** devices such as sensors and wearables, automotive radar, and **mmWave**, the 24–100 GHz radio bands used for 5G and radar), where back-biasing through the ultrathin BOX tunes threshold voltage; **RF-SOI** (high-resistivity handle plus a **trap-rich** polysilicon layer under the BOX, a deliberately defective layer that traps the stray charges that would otherwise let radio signals leak into the substrate) for the antenna switches in essentially every smartphone, Soitec's largest business by volume; and **Photonics-SOI** (220 nm Si on 2 µm BOX), the platform for the optical transceivers in AI clusters. As of 2025, 300 mm SOI wafers sell for several hundred dollars, over $1,000 for specialty stacks.

## Test, Monitor, and Reclaim Wafers

A fab consumes far more silicon than it ships as product. Every tool is qualified and monitored with wafers that never carry a circuit: **particle monitors** (does the tool add fewer than N particles?), **film-thickness monitors** for deposition and etch rates, **dummy wafers** filling empty slots in a 100–150 wafer furnace **boat** (the quartz rack that carries wafers through a batch furnace) so every product wafer sees the same environment, and **send-ahead wafers** (one wafer run first through a step to verify the recipe before the lot follows). A large fab uses 10–25% as many test wafers as product wafers, hundreds of thousands per month for a **GIGAFAB**-class site (TSMC's term for a fab of 100,000 or more **wafer starts**, wafers entering the line, per month), bought as **test grade** (wafers that missed prime spec on a non-critical parameter, ~40–60% of prime price) or reclaimed.

**Reclaim** strips a used test wafer's films (HF/HNO3 or plasma), repolishes it (removing ~10–20 µm), cleans, inspects, and sells it back at a third to a half of a new test wafer's price. A wafer can be reclaimed several times until it falls below a minimum thickness of ~725–750 µm. As of 2025, RS Technologies (Japan) leads with ~30%+ share; Pure Wafer (US), Hamada Heavy Industries, Kinik and Phoenix Silicon International (Taiwan) share the rest of a ~$700 million per year market that tracks fab utilization.

> **Worked example: how many lives a test wafer has, and what reclaim saves.** A test wafer starts at 775 µm and each reclaim cycle takes ~10–20 µm; with the fab's floor at ~725–750 µm, that is (775 − 740) / 15 ≈ 2–3 cycles for a typical spec and up to ~5 for a lenient one, which is what "several times" means. Now the money: a 100,000-wafer-start GIGAFAB using test wafers at 15% of starts consumes ~15,000 a month. Bought new at test grade (~40–60% of a ~$100–150 prime wafer, so ~$50–80), that is ~$0.75–1.2 million a month; if two-thirds of them are reclaimed wafers at a third to a half of the new price (~$20–40), the monthly bill drops by roughly ~$0.3–0.5 million, several million dollars a year for one fab, which is the entire reason a reclaim industry exists.

> **What can go wrong:** Reclaim's failure modes are contamination and thinness. A wafer that carried copper wiring or a metal-deposition film in its previous life will contaminate a furnace or an epi reactor if the strip is incomplete, so fabs segregate their monitor wafers by the process area they came from and reclaimers run TXRF on every lot; a reclaimed wafer that has thinned below the fab's floor trips thickness sensors, sits low in a furnace boat slot and can be mis-handled by an end-effector set up for 775 µm; and each repolish takes a little more flatness and adds a little more edge roll-off, so a reclaimed wafer is never used where a flatness-sensitive tool is being monitored. The fab's incoming inspection (thickness, Surfscan, TXRF on a sample) catches all three, and a reclaim lot that fails is returned. The subtler failure is bookkeeping: a test wafer read into a product lot by a mis-scanned ID goes all the way through the line before the mismatch is noticed at wafer test.

## Packaging and Shipping

Finished wafers are loaded 25 to a **FOSB (front-opening shipping box)**, a polycarbonate container built for shipping rather than tool docking, with cushions that clamp each wafer's edge against vibration; the **FOUP (front-opening unified pod)** is its fab-side twin, the sealed carrier that docks onto every tool's load port and travels the fab's overhead rails (Module 05), and the two differ mainly in that the FOSB is built to survive a truck and an aircraft rather than a load port. Entegris, Shin-Etsu Polymer, and Miraial make them. The FOSB is sealed in a **Class 1** cleanroom (air with fewer than one particle of 0.5 µm or larger per cubic foot), purged with nitrogen, double-bagged in moisture-barrier bags, and air-freighted with shock indicators. At the fab a **wafer sorter** (a robot station that reads each wafer's ID and moves wafers between carriers; Brooks and Rorze make them) reads each T7 mark and transfers the wafers into the fab's own FOUPs; the FOSBs go back for reuse. Shelf life is of the order of 6–12 months, after which adsorbed organics require a reclean; the exact limit is a matter of each customer's specification.

Why the box matters as much as it does: a polished wafer's surface is a nearly perfect adsorber. The chemical oxide the final clean left is hydrophilic and carries a thin water layer, and anything volatile in the surrounding air, the **plasticizers** (softening additives) that outgas from the box's own polymer, residual solvents, fingerprint oils from a glove, sticks to it within hours. That is why the box is purged with nitrogen, why the polymer is chosen and baked to outgas as little as possible, and why the bag is a moisture barrier: the enemy is not dust in transit but the slow accumulation of a molecular film that would later become haze, a bonding failure, or a shifted threshold voltage after the gate oxide is grown on it.

> **What can go wrong:** The classic transport failures are mechanical and chemical. A dropped or vibrated box chips edges and cracks wafers against the cushions, which the shock indicator on the outer carton records and the fab's incoming edge inspection confirms; the lot is claimed against the carrier. A bag or box seal that leaks lets in humid air, and the wafers arrive with elevated haze and organic contamination, measured at the fab by Surfscan on a sample and by **thermal-desorption gas chromatography (TD-GC-MS)**, which heats a wafer or a box to drive off adsorbed molecules and identifies them by mass; the lot is recleaned at the wafer maker's expense. A box that has outlived its cleaning cycle sheds particles from worn cushions onto every wafer it carries, which is why FOSBs are washed and inspected between uses and retired after a set number of trips. And a wafer loaded in the wrong slot orientation (face down, or notch turned) is caught by the sorter, but not before it has been handled once too often.

## Economics and Market Structure

### Prices and volumes

SEMI's Silicon Manufacturers Group reports the industry's volumes in **millions of square inches (MSI)** of wafer area shipped. Shipments peaked at ~14,700 MSI in 2022, fell 14% in 2023 and another ~3% in 2024 as fabs and their customers burned inventory, and recovered 5.8% to 12,973 MSI in 2025; industry revenue was ~$11.4 billion in 2025, roughly flat to slightly down because the mix shifted toward 300 mm while 200 mm and smaller prices softened (as of SEMI's February 2026 release). A 300 mm wafer is 109.6 square inches, and 300 mm wafers account for roughly three-quarters of area shipped, so the world consumes on the order of 7–8 million 300 mm wafers per month, i.e. 20+ million per quarter and ~90 million per year (estimates that count only prime polished and epi wafers, excluding test and reclaim, run somewhat lower, ~15+ million per quarter). By area the growth, as of 2025, is in advanced epi for AI logic and polished/annealed wafers for **HBM** (high-bandwidth memory, the stacked DRAM mounted beside AI GPUs; Module 15) DRAM; 200 mm and smaller volumes are flat to declining.

Prices are set mostly under multi-year **long-term agreements (LTAs)**, contracts that fix volumes and prices for several years, negotiated in the 2021–2022 shortage, which locked in volumes and prices through 2025–2026 and are the reason wafer revenue held up while volumes fell. As of Q1 2026, a standard 300 mm polished prime wafer sells for roughly $100–150; epi wafers ~$200 and up; leading-edge grades (the tightest SFQR, nanotopography, and LPD specs) carry a premium of ~20% over standard prime; SOI several hundred dollars; 200 mm polished ~$40–60. For a fab, silicon is ~1–2% of the cost of a leading-edge processed wafer (reported at roughly $18,000–30,000 at **N3/N2**, TSMC's 3 nm and 2 nm process generations), so wafer makers have little pricing leverage in good times and none in bad.

### The 2023–2025 inventory correction

The 2021–2022 shortage led fabs to build several months of wafer inventory, and when the consumer and mature-node slump arrived in 2023 they deferred LTA deliveries, so wafer shipments fell far more than chip output. SUMCO fell into the red in fiscal 2025 and announced (February 2025) that 200 mm wafer production at its Miyazaki plant would end by the close of 2026, Siltronic slowed the ramp of its new Singapore fab, and GlobalWafers opened its Sherman, Texas fab in May 2025 without committing to a phase-2 timeline. As of Q1 2026, leading-edge logic and HBM demand had pushed SEMI-reported shipments up 13% year-on-year in Q1 2026 (3,275 MSI), while the mature-node 300 mm and 200 mm overhang was, as a forecast, expected to persist into 2027.

## Summary

- Wafering is a controlled descent of damage: saw (5–30 µm cracks) → grind (2–5 µm) → etch (zero) → polish (sub-nanometre); each step removes more than the last one left.
- The ingot is cropped, cut into ~300–400 mm blocks, characterised from end slugs (resistivity, oxygen, carbon, defects, lifetime), ground to 300.00 mm, XRD-oriented and notched on <110>.
- A multi-wire saw runs one ~120 µm diamond wire as a web of hundreds of strands; pitch = slice + kerf ≈ 1.06 mm; ~1,800 wafers from a 2 m ingot; ~15% of the crystal becomes kerf sludge.
- Diamond wire beat slurry because fixed grit cuts faster, thinner and shallower; the cut still leaves cracks, thickness variation from wire bow, and saw marks.
- Double-disk grinding sets TTV below 1 µm with no chuck touching the faces; alkaline etching removes every remaining crack uniformly because it is reaction-rate-limited, whereas acid etch is smoother but diffusion-limited and degrades flatness.
- CMP is chemical first: an alkaline slurry hydrates the surface and silica (softer than silicon) wipes the soft film away, so a polished wafer has no damage layer; DSP for flatness, then a light front-side touch polish to < 0.1 nm RMS.
- 775 µm is a compromise: sag scales as D^4/t^2, a free 300 mm wafer sags ~90 µm, and flatness is only meaningful on a chucked wafer.
- The RCA clean: SC-1 lifts particles by growing and etching a chemical oxide beneath them and keeps them off by electrostatic repulsion; SC-2 dissolves the metals SC-1 deposits; HF strips oxide; Marangoni drying leaves no droplet to become a watermark.
- Flatness vocabulary: TTV, bow, warp, GBIR, SFQR (~20 nm on 26 × 8 mm sites for EUV), nanotopography, edge exclusion (1.5 mm), all measured by dual Fizeau interferometry.
- Particles are counted by laser scattering (intensity ∝ d^6), which is why sub-0.1 nm roughness is a precondition for seeing 26 nm particles; COPs from the crystal show up as pits.
- Epi wafers grow a fresh, void-free layer by step flow; annealed and "perfect silicon" wafers are the cheaper DRAM alternatives; Smart Cut SOI transfers a film by hydrogen implant, bonding and splitting.
- A range of qualified suppliers makes 300 mm wafers; ~7–8 million 300 mm wafers ship per month, a polished prime wafer is ~$100–150 and epi ~$200+, silicon is ~1–2% of a processed leading-edge wafer's cost, and fabs consume 10–25% extra as test and reclaim wafers.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Sawing cuts the wafer to its final thickness."** → The as-cut slice is ~890–950 µm; roughly 115 µm is ground, etched, and polished away to reach 775 µm, and the kerf takes another ~160 µm of the ingot per wafer.
- **"Polishing is just very fine grinding."** → CMP removes silicon chemically (alkaline hydration of the surface) and the silica abrasive is softer than silicon; that is why a polished wafer has no damage layer, which no mechanical process can achieve.
- **"A polished wafer is flat."** → It is flat only when chucked. A free 300 mm wafer sags ~90 µm under its own weight and may have 10–30 µm of warp; the 20 nm SFQR spec refers to the residual within a lithography site after the back is pulled flat and the scanner levels each site.
- **"The notch is a smaller version of the flat, chosen to save space."** → Saving area is one reason, but the notch was also adopted because a flat breaks the axisymmetry that spin coating, spin cleaning, and wafer mechanics depend on at 300 mm.
- **"SC-1 dissolves particles."** → It undercuts them by growing and etching a chemical oxide beneath them and keeps them from redepositing through electrostatic repulsion at high pH; SC-2 is needed separately because SC-1 actually deposits certain metals.
- **"Epi is only for suppressing latch-up."** → For leading-edge logic the main reason is defect-free silicon: the CVD-grown layer contains no COPs, so gate-oxide integrity approaches 100%. Annealed and "perfect silicon" wafers are the lower-cost alternatives used in DRAM.
- **"The back side of the wafer does not matter."** → A 300 mm wafer's back is polished to a mirror because every flatness figure is defined with the back pulled flat on a chuck, a rough or particle-laden back prints through as front-side focus error, and the SEMI T7 identity mark the fab reads is on the back.
- **"A spec of fewer than 10 particles at 26 nm means the wafer carries fewer than 10 particles."** → It means fewer than 10 scatterers that scatter as much as a 26 nm polystyrene sphere. Sizes are PSL-equivalent, not physical, and anything smaller than the detection threshold is not counted at all.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 02 delivered a dislocation-free single-crystal ingot of known dopant type, resistivity, and oxygen content, grown by Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic, or SK Siltron from Module 01's polysilicon. This module turned that ingot into the wafer maker's actual product: about 1,800 polished, cleaned, inspected, individually serialized 775 µm discs per ingot, or their epitaxial, annealed, or SOI derivatives, packed 25 to a FOSB and air-freighted to TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK hynix, Micron, and the rest. Along the way it consumed diamond wire, colloidal silica, polyurethane pads, KOH, HF, HCl, NH4OH, H2O2, TCS, hydrogen, and ultrapure water, most of which are the subject of Module 04 on fab consumables and non-silicon substrates (silicon carbide, gallium nitride, and the other compound wafers made with related but different techniques). Module 05 then follows the FOSB through the fab's loading dock into a FOUP and onto the process line, where the fab's own cleaning, oxidation, and deposition steps (Module 06) begin by stripping the very chemical oxide this module's final SC-1 left behind.
