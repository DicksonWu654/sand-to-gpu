# Module 08: Photolithography II: EUV in Full Detail

Module 07 ended with a problem. The best deep-ultraviolet scanner, the 193 nm ArF immersion tool, cannot draw two dense lines closer together than ~76–80 nm centre to centre in one exposure, a **half-pitch** (half the centre-to-centre spacing of a dense line array) of ~38–40 nm. That floor is set by the wavelength and by the **numerical aperture (NA)**, the sine of the half-angle of the cone of light the lens delivers to the wafer, and 193 nm at NA 1.35 is as far as that road goes. Yet the tightest wiring on a 7 nm-class chip sits at ~40 nm pitch, half the DUV limit, and the transistor fins (Module 11) are tighter still. Those layers were built by **multi-patterning**: the layer is split into two, three or four interleaved lithography passes, each printing a pattern coarse enough for 193 nm, with spacer and cut steps in between. Every extra pass costs a mask, a trip through the **resist track** (the machine that coats, bakes and develops the resist, Module 07), an etch, a clean, a **metrology** (measurement) step, about a day of cycle time, and one more chance to spoil the **overlay**, how well a layer lines up with the layer beneath it. At 7 nm-class nodes the tightest layers needed three or four passes plus several cut masks, and the overlay budget was being spent faster than it was being earned. **Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography** exists to collapse those three or four passes back into one by cutting the wavelength by a factor of 14, from 193 nm to 13.5 nm.

The price of that factor of 14 is that almost nothing about a DUV scanner survives. There is no lens, because no material transmits 13.5 nm light. There is no laser at 13.5 nm; the light comes from a tin plasma at half a million kelvin, created 50,000 times per second by a 10.6 µm CO₂ laser hitting falling droplets of molten metal. There is no air, because air absorbs the light within centimetres; the whole machine is a vacuum vessel filled with a few pascals of hydrogen, some 30,000 times thinner than the air around you. There is no transmissive photomask, so the reticle, the plate that carries the pattern, is a mirror, and the light has to come in at an angle, which distorts the image in ways a transmissive mask never did. And a 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV, thirty to forty times the energy of a visible-light photon, so a normal exposure dose consists of so few photons that their counting statistics show up as defects on the wafer. This module goes through each of those problems and how ASML, Zeiss, Trumpf, Cymer, and the resist and mask suppliers solved them, in enough detail to explain why one of these machines costs $200 million, or $380 million for the High-NA version.

By the end you will be able to explain six things to a process engineer without notes: why the light is 13.5 nm and not some other short wavelength (the mirror decides, not the source); how a 27 µm droplet of tin becomes a 500 W lamp fifty thousand times a second; why every mirror in the path costs 30% of the light and why that single fact shapes the whole machine; why the mask is itself a mirror and what lighting it at an angle does to the printed image; why ~20 photons per square nanometre turn chip **yield** (the fraction of chips on a wafer that work) into a problem in statistics; and why the next machine, at NA 0.55, prints only half of a field, the rectangle one scan exposes, and is anyway the only way forward.

## Before you start

- **Photon energy.** Light comes in packets called photons, and the energy of each packet is E = 1240 eV·nm / λ, where λ is the wavelength. An **electron-volt (eV)** is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt; visible light carries 2–3 eV per photon, 193 nm light 6.4 eV, and 13.5 nm light 92 eV (Module 07).
- **Diffraction and the Rayleigh criterion.** The finest dense line pattern a projection system can print has half-pitch = k1·λ/NA, where NA is the numerical aperture defined in the opening and **k1** (never below 0.25) measures how cleverly that light is used. The floor at 0.25 exists because at least two of the beams the mask diffracts must fit through the optics to form any image at all. The **depth of focus**, the range of focus over which the image stays sharp, is ≈ λ/NA² (Module 07).
- **Chemically amplified resist in three sentences.** The resist is a polymer plus a **photoacid generator (PAG)** plus a **quencher** base. Exposure makes acid; a **post-exposure bake (PEB)** lets each acid molecule hop around and strip hundreds of **protecting groups** (bulky side groups hung on the polymer chain that keep it insoluble until an acid removes them; this module uses that one name for them throughout) off the polymer. A developer then removes the exposed areas (positive tone) or the unexposed areas (negative tone) (Module 07).
- **Multi-patterning.** **LELE** (litho-etch-litho-etch) prints a layer in two interleaved passes; **SADP/SAQP** (self-aligned double and quadruple patterning) deposit spacers on the sidewalls of a printed "mandrel" pattern to double or quadruple the number of lines; **cut** or **block** masks then chop the resulting loops into wires. Each extra pass costs money, a day, and overlay (Module 07).
- **Overlay and edge placement error.** **Overlay** is how well a layer lines up with the one beneath it, ~1.5–2 nm at EUV nodes. **Edge placement error (EPE)** adds the feature-size error and the edge roughness to the overlay error: it is the total uncertainty in where a printed edge actually lands (Modules 07, 13).
- **Reticle and pellicle.** The **reticle** (photomask) carries the pattern of one **field** (the rectangular area, 26 × 33 mm on the wafer, that a single scan exposes; a 300 mm wafer holds ~100 of them) on a 152 mm plate, drawn 4× larger than it will print, written by a scanning electron beam. A **pellicle** is a thin membrane held above the pattern so that particles landing on it sit out of focus and do not print (Modules 04, 07).
- **Pressure units.** A **pascal (Pa)** is the SI unit of pressure; 1 atmosphere ≈ 101,000 Pa ≈ 1,013 mbar ≈ 1 bar. "Vacuum" in this module means 1–100 Pa, a thousandth to a hundred-thousandth of atmospheric pressure (Modules 05, 06).
- **Plasma and temperature in eV.** A **plasma** is a gas so hot that atoms lose electrons and the electrons and ions move freely. Physicists quote plasma temperatures in eV: 1 eV of temperature ≈ 11,600 K. A charge state written Sn¹⁰⁺ means a tin atom missing 10 of its 50 electrons.
- **Thin films by sputtering and ion-beam deposition.** In **sputtering**, argon ions knock atoms off a target and those atoms land on the substrate one layer at a time, with thickness controlled by time; ion-beam deposition is the same idea with a separate ion gun (Module 06).
- **Counting statistics and node names.** When independent random arrivals average N per patch, the actual count varies from patch to patch by about √N. And node names (N7, N5, N3, N2, 18A, 14A) are generation labels, not physical lengths; nothing on an N3 chip is 3 nm (Module 00).

## Why 13.5 nm

### Everything absorbs EUV

Photon energy is E = hc/λ, where h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light and λ is the wavelength; in convenient units this is E = 1240 eV·nm / λ. The equation says that energy per photon is inversely proportional to wavelength: halve the wavelength and each photon carries twice the energy. At 193 nm that is 1240/193 = 6.4 eV, deep in the ultraviolet but still below the energy needed to free an electron from most atoms, which is why fused silica and calcium fluoride transmit it. At 13.5 nm the photon carries 1240/13.5 = 91.8 eV, call it 92.

Two sentences of school physics explain why that matters. A photon is absorbed only if it can hand its whole energy to an electron, either freeing it (the **photoelectric effect**) or lifting it to an allowed higher energy level; a photon that can do neither, as a 6.4 eV photon cannot in fused silica, passes through the material and is merely slowed, and that slowing is what refraction is. At 92 eV every element has electrons, in its outer shell and usually in inner shells too, that the photon can eject, so a 92 eV photon absorbed by any solid ejects a photoelectron; the material absorbs, it does not refract. The **absorption length** (the distance over which the intensity falls to 1/e, about 37%, of its starting value) in silicon at 13.5 nm is under 1 µm; in most metals and oxides it is tens to a few hundred nanometres. A film ten absorption lengths thick passes e⁻¹⁰, about 0.005%, essentially nothing. Even gases absorb strongly: at atmospheric pressure the absorption length in air is a few hundred micrometres. There is therefore no lens material, no window material, and no possibility of running in air. EUV optics must be all-reflective, and the beam path must be in vacuum.

### The Mo/Si Bragg mirror sets the wavelength

But ordinary mirrors do not work either. A metal surface at normal incidence reflects a few percent at best at 13.5 nm, because the refractive index of every material is close to 1. (At these photon energies the index is written as a complex number, n = 1 − δ + iβ, where δ is the tiny amount by which the material bends light and β is how strongly it absorbs; both are small. The bending part, 1 − δ, is what a mirror cares about, and δ is only of order 0.01 to 0.1, so n sits just below 1 rather than at the 1.5 of school-physics glass.) A mirror works by an index step, and there is almost no step to work with. The trick, developed at Bell Labs and elsewhere in the 1980s, is the **multilayer mirror**: a stack of alternating thin films of two materials chosen so that the small reflections at each interface add in phase.

> **Intuition:** A single Mo/Si interface reflects only ~0.1–0.2% of the light (an order-of-magnitude estimate from the index step of ~0.08). But stack ~100 interfaces and space them so that the round trip between neighbours is exactly one wavelength, and every reflected wave crests at the same moment; their amplitudes add rather than their intensities, like pushing a swing once per swing period and watching it climb. A hundred tiny in-phase pushes would in principle give a perfect mirror; in practice the light that has not yet been reflected is being absorbed as it goes deeper, so the sum saturates near 70%. The mirror is also fussy: change the wavelength by a few percent and the pushes fall out of step, so the reflectance collapses. That fussiness is both the mirror's limitation and the reason the whole industry agreed on one wavelength.

This is a **Bragg reflector**, the same physics as X-ray diffraction from a crystal, and the condition for the reflections to add at near-normal incidence is 2d·cos θ = λ, where d is the bilayer period (the thickness of one Mo layer plus one Si layer) and θ is the angle between the incoming light and the mirror's normal. It says the extra path a wave travels to reach the next interface and come back, 2d·cos θ, must be one wavelength. At θ near zero, d ≈ λ/2 ≈ 6.75 nm; the real period is a little larger because the light inside the stack has a slightly longer wavelength.

> **Worked example: the bilayer period.** Inside the stack the wavelength is λ/n_avg, where n_avg is the average refractive index of the bilayer, so the period is d = λ/(2·n_avg). Take δ ≈ 0.077 for molybdenum and δ ≈ 0.001 for silicon at 13.5 nm (both rounded from tabulated optical constants), and a Mo fraction of 0.4: δ_avg = 0.4 × 0.077 + 0.6 × 0.001 ≈ 0.031, so n_avg ≈ 0.969. Then d = 13.5/(2 × 0.969) ≈ 6.97 nm, which is why real Mo/Si mirrors have a period of 6.9–7.0 nm rather than 6.75 nm. Now the fussiness: a 1% error in the period shifts the reflectance peak by 1% of the wavelength, 0.135 nm. A single mirror's peak is only ~0.5 nm wide, so 1% is a quarter of the passband, and the eleven mirrors in a scanner all have to sit on the same 0.5 nm. If they were each detuned by 1% in random directions, the system would lose most of its light.

The material pair is chosen to maximize the reflectance difference at each interface while minimizing absorption inside the stack. δ is proportional to electron density, so pairing a dense metal with light silicon gives the largest index step, and molybdenum's high electron density makes it the metal of choice. Silicon is the partner because of an **absorption edge**: the photon energy at which a new, more tightly bound electron shell becomes ejectable. Just below that energy, at slightly longer wavelength, the photon cannot use that channel and the material is unusually transparent. Electron shells are labelled from the inside out K, L, M, and so on (the K shell is the innermost, n = 1; the L shell is the second, n = 2, which in silicon holds the 2s and 2p electrons), and the **L-edge** is the photon energy needed to eject an electron from that second shell. Silicon's L-edge sits at 99.8 eV (12.4 nm); at 13.5 nm the photon has 92 eV, just short of the 99.8 eV needed to free a silicon 2p electron, so silicon at 13.5 nm is about as transparent as any solid gets at EUV. A **Mo/Si multilayer** with ~40 to 50 bilayers, each ~6.9 to 7.0 nm thick (roughly 2.8 nm Mo and 4.1 nm Si, the Mo fraction near 0.4), has a theoretical peak reflectance of ~74–75% and a practically achieved ~67–70% at 13.5 nm. The reflectance curve of a single mirror is a peak roughly 0.5 nm (~4%) wide at half maximum (the **full width at half maximum (FWHM)**, the width of a peak measured where it has fallen to half its top value); after ten or more reflections in series the system passband narrows to about 2% (13.5 nm ± 1%, ~0.27 nm), which is the **in-band** definition used for source power, and it falls off steeply above ~14 nm and below ~12.5 nm. The wavelength of EUV lithography is set by where that mirror works, not by anything about the light source.

The stacks are deposited by magnetron **sputtering** (argon ions knock atoms off a molybdenum or silicon target and the atoms land on the substrate one atomic layer at a time, Module 06) or, for the highest quality, by ion-beam deposition, with thickness control at the 0.01 nm level across a 300 mm or larger substrate, for the reason the worked example gave. Reticle blanks are coated by ion-beam deposition (Veeco is the usual tool supplier, as far as public information goes); the scanner's own mirrors and the source's **collector** (the large curved mirror that sits behind the tin plasma and gathers its light, described under The Laser-Produced Plasma Source) are coated in Zeiss's in-house coating facility. The finished period is verified by at-wavelength **reflectometry** (shining 13.5 nm light of a precisely known wavelength on the sample and measuring what fraction comes back, then repeating across the wavelength band to trace out the peak). The light for that measurement has historically come from a **synchrotron**, a ring accelerator in which electrons forced around a curve emit intense, tunable light from infrared to X-rays, EUV included; a **beamline** is one of the ring's light outlets, a pipe with a wavelength selector and a measurement chamber at its end, and the German metrology institute PTB's beamline at the BESSY II ring in Berlin is the industry's reference for EUV reflectometry. Laboratory EUV reflectometers with small plasma sources now do routine checks, and in the tool the period is monitored indirectly by wavefront and throughput measurements. Mo and Si interdiffuse into molybdenum silicide at the interfaces, which blurs the interface and costs reflectance; commercial stacks insert sub-nanometre diffusion barriers (boron carbide or carbon) and cap the top with ~2 to 2.5 nm of ruthenium to resist oxidation and hydrogen-induced blistering.

> **What can go wrong:** Four things degrade a multilayer in service. Interdiffusion at the Mo/Si interfaces (accelerated by heat) turns sharp steps into molybdenum silicide ramps, softening the index step. Oxidation of the ruthenium cap or of exposed silicon adds an absorbing skin. Hydrogen, which the tool deliberately runs in, can diffuse under the cap and lift it in blisters. And a period drift or a coating run that came out 1% thick detunes the mirror from its neighbours. Engineers see all four as a loss of reflectance: at the factory by at-wavelength reflectometry (the synchrotron beamlines and laboratory reflectometers just described), and in the scanner as a slow decline in throughput at fixed source power and as wavefront drift measured on the wafer stage. The fixes are a better barrier layer or cap at the design stage, hydrogen cleaning and mirror actuation in the tool, and, in the end, a collector swap or a mirror recoat.

Each reflection loses at least 30% of the light. That single fact drives much of the EUV story: with ten or eleven reflections between the source and the wafer, only about 1 to 2% of the in-band light created at the source's focus ever reaches the resist. Every watt of source power is precious, and every mirror in the design is argued over.

### Why tin

Given that the mirror wants 13.5 nm ± 1%, the source has to emit as much energy as possible into that band. Several plasmas were tried. Xenon emits around 10.8 to 11 nm and was the source in the early 2000s prototypes; its conversion efficiency into the Mo/Si band was under 1%. Lithium has a strong single line at 13.5 nm (doubly ionized lithium is a one-electron ion whose strongest line happens to fall there) but is chemically vicious and hard to handle. Tin won because of what its 50 electrons do when a dozen of them are stripped away. Strip 8 to 14 of them and the remaining outer electrons sit in shells labelled 4p, 4d and 4f; jumps between these shells emit photons of roughly 90 eV. Because each ion still has dozens of electrons, there are hundreds of thousands of slightly different 4d–4f and 4p–4d jumps, and their lines merge into one broad hump: an **unresolved transition array (UTA)** peaking at ~13.5 nm. Which charge states are present depends on temperature: the **ionization balance** is the mix of charge states in a plasma at a given temperature. A tin plasma at an electron temperature of ~30 to 40 eV (350,000 to 500,000 K, using 1 eV ≈ 11,600 K) has its ionization balance right in the Sn⁸⁺ to Sn¹⁴⁺ range and emits a broad hump centred near 13.5 nm that overlaps the mirror band well.

The figure of merit is **conversion efficiency (CE)**: the fraction of the laser energy that comes out as in-band EUV (13.5 nm ± 1%) radiated into the forward half-sphere facing the collector (the large curved mirror behind the plasma, introduced above and described in the next section), per joule of laser energy delivered. Production tin sources reach ~5 to 6%, the highest of any candidate; everything else the plasma radiates is waste.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The mirror's 2% passband means anything the plasma radiates outside it is not light, it is heat and noise. Out-of-band EUV and visible light land on the collector as ~2 kW of heat that has to be conducted out of a mirror that must not warp. Any deep-ultraviolet that survives the mirrors is worse: it exposes the resist without carrying image information, adding a uniform fog that lowers contrast. That is why spectral purity is a source specification, monitored in the tool, and not just a physics curiosity.

## The Laser-Produced Plasma Source

The EUV source is a machine in its own right, built by **Cymer** in San Diego (bought by ASML for about $2.5 billion, announced in 2012 and closed in 2013, because the source was the pacing item of the whole program) with the drive laser from **Trumpf** in Ditzingen, Germany. It occupies a vessel below and beside the scanner body, with the laser and its power supplies and cooling taking up more floor space in the **sub-fab** (the floor beneath the cleanroom where pumps, lasers, chillers and gas cabinets live, Module 05) than the scanner does in the cleanroom. Its job is to produce, at an **intermediate focus (IF)** roughly the size of a pinhead, a steady stream of 13.5 nm light at hundreds of watts, with a spectral purity and pointing stability that the illuminator downstream can live with, for months without opening the vessel.

A note on names, because model numbers appear from here on. ASML calls its 0.33 NA EUV scanners **NXE** (NXE:3100, 3300, 3400, 3600, 3800; the number rises with each generation and a letter suffix marks a sub-version), its 0.55 NA High-NA EUV scanners **EXE** (EXE:5000, 5200), and its 193 nm immersion DUV scanners **NXT** (NXT:1980i, 2000i, and so on). All three families share ASML's **TWINSCAN** two-wafer-stage platform, described under The Wafer Side.

Everything the source does happens in a cycle 20 µs long, repeated 50,000 times a second. One cycle, in order:

1. **A droplet falls.** The droplet generator pinches off a 27 µm sphere of molten tin, which falls through the vessel at 70–80 m/s.
2. **The droplet is detected.** Continuous lasers light the droplet stream and fast detectors measure each droplet's position and arrival time.
3. **The pre-pulse hits.** A weak laser pulse strikes the sphere and flattens it into a disk or mist a few hundred micrometres across.
4. **The target expands.** For 1–3 µs the flattened target drifts downstream and spreads out while the main pulse is steered to where it will be.
5. **The main pulse hits.** A few hundred millijoules of 10.6 µm light are absorbed, heating the tin to ~30–40 eV.
6. **The plasma radiates.** For ~100 ns the Sn⁸⁺–Sn¹⁴⁺ ions emit their 13.5 nm hump in all directions; the flash is over within a few hundred nanoseconds.
7. **The collector focuses.** The large mirror behind the plasma catches ~40% of the directions and refocuses the light to the intermediate focus, over a metre away.
8. **The light leaves through the IF.** An energy sensor at the IF measures the pulse and the scanner's dose controller logs it.
9. **The debris is stopped.** Hydrogen gas slows the tin ions and atoms before they reach the collector and turns deposited tin into a gas the pumps remove.
10. **The next droplet arrives.** 20 µs after the last.

Each subsection below expands one or two of these steps.

### The droplet generator

The fuel is tin, delivered as a stream of identical microscopic droplets. Inside the source vessel, a reservoir holds a few hundred grams of tin heated to ~250 °C, just above tin's 232 °C melting point, and pressurized with argon to tens of bar (1 **bar** is about atmospheric pressure, so tens of bar is scuba-tank-regulator territory). The liquid is forced through a nozzle only a few micrometres in diameter, producing a filament of tin roughly 20 µm thick moving at 70 to 80 m/s. A liquid jet of that diameter is unstable: surface tension amplifies any small perturbation at wavelengths longer than the jet circumference (the **Rayleigh-Plateau instability**), and the jet spontaneously breaks up into droplets. Left alone, it breaks up irregularly. A **piezoelectric** actuator on the nozzle (a crystal that flexes by nanometres when a voltage is applied) is driven at the droplet frequency so that it squeezes the nozzle once per cycle and the breakup is deterministic: one droplet per squeeze, every cycle. Satellite droplets that form between the main ones are made to merge with them within a short distance downstream by choosing the modulation waveform carefully.

The result is a train of tin spheres ~25 to 30 µm in diameter, spaced uniformly, falling through the vacuum at 70 to 80 m/s, at a repetition rate of 50 kHz in the NXE:3400/3600 generation and higher (60 kHz and, in the 2026 1 kW demonstration, 100 kHz) in the newest sources. Fifty thousand droplets per second, each one a target that must be hit by a laser pulse focused to a spot not much larger than the droplet itself, on a path of a few hundred millimetres, after which the surviving material must be removed from the chamber. Positional jitter of the droplet train is a few micrometres; if it drifts more than that, the laser misses and EUV output collapses. The droplet generator is a consumable: the nozzle erodes and clogs, the tin runs out, and the whole unit is swapped every few weeks to a few months of operation.

> **Worked example: tin consumption and droplets per wafer.** Take a 27 µm droplet. Its volume is V = ⁴⁄₃πr³ with r = 13.5 µm, which is 1.03 × 10⁻¹⁴ m³. Liquid tin has a density of ~7,000 kg/m³, so each droplet has a mass of ~72 nanograms. At 50,000 droplets per second the generator consumes ~3.6 mg/s, i.e. ~13 g per hour or ~0.3 kg per 24 hours of continuous operation (the droplet train runs continuously; droplets that are not hit by the laser are caught in a tin catcher). A month of full-time operation therefore uses on the order of 10 kg, which is why the reservoir holds kilograms of tin and is refilled (in newer sources in line, without opening the vessel) or swapped every few weeks. Tin cost is irrelevant at ~$30 per kilogram; the problem is where the fraction that is vaporized goes, because every atom that does not leave through the pumps ends up on something. Per wafer: an NXE:3800E at ~220 wafers per hour spends ~16 seconds per wafer, so on the order of 800,000 droplets are fired per wafer (the source runs continuously, whether or not the wafer stage is actively scanning). How many of those pulses any one spot on the wafer actually sees depends on how the exposure is done, which is worth previewing here (the full picture is under The Wafer Side, Stages in vacuum). The scanner does not flash a whole 26 × 33 mm field at once: the optics light only a narrow curved strip, the **slit**, ~26 mm wide and 2 to 3 mm tall at the wafer, and the wafer stage sweeps the field through that strip along its 33 mm length at ~0.6 to 0.8 m/s (an order-of-magnitude figure derived in that section). Each field therefore occupies ~0.1 to 0.15 s of wall-clock time, roughly 50 ms of scanning plus the turnaround to the next field, during which the source fires ~5,000 to 7,500 pulses; but a given point on the wafer is lit only while the slit passes over it, for (2.5 mm)/(0.7 m/s) ≈ 3.5 ms, which at 50 kHz is only ~150 to 250 pulses. The dose at any point is the sum over those couple of hundred pulses, so a 5% pulse-to-pulse energy jitter averages down to ~5%/√200 ≈ 0.35% in delivered dose, small enough against the few-percent dose tolerance of a layer, but not negligible, which is one reason the dose controller also trims the laser energy pulse by pulse.

> **What can go wrong:** The generator's failure modes are all versions of "the laser misses". A nozzle that partly clogs shifts or wobbles the jet; a worn piezo or a temperature drift changes the breakup timing; either way the droplet is not where the pulse arrives. The droplet camera and detection lasers see the jitter first, and the per-pulse energy sensor at the IF sees the EUV energy drop within a few pulses. The scanner's dose controller responds by slowing the wafer scan so that each field still receives its full dose, so the first symptom on the fab floor is a throughput dip rather than a bad wafer; if the source cannot recover, the tool stops and the generator is swapped, which costs hours of downtime plus pump-down. Because the generator is a known consumable, fabs trend the jitter and swap on schedule rather than on failure.

### The Trumpf CO₂ drive laser

The laser that turns tin droplets into plasma is a **CO₂ laser** operating at 10.6 µm, an infrared wavelength 785 times longer than the EUV it produces. The choice is deliberate and rests on how light gets into a plasma.

> **Intuition:** The free electrons in a plasma slosh back and forth at a natural frequency, the **plasma frequency**, that rises with their density. Light whose frequency is lower than the plasma frequency cannot get in; it is reflected, which is why AM radio bounces off the ionosphere while higher-frequency FM passes through. So for a given laser there is a **critical density** beyond which the beam cannot penetrate, and a long-wavelength, low-frequency laser is stopped at a lower electron density. It therefore deposits its energy in the thinner, outer part of the plasma, from which the EUV can escape instead of being re-absorbed on the way out.

The critical density scales as 1/λ². For 10.6 µm it is ~10¹⁹ electrons per cm³ (a few times fewer than the number of molecules in a cubic centimetre of the air around you), while for a 1.06 µm solid-state laser it is 100 times higher, ~10²¹ cm⁻³. A plasma heated at lower density is less opaque to its own EUV emission, and its ionization balance sits in the Sn⁸⁺–Sn¹⁴⁺ range over a larger volume. The practical result is that CO₂-driven tin plasmas reach 5 to 6% conversion efficiency, while 1 µm-driven plasmas plateau at 2 to 3%. Solid-state lasers are more compact and efficient, so this remains an active research topic (Gigaphoton and several academic groups have pursued 1 µm and even 2 µm thulium drive lasers), but production sources are CO₂.

The laser system is a **master oscillator, power amplifier (MOPA)** chain: a small, well-controlled laser sets the pulse shape and timing, and a chain of amplifiers adds power without changing the pulse's character. The seed is a radio-frequency-excited CO₂ oscillator (the gas is energized by a radio-frequency field between electrodes rather than by a DC spark), itself producing pulses of tens of nanoseconds, which is shaped and gated, sent through pre-amplifiers, and then through five large RF-excited CO₂ amplifier stages, each several metres long, built by Trumpf on the platform of its industrial cutting and welding lasers, which amplify the few-watt seed by more than 10,000×. The output is tens of kilowatts of average power in production sources (~25 to 40 kW; ASML has discussed higher for the 1 kW roadmap), pulsed at the droplet frequency, so each main pulse carries a few hundred millijoules in a few tens of nanoseconds: 300 mJ in 30 ns is 10 MW of peak power, delivered to a spot a few hundred micrometres across. The beam is transported ~20 metres through an evacuated **beam transport system** from the laser bay in the sub-fab up through the floor into the source vessel, steered by water-cooled mirrors, and finally focused through a hole in the centre of the collector mirror (the large mirror behind the plasma, described below) onto the droplet.

A subtlety that took years to control: a molten tin droplet is a nearly perfect spherical mirror at 10.6 µm. Some of the laser light that hits it reflects straight back up the beam path into the amplifier chain, where it is amplified in reverse and can destroy the seed optics. The laser therefore carries **optical isolators** (one-way valves for light, built either from a crystal that shifts the frequency of light passing through it or from polarizers), and the amplifiers are timed and run saturated (driven so hard by the forward pulse that they have little gain left to give) so that the back-reflection is barely amplified. **Thermal lensing** (the hot gas in an amplifier acts as a weak lens that shifts the focus), **wall-plug efficiency** (laser watts out per electrical watts in, a few percent for CO₂, so the laser alone dissipates on the order of a megawatt of electrical power as heat), and the gas mixture lifetime are the other everyday engineering problems of the drive laser.

### Pre-pulse and main pulse

Hitting a 27 µm sphere with a 10.6 µm beam is inefficient: the sphere is smaller than the focused spot and denser than optimal, and most of the laser energy would either miss or be reflected. Every production source therefore uses two pulses per droplet.

The **pre-pulse** arrives first. In early sources it was a separate solid-state laser (an **Nd:YAG**, the common neodymium-doped crystal laser emitting at 1.06 µm); newer sources derive it from the CO₂ chain. It is a low-energy pulse (millijoules, and in recent designs picoseconds long) that does not aim to make EUV; its purpose is to deform the droplet. The pre-pulse vaporizes a thin skin on the lit face of the droplet; the vapour flies off and its recoil hits the rest of the drop like a hammer blow. Liquid tin cannot compress, so it spreads sideways, exactly as a water drop flattens when it hits a table, and the droplet expands laterally into a thin disk or pancake, a few hundred micrometres across, over the next 1 to 3 microseconds, during which the target has moved a few hundred micrometres downstream. With a short (picosecond) pre-pulse the blow is sharp enough that the droplet instead shatters into a cloud of fine mist. Either way the target that the main pulse sees is now a low-density, laser-spot-sized object with far more surface area.

The **main pulse** then arrives, timed to the microsecond and pointed to the tens-of-micrometres where the expanded target now is. Its few hundred millijoules are absorbed efficiently by the disk, heating the tin to ~30–40 eV. The ions strip to Sn⁸⁺ through Sn¹⁴⁺ and radiate their UTA; the plasma expands and cools within a few hundred nanoseconds, and the EUV flash is over. The output from a single pulse is a few millijoules of in-band light into the hemisphere facing the collector. Fifty thousand of those per second is the source's several hundred watts.

The two-pulse scheme, the timing loops that keep the main pulse landing on the pancake, and the target shaping are the heart of Cymer's conversion-efficiency gains from ~2% in 2010 to ~5.5% around 2017 and up to 6% or a little more since. The 2026 demonstration of 1,000 W in-band relied on a further refinement: pre-shaping with two pulses and doubling the droplet rate to 100 kHz, so the per-pulse energy did not have to double.

> **Worked example: from laser pulse to watts at the intermediate focus.** This is an order-of-magnitude reconstruction, not ASML's budget, but it is a model you can reuse. A 25 kW laser firing at 50 kHz delivers 25,000/50,000 = 0.5 J per pulse. Multiply by a conversion efficiency of 0.055: 27 mJ of in-band EUV radiated into the forward hemisphere. Solid angle is measured in **steradians (sr)**, the three-dimensional analogue of an angle: just as a full circle is 2π radians, the full sphere of directions around a point is 4π ≈ 12.6 sr, and a hemisphere is 2π ≈ 6.3 sr. The collector spans ~5 sr of that hemisphere's 6.3 sr, so it catches a fraction 5/6.3 ≈ 0.8. Its reflectance when new is ~0.62. Hydrogen absorption along the path and the spectral filtering cost perhaps another 10%, a factor of ~0.9. So the energy at the IF per pulse is ≈ 0.5 J × 0.055 × 0.8 × 0.62 × 0.9 ≈ 12 mJ, and at 50,000 pulses per second that is ≈ 600 W, right in the NXE:3800E band. The model has four knobs: laser power (pulse energy), conversion efficiency, collector solid angle and reflectance, and droplet rate. The February 2026 1 kW demonstration mainly turned the last one, running 100 kHz at similar per-pulse energy (12 mJ × 100,000 ≈ 1,200 W before losses), which is why it did not need a laser twice the size.

### Collector

Directly behind the plasma sits the collector, an ellipsoidal mirror ~650 mm in diameter. An ellipse has two foci, and every ray leaving one focus reflects to the other; the plasma sits at the first focus and the intermediate focus is the second, a little over a metre away. The collector is a normal-incidence Mo/Si multilayer, graded in period across its surface because the angle of incidence varies from the centre to the rim, so the Bragg condition has to be met everywhere. It subtends ~5 steradians of the full sphere's 12.6 sr (the unit defined in the worked example above), so it sees about 40% of all the directions the plasma radiates into, and it reflects ~60 to 65% when new. It has a central aperture for the laser beam, and its front surface faces a plasma that emits ~2 kW of unwanted out-of-band radiation, tin ions at keV energies (a keV is 1,000 eV), tin neutrals, and tin microdroplets, all of which want to land on it.

### Source power at the intermediate focus

The power delivered to the IF is what ASML quotes as source power, and it has risen by a factor of four across the production generations:

| Scanner generation | Years | In-band power at the IF | Rated throughput (see Throughput, under The Wafer Side) |
|---|---|---|---|
| NXE:3400B / 3400C | 2017–2020 | 250 W | 125 wph (3400B, 2017); 170 wph at 20 mJ/cm², 135 wph at 30 mJ/cm² (3400C) |
| NXE:3600D | 2021 | ~350–400 W | 160 wph at 30 mJ/cm² |
| NXE:3800E | 2024 onward (as of 2025) | 500–600 W | > 195 wph initially, 220 wph with upgrades, at 30 mJ/cm² |
| EXE:5000 / EXE:5200B (High-NA) | 2023–2025 | 500–600 W | ~185 wph at 20 mJ/cm² (5000); 175 wph at 50 mJ/cm² (5200B), 220 targeted |
| Proof-of-concept source | February 2026 | 1,000 W | ~330 wph at 30 mJ/cm² projected by 2030; path to 1.5–2 kW stated (as of mid-2026) |

Every step has come from some combination of laser power, droplet rate, conversion efficiency, and collector efficiency, the four knobs of the worked example above: the climb from the 10–30 W of 2012 to 250 W was mostly conversion efficiency (~2% to ~5.5%, through the pre-pulse scheme), 250 W to 600 W came from laser power, collector efficiency and a modest droplet-rate increase (50 to 60 kHz), and the 1 kW demonstration was mostly droplet rate (100 kHz). What those watts buy in wafers per hour, and why the answer is not simply proportional, is worked out under Throughput and the dose trade-off.

### Tin debris mitigation

A tin plasma that expands at tens of kilometres per second, 50,000 times a second, next to a $1 million mirror is the central reliability problem of EUV. Three things arrive at the collector: fast ions (which **sputter** the multilayer: an incoming ion knocks atoms out of the surface), slow neutral atoms and clusters (which deposit as a tin film), and occasionally larger fragments. One **monolayer** (a single atomic layer) of tin on the collector costs about 1% reflectance; a few nanometres is a serious throughput loss.

The mitigation is built around hydrogen. The source vessel is not a hard vacuum but is back-filled with H₂ at a pressure on the order of 1 mbar (roughly 100 Pa, a thousandth of atmospheric pressure), flowing continuously. The gas serves three purposes. First, it decelerates the ions and atoms by collisions before they reach the collector, so the fast ions are **thermalized** (slowed by collisions to the temperature of the gas) and far fewer sputter the mirror. Second, the EUV light and the plasma dissociate H₂ into **hydrogen radicals** (lone H atoms, far more reactive than H₂), and atomic hydrogen reacts with tin to form **stannane (SnH₄)**, a gas at room temperature, which the vacuum pumps remove. Tin that has already deposited on the collector is slowly etched away by the same reaction, so the collector partly cleans itself in operation. Third, hydrogen is nearly transparent to 13.5 nm (it is the least absorbing gas at that wavelength; at 100 Pa it costs only ~10% per metre of path, and the vessel's actual pressure profile and path lengths are engineered to keep the total loss to a few percent), so the buffer pressure can be high enough to be useful without killing the light.

The source and the scanner want different pressures: the source vessel runs at ~100 Pa to stop tin, but the optics downstream want only a few pascals, because every metre of gas costs light. At the IF, where the source vessel connects to the scanner, a small aperture with a fast outward jet of hydrogen, the **dynamic gas lock**, lets the light through while blocking gas and tin from flowing into the scanner. Around the collector, gas flows are arranged as curtains and jets that push debris away from the mirror and away from the IF. Some designs add a **plasma flush**, an actively generated hydrogen plasma to accelerate tin removal. Gigaphoton's competing source design (never adopted by ASML) uses a superconducting magnet (windings that carry current without resistance when cooled) of about 1 **tesla (T)**, the unit of magnetic field strength (an MRI magnet is 1.5–3 T; Earth's field is ~0.00005 T), to steer the ions along field lines away from the collector; ASML's production approach is gas-based.

None of this is perfect. Collector reflectance decays with pulse count, historically a few percent per billion pulses; at 50,000 pulses per second a billion pulses is under six hours of firing. Run that sum at full duty and a collector would be finished in days, so two unstated assumptions are needed to turn it into the "every few weeks" that early fabs actually saw: early tools fired for only a fraction of the day (availability was under 70%, and the source is idle whenever the scanner is, so call the duty cycle ~30–50%), and a collector was tolerated until it had lost ~20–30% of its reflectance, with the dose controller slowing the scan to compensate. At 1–2% per billion pulses and ~1.5 × 10⁹ pulses per day at that duty cycle, the loss is ~1.5–3% per day and a 25% threshold is crossed in one to three weeks; these are order-of-magnitude reconstructions, since the real curves are proprietary. Progress on mitigation has stretched collector life to many months and ASML has targeted more than a year. The collector is designed to be removed and replaced in a swap that takes a shift, and the old collector is sent back to Cymer for stripping and recoating; the swap itself, plus the pump-down and re-alignment, is part of the machine's scheduled downtime budget.

> **What can go wrong:** The collector's decline is gradual and is trended, not discovered. The tool logs IF power at fixed laser energy pulse by pulse; as tin accumulates faster than the hydrogen etches it away, that ratio falls, the dose controller quietly slows the scan to keep dose per field constant, and throughput drifts down by a few percent a month. Tin can also build up on the IF aperture itself, clipping the beam. When the throughput loss outweighs a shift of downtime, the fab schedules a collector swap: vent, exchange, pump down, realign, requalify, roughly a shift plus the pump-down. The old collector goes back to Cymer, where a reflectance map made during refurbishment shows exactly where the tin and the sputter damage were, and that map feeds back into the gas-flow design of the next generation.

### Targeting, metrology, and availability

Keeping the laser on the droplets is a closed-loop control problem at 50 kHz. A **droplet detection module** illuminates the droplet stream with continuous lasers and watches the scattered light with fast **photodiodes** (semiconductor light sensors that turn a flash of light into a current pulse within nanoseconds) and cameras to measure each droplet's position and timing. Fast steering mirrors in the final focus assembly correct the pointing of the pre-pulse and main pulse, and the trigger timing is adjusted pulse by pulse. Energy sensors at the IF measure the EUV energy of every pulse; the scanner's dose controller uses this to adjust the drive laser and, if the source falls short, slows the wafer stage scan so that dose per area is preserved at the cost of throughput. Spectral purity is monitored because the plasma emits from the infrared to the EUV, and out-of-band DUV in particular exposes resist without contributing to the image; a spectral purity filter or the multilayer's own selectivity, plus the mirrors of the **illuminator** (the set of mirrors between the intermediate focus and the reticle that shapes and evens out the light, described under The Optics, some of which the light strikes at a glancing, grazing angle), keep the DUV fraction at the wafer low.

Source availability was the reason EUV was a decade late. **Availability** is the fraction of time the tool could actually expose wafers, measured over a rolling four weeks, and it counts every hour the tool cannot expose, including scheduled collector and droplet-generator swaps, so a source that fails every few days can never reach 90% no matter how good it is while running. In 2012 sources at customer sites delivered ~10 to 30 W with availability well under 70%; every droplet-generator clog, collector swap, and laser fault took the scanner down. ASML's goal through the 2010s was 250 W at greater than 90% four-week availability; 250 W was demonstrated in 2016 and reached at customer sites around 2017–2018 with the NXE:3400B, which is when Samsung and TSMC committed to EUV in production. By the early 2020s availability of 90% and above became typical, and the source no longer limits wafer output, though it still limits how far throughput can grow.

> **Why it matters downstream:** The per-pulse energy sensor at the IF is the first link in the chain that controls the printed feature size. Dose sets the position of every resist edge (a few percent more dose and a line is a fraction of a nanometre narrower), so the dose controller, integrating thousands of pulses per field and trimming laser energy and scan speed, is doing **critical dimension (CD)** control, the CD being the printed width of the feature that the layer's specification is written around, before the wafer has left the scanner. Module 13 closes that loop from the other end, measuring the printed CD and feeding corrections back to the scanner's dose and focus settings.

## The Optics

### The illuminator

Light leaving the intermediate focus enters the illuminator, whose job is to make the slit (introduced in the droplet worked example: the narrow curved strip of the reticle that is lit at any instant, a piece of the ring field explained at the end of this subsection) uniformly bright, and to set the angular distribution of the light arriving at the reticle, the **illumination pupil**, one of the strongest levers on resolution and **process window** (the range of dose and focus over which the print stays within specification), exactly as in DUV.

> **Intuition:** A pattern of lines at pitch p acts as a grating and bends light into beams at angles of roughly λ/p on either side of the straight-through beam. To form any image of those lines, at least two of the beams must fit inside the cone the projection optics can accept; a single beam carries no pattern. For the finest pitches the side beams are bent so far that they fall outside the cone, unless the illumination itself arrives tilted, so that one side beam and the straight-through beam straddle the cone. That is why the **pupil** (a picture of where the light comes from, as seen from the reticle) is deliberately shaped: a ring (**annular**), two spots (**dipole**, best for lines in one direction), four spots (**quasar**), or an arbitrary **freeform** pattern computed for a specific layout. The pupil shape is chosen per layer and is part of the recipe.

With no lenses available, the illuminator is built from two **facet mirrors** (a field facet mirror and a pupil facet mirror), each a segmented surface of hundreds of small, individually shaped and tilted tiles, acting as a reflective **fly's eye** integrator: each field facet projects its own image of the source onto the same reticle slit, so hundreds of overlapping images average the plasma's uneven brightness into a uniform arc, like many overlapping spotlights on a stage, while the pupil facets set the angle each contribution arrives from. By choosing which field facets are routed to which pupil facets (with tiltable facets in ASML's **FlexPupil** illuminators), the tool synthesizes annular, dipole, quasar, and freeform pupils without absorbing apertures that would waste light. One or two **grazing-incidence** fold mirrors complete the path. Because n < 1 at EUV, light striking a surface at a glancing angle of a few degrees is almost fully reflected, **total external reflection**, the mirror image of the total internal reflection that keeps light inside an optical fibre; think of a stone skipping on water. Grazing mirrors reflect over 80% at 13.5 nm, which is why the illuminator uses them wherever the geometry permits.

The numbers, as far as they are public: the two facet mirrors are each a few hundred millimetres across and carry a few hundred facets apiece (order of magnitude; Zeiss does not publish the counts), the field facets shaped as small arcs a few millimetres wide and a few centimetres long so that each one images directly onto the arc-shaped slit, and the pupil facets as small round or hexagonal tiles. The two facet mirrors are normal-incidence multilayers at ~67% each, and with one or two grazing folds at over 80% the illuminator as a whole passes roughly a quarter to a third of the light that arrives at the intermediate focus. Facet tilts are set by actuators under each tile and are repositioned in seconds when the layer recipe changes the pupil. The illuminator, like the projection optics, is designed and built by Carl Zeiss SMT and delivered to ASML as a sealed, aligned module; the FlexPupil control is ASML's.

> **What can go wrong:** The illuminator fails in three visible ways. Slit non-uniformity: if the overlapping facet images do not average out, one end of the slit is a few percent brighter than the other, so lines printed at one side of the field come out narrower than at the other; the scanner measures the intensity profile along the slit with a sensor on the wafer stage before every lot and corrects it by re-trimming facets and by its dose map. Pupil errors: a facet actuator that sticks, or a tile that has lost reflectance to contamination, leaves a dark spot or an asymmetry in the pupil, which prints as a horizontal-vertical bias or a pattern shift through focus; a pupil sensor on the wafer stage images the illumination pupil directly, and the fix is to re-route around the bad facet. And transmission loss: carbon growth or tin contamination on the first mirrors after the intermediate focus, the ones that see the most power, shows up as falling throughput at fixed source power, and is cleaned by the same hydrogen radicals that clean the collector.

The field at the reticle is not a rectangle but a curved **ring-field** arc, ~104 mm wide and a few millimetres tall at the reticle (26 mm × 2 to 3 mm at the wafer). Why a ring rather than a disk? Two constraints combine. First, the mirrors must be used off-axis: if they all shared a common axis, each would sit in the path of the light travelling to or from its neighbour and block it (a reflecting telescope tolerates a central obstruction; a lithography lens avoids one wherever it can, because the blocked angles are exactly the diffracted beams that carry the pattern, and the High-NA optics accept only a small one, described later). Second, in a system whose mirrors are surfaces of revolution about one axis, the residual aberrations depend only on distance from that axis, and the designer can drive them to zero at one chosen radius, not at every radius. The region where the image is sharp is therefore a thin ring at that radius, and the slit is the piece of the ring that the reticle covers, curved because the ring is. So the exposure is done by scanning the reticle and wafer through this arc, as in a DUV scanner (whose slit is a straight strip), but with the extra constraint that the ring field must be off-axis.

### The projection optics

The **projection optics box (POB)** in every NXE model is a **six-mirror, 4× reduction, 0.33 NA** all-reflective system designed and built by **Carl Zeiss SMT** in Oberkochen. An NA of 0.33 means the cone of light converging on the wafer has a half-angle of 19°; 0.55 (High-NA) means 33°. A larger NA collects more diffracted beams, so it prints finer features, at the price of a shallower depth of focus. **4× reduction** means the reticle pattern is drawn four times larger than it prints, so a 13 nm half-pitch line on the wafer is 52 nm on the reticle.

> **Intuition:** A curved mirror focuses light just as a lens does; a shaving mirror is a one-element imaging system. The difficulty is that a single curved mirror makes a distorted image everywhere except on its axis, so the six mirrors are arranged in series, each shaped to cancel the distortions the previous ones left behind, doing what a DUV lens does with 30 to 40 glass elements. Six is the compromise: four mirrors cannot reach 0.33 NA with the required correction over a usable field, and eight would cost two more 30% taxes on the light.

The mirrors are **aspheric** (not pieces of a sphere) and, in the newest designs, **freeform** (no axis of symmetry at all, so the surface is described by a map of heights rather than a formula), so that six surfaces can do the work of many. Mirror diameters range from ~100 mm to ~500 mm in the NXE optics and larger in High-NA. The full imaging field at the wafer is 26 mm × 33 mm, the same as DUV, so a reticle patterned over 104 mm × 132 mm prints a 26 × 33 mm field.

Each mirror is a substrate of ultra-low-expansion glass (**Zerodur** from Schott, a glass-ceramic, or Corning **ULE**, a titania-doped silica; both are formulated so that heating barely changes their dimensions) polished and then coated with the same Mo/Si multilayer as the collector. The requirements on the substrates are where EUV optics become extraordinary. Surface errors are sorted by their spatial period, because each band does a different kind of damage:

| Band | Spatial period | What it does to the image | Specification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Figure (low spatial frequency) | Millimetres to the full mirror | Aberrations: wrong focus, distorted shapes. A height error h produces a wavefront error of 2h on reflection | ~50 pm RMS or better per mirror |
| Mid-spatial-frequency roughness | Micrometres to millimetres | Scatters light at small angles into the image, producing **flare**, a uniform haze that lowers contrast | Flare a few percent in NXE tools; modelled in optical proximity correction (OPC, defined below) |
| High-spatial-frequency roughness | Sub-micrometre | Scatters light at wide angles, out of the pupil entirely, so it is simply lost | Below ~0.1 nm RMS |

**RMS** (root-mean-square) is the typical deviation of the surface from its ideal shape, averaged over the mirror; **wavefront error** is how far the crests of the reflected light wave depart from the ideal, and it must be a small fraction of 13.5 nm across the whole system. Fifty picometres is a quarter of a silicon atom's diameter. Zeiss's own comparison: if an NXE mirror were scaled up to the size of Germany, the largest bump would be about a millimetre high; for the High-NA mirrors it would be a tenth of a millimetre. Flare has to be modelled in **optical proximity correction (OPC)**, Module 07's practice of pre-distorting the mask pattern so that the print matches the design.

The mirrors are shaped by successive polishing, measured by **interferometry** (comparing the mirror against a reference by counting the interference fringes between light reflected from each) at visible wavelengths and at-wavelength, and finished by **ion beam figuring (IBF)**: a focused argon ion beam rasters over the surface, sputtering away material with a dwell time computed from the measured error map, removing picometres to nanometres locally without touching the surrounding area. The measurement is the harder half of the problem. Zeiss builds its own interferometers and reference optics, and the final test is exposing wafers in a fully assembled system. The projection optics box is delivered as one aligned unit, kept in vacuum, and its mirrors are actuated in situ, each on a **hexapod**-like mount (six legs, each a nanometre-resolution actuator, so the mirror can be tilted and shifted in every direction), so the scanner can correct aberrations that drift with temperature and multilayer aging over the tool's life.

> **What can go wrong:** The mirrors absorb the 30% of the light they do not reflect, and at 500 W sources that is watts of heat in a mirror whose shape is specified to 50 pm; the resulting thermal bumps are a real, measurable aberration. Hydrocarbons that reach the optics are cracked by EUV into carbon films that grow on the multilayer, and the multilayer itself ages (interdiffusion, oxidation). All three show up as wavefront error, which the scanner measures at-wavelength with a sensor on the wafer stage that images test patterns through the full optical path, and as lost throughput. Corrections come in layers: the hexapod actuators bend and shift the mirrors to cancel low-order aberrations, hydrogen radicals in the vessel etch the carbon away, and a slow drift that exceeds the actuators' range means a service visit. The optics box itself is designed never to be opened at the customer.

> **Worked example: transmission from source to wafer.** Count the reflections in an NXE system after the intermediate focus: about four in the illuminator (two facet mirrors and two fold mirrors; the grazing ones are more efficient but call all four ~0.67 to be conservative), one at the reticle (~0.65 for a patterned mask, less than a bare mirror because of the absorber and the cap), and six in the projection optics at ~0.67. That is 0.67⁴ × 0.65 × 0.67⁶ ≈ 0.20 × 0.65 × 0.091 ≈ 0.012, so ~1.2% of the light at the IF reaches the wafer plane. Add a pellicle (a protective membrane over the reticle, covered in the next section) at ~90% single-pass transmission, which the light crosses twice, giving another factor of 0.81, and hydrogen absorption over the beam path of a few percent, and the net is about 1%. A 500 W source therefore delivers on the order of 5 W to the wafer. A full 300 mm wafer has an area of 707 cm²; at a typical exposure **dose** of 30 mJ/cm² (the light energy per unit area the resist needs in order to switch from insoluble to soluble, in millijoules per square centimetre; the number is a property of the resist and is discussed under The Wafer Side) it needs 0.030 J/cm² × 707 cm² = 21 J. At 5 W that is a bit over 4 seconds of pure exposure, and the tool's 16 seconds per wafer at 220 wph is consumed largely by scanning overhead, stage turnaround between fields, and wafer exchange. That is why ASML's throughput gains come from both the source (dose time) and the stages (everything else).

## The Reflective Reticle

### Structure

An EUV reticle is a 152 mm × 152 mm × 6.35 mm plate of low-thermal-expansion glass (ULE) polished to a flatness of tens of nanometres, coated with the same ~40-bilayer Mo/Si multilayer and a ruthenium cap as the mirrors, and then with an **absorber** layer, ~55 to 70 nm of tantalum-based material (TaN or TaBN, with a thin TaBO or TaON anti-reflective top for inspection contrast). The pattern is written into the absorber by **e-beam writing** (a focused electron beam draws the pattern pixel by pixel over many hours, Module 04): reflective areas are bright, absorber-covered areas dark. The backside carries a conductive chromium-nitride film because the reticle is held by an electrostatic chuck; a vacuum chuck cannot work in vacuum. **Hoya** and **AGC** supply the blanks. A buried bump or pit in the multilayer larger than ~1 nm high and a few tens of nanometres wide prints as a **phase defect**: the bump shifts the timing of the reflected wave's crests just enough that they cancel against their neighbours, so it prints as a dark spot even though nothing absorbs. The yield of blanks free of such defects has been a problem for 15 years. **Captive** mask shops (in-house, at TSMC, Samsung, and Intel) plus **merchants** (Toppan/Photronics and DNP) deliver finished reticles at hundreds of thousands of dollars per plate.

From blank to finished mask, in order:

1. **Substrate.** A 152 × 152 × 6.35 mm ULE plate is polished flat to tens of nanometres, because any waviness becomes an image placement error at 4× reduction.
2. **Backside film.** A conductive CrN layer is deposited so the electrostatic chuck can hold the plate.
3. **Multilayer.** ~40 Mo/Si bilayers are laid down by ion-beam deposition, with the period held to 0.01 nm across the plate.
4. **Cap.** ~2–2.5 nm of ruthenium protects the top silicon from oxidation and cleaning.
5. **Blank inspection.** An **actinic** tool (one that inspects at the working wavelength, using 13.5 nm light itself, so that it sees exactly what the scanner will see; Lasertec's ABICS, described under Inspection and heating) maps every phase defect, so the pattern can later be shifted to bury known defects under absorber where possible.
6. **Absorber.** 55–70 nm of TaN/TaBN plus a thin anti-reflective top are deposited over the whole plate.
7. **Write.** A resist is coated and the electron beam exposes the pattern, taking hours to a day for a dense layer.
8. **Etch and strip.** The absorber is etched where the resist was opened, stopping on the ruthenium cap, and the resist is removed.
9. **Inspect and repair.** Actinic pattern inspection (Lasertec ACTIS) or e-beam inspection finds printable defects; a focused ion or electron beam repairs them by removing or adding absorber.
10. **Pellicle and pod.** A pellicle is mounted if the fab wants one, and the reticle is sealed in a dual pod for transport.

### The 6° angle and mask 3D effects

Because the reticle is a mirror, the illumination cannot arrive along the optical axis, or the reflected light would go straight back into the illuminator. The **chief ray angle (CRA)**, the angle of the central ray of the illumination cone from the reticle's normal, is 6° in the NXE optics. The cone around it is narrow because a 4× reduction system squeezes light from a 4× larger area into a 4× smaller one, so angles at the wafer are 4× steeper than at the reticle (size times angle is conserved through an imaging system): the reticle-side NA is 0.33/4 = 0.0825, i.e. ± 4.7°, and the whole cone spans roughly ± 5° around the 6° chief ray. That 6° tilt, combined with an absorber 60 to 70 nm tall, roughly the same height as the width of the features being printed at 4× (a 13 nm half-pitch line on the wafer is 52 nm on the reticle), produces a family of problems collectively called **mask 3D effects**:

- **Shadowing.** The absorber casts a shadow on the multilayer on one side of every feature. Lines running perpendicular to the plane of incidence are shadowed differently from lines parallel to it, producing a **horizontal-vertical (HV) bias** of a few nanometres between identical features drawn in the two orientations, which OPC must correct.
- **Pattern shift through focus (non-telecentricity).** A **telecentric** system is one where the rays hit the wafer perpendicularly, so that defocus blurs the image but does not move it sideways. On the reticle side an EUV system is not telecentric: the reflected light from a feature leaves at an angle, so when the wafer moves through focus the image moves sideways. The shift depends on feature size and pitch, so it cannot be fixed by a global overlay correction; it becomes part of the **edge placement error (EPE)** budget, the total error in where a printed edge lands.
- **Best-focus shifts and contrast loss** between different pitches, because the phase of the light diffracted off the absorber edge varies with the absorber's height and refractive index.

> **Worked example: how big is the shadow.** Light arriving 6° off normal past an absorber 60 nm tall casts a shadow of length 60 nm × tan 6° ≈ 60 × 0.105 ≈ 6.3 nm on the multilayer, at mask scale. Divided by the 4× reduction that is ~1.6 nm at the wafer, on a 13 nm line: a 12% width change on one orientation and not the other, the size of the HV bias that OPC has to remove. The same geometry explains the pellicle's cost: a membrane of single-pass transmission T is crossed twice, on the way in and on the way out, so the dose at the wafer is scaled by T². For the polysilicon and silicide pellicles at 83–88% single pass, T² = 0.83² ≈ 0.69 to 0.88² ≈ 0.77, which is the 20–30% dose penalty quoted below.

Mitigations are all in progress: thinner absorbers; absorbers with higher extinction (ruthenium-, platinum-, or nickel-based alloys) so they can be thinner; **low-n absorbers** that act as **attenuated phase-shift masks (att-PSM)** at EUV (Module 07's mask type whose dark areas leak a little light 180° out of phase to sharpen edges); and mask-3D-aware OPC (Module 07's OPC problem, made harder by a full 3D electromagnetic simulation of every edge). The most important mitigation, though, is the reason the High-NA optics are **anamorphic** (having a different magnification in the two directions, 8× along the scan and 4× across it, so that the reticle-side angles stay small in the direction where shadowing is worst), covered under High-NA.

### Pellicles

A DUV reticle is protected by a **pellicle**, a thin transparent membrane held a few millimetres above the mask surface so that any particle landing on it sits out of the focal plane and does not print. For EUV no membrane material is transparent enough; a polymer would absorb everything. The first EUV tools ran without pellicles, which meant every particle that landed on the reticle printed on every **die** (one chip's worth of wafer, Module 00) of every wafer until the reticle was inspected and cleaned. Fabs managed this with very frequent reticle inspection, in-vacuum reticle handling with the mask kept in a **dual pod** (the outer pod is a sealed plastic carrier box like the ones wafers travel in, the inner pod a clean metal enclosure that is only opened inside the scanner's reticle load lock), and by accepting some yield loss on layers with large dies.

The EUV pellicle developed by ASML with **imec** (the Belgian nanoelectronics research institute in Leuven where much of the industry's shared EUV development is done) was a ~50 nm freestanding polysilicon membrane, later a metal-silicide stack, with ~83 to 88% single-pass transmission. Light crosses it twice, so the dose penalty is 20 to 30%, and the membrane, heated by absorbed light in vacuum with no convection to cool it, sags and fails at higher source powers. **Mitsui Chemicals** licensed ASML's pellicle in 2021 and mass-produces it. The next generation is the **carbon nanotube (CNT) pellicle** developed by imec and commercialized by Mitsui (partnership signed December 2023; a 5,000-sheet-per-year plant at Iwakuni-Ohtake completed at the end of 2025 for introduction in 2025–2026, as of mid-2026): a sparse mat of nanotubes that transmits 92% or more single-pass (imec has shown > 94%, i.e. ~85–89% double-pass) and is designed to survive 600 W and, eventually, 1 kW sources because the tubes radiate heat efficiently; hydrogen-radical etching of the carbon is the remaining lifetime question. Pellicle use is a fab decision: TSMC uses pellicles selectively, where the yield gain outweighs the throughput loss, and some memory makers ran pellicle-free longer.

> **What can go wrong:** On a pellicle-free reticle a single 50 nm particle sits in the focal plane and prints as a ~12 nm defect (50 nm/4) on every die of every wafer until someone notices, and at 13 nm half-pitch a 12 nm blob is a short or an open. Fabs catch it two ways: by inspecting the reticle at intervals (every few hundred or few thousand wafers, depending on the layer) and by the wafer inspection tools of Module 13, which flag a defect that repeats at the same field position on every die. The fix is a reticle clean, and the yield loss is the wafers exposed in between. Pellicles trade that risk for two of their own: the membrane sags or ruptures as source power rises, since it cannot shed heat by convection in vacuum, and the CNT pellicle is slowly thinned by the same hydrogen radicals that keep the optics clean, which is why its lifetime at 600 W and above is still being qualified.

### Inspection and heating

The problem: a buried multilayer bump 1 nm high does not absorb anything, and a 60 nm absorber edge at mask scale is a 15 nm edge at the wafer, so neither a visible-light nor a DUV inspection microscope can see the defects that matter at the required sensitivity. A phase defect is invisible at any wavelength except the one whose Bragg reflection it disturbs. EUV reticle inspection has therefore moved to actinic tools, the at-wavelength inspectors introduced in the blank-to-mask list above, which use 13.5 nm light and so see the mask exactly as the scanner does.

How an actinic inspector works follows from everything in this module: no lenses, so the microscope is a set of Mo/Si multilayer mirrors in vacuum, and no laser, so the light comes from a small plasma source of its own that is far dimmer than a scanner's, which is why the inspection is slow. **Lasertec** of Japan is the sole supplier, with two machines. **ABICS**, for blanks, works in **dark field**: a perfect blank reflects the illumination cleanly in one direction, so a detector placed off that direction sees nothing until a bump or pit scatters light into it, and the image is a black background with a bright dot at every defect, which is the most sensitive way to find a few defects on an otherwise empty plate. **ACTIS**, for patterned masks, works in **bright field**: an EUV mirror objective forms a magnified image of the pattern on an EUV-sensitive camera, and software compares each region with the neighbouring die or with the design database, flagging any difference. Sensitivity is set by the physics of the scanner itself: ABICS finds the ~1 nm-high, tens-of-nanometre-wide phase defects that would print, and ACTIS finds absorber defects of a few tens of nanometres at mask scale, under 10 nm at the wafer (order of magnitude; Lasertec's exact specifications are customer-confidential). Covering the full 104 × 132 mm pattern area at that resolution takes hours per mask (an order-of-magnitude figure), and each machine costs on the order of tens of millions of dollars, so a mask shop owns a few and schedules them carefully. KLA competes with e-beam and DUV approaches, which are faster but cannot see phase defects. Because reticle defects print on every die, EUV reticle inspection frequency is orders of magnitude higher than for a pelliclized DUV mask: a pellicle-free EUV reticle is re-inspected every few hundred to few thousand wafers, as described under Pellicles, and every re-inspection is a trip out of the scanner's vacuum and back.

Finally, the reticle absorbs roughly 40% of the light hitting it (the absorber areas absorb almost all, the multilayer areas ~30%). At 500 W at the IF and 26 mm × 104 mm of illuminated arc, the mask's local temperature rises during exposure, and even with ULE glass the expansion produces overlay error at the nanometre level; the scanner models and pre-compensates reticle heating field by field.

> **Why it matters downstream:** That 40% absorption is why reticle heating is an overlay term, not a footnote. The heat load moves with the scan, so the reticle expands unevenly, and a nanometre of pattern displacement at the reticle is a quarter of a nanometre at the wafer, against an overlay budget of ~1.5–2 nm. The scanner therefore carries a thermal model of the reticle, fed by the dose and pupil of the layer being printed, and adjusts the stage trajectory and mirror actuators field by field to cancel the predicted drift. When the model is wrong (a new absorber, an unusual pattern density) the error shows up in Module 13's overlay metrology as a signature that grows through the lot.

## The Wafer Side

### A scanner that is a vacuum chamber

Everything downstream of the source, the illuminator, reticle stage, projection optics, and wafer stage, sits inside a single vacuum vessel about the size of a small bus, pumped continuously and back-filled with hydrogen at a few pascals. Wafers enter through **load locks** (small chambers that are pumped down and vented so the big vessel never sees air) from an atmospheric wafer handler that receives **FOUPs** (front-opening unified pods, the sealed 25-wafer boxes wafers travel in) from the track. Module 07's TEL or SCREEN track is docked to the scanner exactly as with DUV, but the interface includes a vacuum pump-down cycle per wafer. Hydrogen serves the same anti-contamination role here as in the source: EUV light cracks any hydrocarbon that **outgasses** (molecules slowly leaving a solid into the vacuum) from the resist, the cables, or the stage components into carbon that deposits on the mirrors, and the hydrogen radicals etch it off again. Every material inside the vessel has to be qualified for outgassing, and every resist has to pass an outgassing test before it is allowed in the tool, because a resist that deposits carbon on the $100 million optics box is unacceptable.

A wafer's path through the tool, in order:

1. **FOUP to track.** The wafer leaves its FOUP and is coated with underlayer and ~25 nm of EUV resist on the docked track (details under EUV Resists).
2. **Atmospheric handler.** A robot passes the coated wafer to the scanner's front end at atmospheric pressure.
3. **Load lock.** The wafer enters a small chamber that is pumped from air to a few pascals in tens of seconds; the main vessel never sees air.
4. **Measurement stage.** In vacuum, the wafer is clamped to a chuck and one of the two wafer stages measures it: alignment marks give its position and distortion, and a level sensor maps its height to the nanometre.
5. **Stage swap.** The measured wafer's stage moves under the projection optics while the other stage, carrying the just-exposed wafer, moves out.
6. **Exposure.** The stage scans each of ~100 fields through the arc-shaped image while the reticle stage scans in step at 4× the speed; the whole wafer takes ~16 s.
7. **Unload.** The wafer goes back through a load lock, which vents to air, and the atmospheric handler returns it to the track.
8. **Track.** Post-exposure bake, develop, rinse and dry happen on the track; then the wafer goes to metrology (Module 13).

Particles are a larger problem than in DUV because there is no pellicle by default and because vacuum has no air flow to carry particles away; moving stages, cable slabs, and door mechanisms are all engineered to shed nothing. Wafer clamping is electrostatic: the wafer table, made by ASML's Berliner Glas subsidiary, is a ceramic plate with embedded electrodes and thousands of **burls**, small bumps on the order of half a millimetre across (an estimate; the design is proprietary), so that the wafer touches only a fraction of a percent of the chuck's area and a stray particle between wafer and chuck is far more likely to fall in a gap than to sit on a bump and tilt the wafer. A voltage between the buried electrodes and the wafer pulls the wafer down, like a balloon rubbed on a sweater sticking to a wall, with a clamping pressure on the order of kilopascals (an estimate), and it works in vacuum where suction cannot. Cooling of the wafer during exposure is done through the small amount of hydrogen gas trapped under the wafer, since there is no air.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Silicon expands 2.6 parts per million per kelvin. A 0.01 K temperature difference across a 300 mm wafer therefore changes its diameter by 300 mm × 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ × 0.01 ≈ 8 nm, several times the ~1.5–2 nm overlay budget, from a temperature change you could not measure with a good thermometer. The scanner's alignment model corrects a uniform expansion, but not the local hot spots that the exposure itself creates, which is why the few watts that the resist absorbs have to be conducted away through the hydrogen film under the wafer and why the chuck's thermal design is an overlay specification.

> **What can go wrong:** Three things routinely bite. A resist that outgasses more than its qualification promised leaves carbon on the projection optics, seen as a throughput loss and a wavefront drift; every new resist passes a **witness-sample** outgassing test (a clean multilayer mirror sample sits beside the resist while it is irradiated with EUV, and is then checked for carbon growth) in a dedicated chamber before it is allowed near a production tool. Particles shed by stages, cable carriers or load-lock doors land on wafers or, worse, on the reticle, and are found by wafer inspection as non-repeating (wafer) or repeating (reticle) defects. And when the source underperforms, the dose controller slows the scan, so the first sign of a sick source is a tool that is running but delivering fewer wafers per hour than its specification; fabs watch that number daily.

### Stages in vacuum

The problem the stages solve is easy to state and brutal to meet: carry a 300 mm wafer through a strip of light at the better part of a metre per second, in vacuum, while holding its position to a nanometre, and do it for ~100 fields with as little dead time between them as possible, because once the source is past ~500 W the dead time, not the exposure, is most of the 16 seconds a wafer takes. It is worth seeing exactly what "scanning" means before the numbers.

```
   Top view of one 26 x 33 mm field on the wafer (not to scale)

   +----------------------------+  ^
   |                            |  |
   |                            |  |     the slit: a fixed, curved strip of light
   |  ======== slit ==========  |  |     26 mm wide and 2-3 mm tall; the wafer
   |  ========================  |  | 33  stage carries the field through it at
   |                            |  | mm  ~0.6-0.8 m/s, so every point is lit for
   |                            |  |     a few milliseconds, then the stage stops,
   |                            |  |     steps 26 mm sideways, and scans the next
   +----------------------------+  v     field the other way
   <---------- 26 mm ---------->

   Above it the reticle stage carries the 4x larger pattern through the
   4x larger arc, in step, at 4x the speed (~2.5-3 m/s).
```

The slit does not move; the wafer does. The projection optics image the lit arc of the reticle onto a fixed arc in space, and the wafer stage drives the field through that arc along its 33 mm length while the reticle stage drives the reticle through the illuminated arc at the reticle at exactly four times the speed, so that every point of the pattern and its image on the wafer stay locked together as they cross the slit. Every point on the wafer is lit only while the 2 to 3 mm slit passes over it, a few milliseconds, and its dose is the sum of the source pulses that arrive in that window. At the end of each field the wafer stage decelerates, steps sideways by one field width, and accelerates back up to speed in the opposite direction, so the fields are exposed in a serpentine; the reticle stage reverses in step. Because the reticle moves four times as far in the same time, it needs four times the acceleration to turn around in the same time, which is why the reticle stage has the highest accelerations in the machine.

The stage architecture is inherited from TWINSCAN, ASML's two-stage design in which one wafer is measured (aligned and height-mapped) on one stage while the other is exposed, and then the stages swap, so the measurement time is hidden behind the exposure time. In vacuum the long-stroke stages are **magnetically levitated** planar motors (no air bearings, no lubricant, the moving part floating on magnetic fields), and the position is tracked by laser **interferometers** (counting the fringes of a reflected laser beam) and, on newer tools, by optical **encoders** reading a fine grating, at sub-nanometre resolution. The accelerations are the sort a race car would envy: ASML states that the High-NA EXE wafer stage accelerates at 8 g, twice the NXE wafer stage, and the EXE reticle stage at 32 g, four times the NXE's, which puts the NXE stages at roughly 4 g (wafer) and 8 g (reticle). Reaction forces are absorbed by balance masses so the vessel does not shake, and the stage's control loop, running at tens of kilohertz, holds the wafer on its planned trajectory as it crosses the slit. Two figures of merit describe how well it does that: the **moving average (MA)** of the position error over the few milliseconds a point spends under the slit, which becomes an overlay error, and the **moving standard deviation (MSD)**, the wobble around that average, which smears the image and is seen as a CD change and contrast loss (**fading**). MA must sit well inside the ~1.5–2 nm overlay budget and MSD must be a small fraction of the ~13 nm feature; ASML's exact figures are not public. The NXE:3800E's ~35–40% throughput gain over the NXE:3600D (220 versus 160 wph at 30 mJ/cm²) came as much from a new wafer stage, faster reticle stage, and faster wafer handling as from source power. The stages are ASML's own design; nobody else makes anything like them.

> **Worked example: the scan, pulse by pulse.** Everything here follows from the dose. The light arriving at the wafer, P ≈ 5 W for a 500 W source (the transmission worked example), is spread over a slit w = 26 mm wide, and a point on the wafer collects it for the time the slit takes to pass, h/v, where h is the slit height and v the scan speed. So the dose is D = P/(w × v), independent of the slit height, and the scan speed follows: v = P/(w × D) = 5 W/(2.6 cm × 0.030 J/cm²) ≈ 64 cm/s ≈ 0.6 m/s at 30 mJ/cm², and ~0.3 m/s at 60 mJ/cm². (These are order-of-magnitude figures; ASML does not publish scan speeds.) The reticle stage runs at 4× that, ~2.6 m/s. A point on the wafer is under a 2.5 mm slit for 2.5 mm/0.64 m/s ≈ 3.9 ms, during which a 50 kHz source fires ~195 pulses: with a 2 to 3 mm slit, ~150 to 250 pulses decide the dose at every point, which is the number the droplet-generator worked example used for the jitter averaging. One field takes (33 mm + 2.5 mm of slit overhang)/0.64 m/s ≈ 55 ms to scan, and ~100 fields (including the partial ones at the wafer edge) take ~5 s, a little more than the 4.2 s of pure exposure because of the overhang and the partial fields. The turnaround: reversing 0.64 m/s at ~4 g (~40 m/s²) takes 2 × 0.64/40 ≈ 32 ms, and the 26 mm sideways step at the same acceleration takes 2 × √(0.026/40) ≈ 51 ms; overlapping the two, each turnaround costs ~50–60 ms plus settling, so ~100 turnarounds cost ~6–7 s. The remaining ~4–5 s of the 16 s wafer is the stage swap, wafer exchange and load-lock cycle, which do not overlap with exposure. Now put a 1 kW source in: v doubles to ~1.3 m/s, the scan time halves to ~2.5 s, and each point sees only ~100 pulses, but the ~6–7 s of turnarounds and the ~4–5 s of swap and exchange are untouched unless the stages get faster too. That is the arithmetic behind ASML's "source plus stages" roadmap and behind the EXE's 8 g and 32 g stages.

> **What can go wrong:** Stage problems print as overlay and CD errors rather than as obvious defects. Vibration or a servo that lags the trajectory raises MSD, blurring the image across the slit so that lines print wider and with less contrast (fading), while a bias in MA shifts every feature in the field by the same amount; both are caught by the overlay and CD metrology of Module 13, which can distinguish a field-position signature (stage) from a lens or reticle-heating signature (optics), and by the stage's own error logs, which the tool reports per field. A particle under the wafer that lands on a burl tilts the wafer locally by nanometres, which the level sensor sees as a height bump and the focus control cannot fully follow, so the fix is to find and remove the particle from the chuck. Contamination on an encoder grating or a drifting interferometer path (the hydrogen pressure changes the refractive index of the gas the beam crosses) shows up as slow overlay drift and is corrected by recalibration against alignment marks. And the stage swap and wafer exchange, which are pure overhead, are timed to the millisecond; a slow load-lock pump-down or a robot that has to retry a handoff costs wafers per hour directly, which is why fabs watch throughput per tool daily.

### Throughput and the dose trade-off

Dose, defined in the transmission worked example, is the light energy per unit area delivered to the resist, in millijoules per square centimetre (mJ/cm²); a resist switches from insoluble to soluble at a threshold dose, so the dose is a recipe parameter, not a free choice. ASML specifies throughput at a reference dose. The NXE:3400C was rated at 170 wafers per hour (wph) at 20 mJ/cm² (135 wph at 30 mJ/cm²); the NXE:3600D at 160 wph at 30 mJ/cm²; the NXE:3800E at > 195 wph initially and 220 wph with upgrades, both at 30 mJ/cm², with the 500–600 W source (as of 2025). A fab that runs a layer at 60 mJ/cm² (which many **contact** and **via** layers do, to suppress stochastic defects; contacts are the small vertical holes that connect a transistor to the first wire, and vias connect one wiring layer to the next, Module 12) doubles the exposure time. The relationship between dose and throughput is not linear because of the fixed overhead per wafer, and the one-line model below shows why.

> **Worked example: seconds per wafer.** Time per wafer ≈ overhead + (dose × wafer area)/(power at the wafer). At 30 mJ/cm² a 707 cm² wafer needs 21 J; with ~5 W at the wafer that is 21/5 ≈ 4.2 s of exposure. The NXE:3800E's 220 wph means 3,600/220 ≈ 16 s per wafer, so the overhead (field-to-field turnarounds, stage swap, wafer exchange, load-lock cycles) is ~16 − 4.2 ≈ 12 s. Now double the dose to 60 mJ/cm²: exposure becomes 8.4 s, the wafer takes ~20 s, and throughput falls to ~175 wph, not to half. Now give the tool a 1 kW source, ~10 W at the wafer: exposure at 30 mJ/cm² drops to 2.1 s, but ASML's projected ~330 wph needs 3,600/330 ≈ 10.9 s per wafer, so the overhead must fall from ~12 s to ~9 s. That is why ASML's roadmap is always "source plus stages": past ~500 W, the source alone buys less than the stages and wafer handling do.

That relation is the whole economic argument for the source power roadmap, and for the 1 kW source's projected ~330 wph at 30 mJ/cm² by 2030 (as stated in 2026).

> **Worked example: what an EUV pass costs.** All of these are estimates; ASML does not publish list prices and fabs do not publish costs. A $200 million tool depreciated over 5 years (its purchase price spread evenly over five years of accounting) is $40 million per year. Service contracts, collector and droplet-generator swaps, and ~1 to 1.5 MW of electricity (mostly the laser) add on the order of $10–20 million per year (a rough estimate). A realistic ~150 wph (after dose, availability and rework) × 7,000 productive hours per year ≈ 1.05 million wafer-passes per year, so the tool alone costs ~$50–60 per pass, ~$38 of it depreciation, matching Module 07. Add mask amortization (a reticle at hundreds of thousands of dollars spread over its life), EUV resist and underlayer, the track, and metrology, and an all-in EUV pass is ~$100–150 (Module 07's figure). A leading-edge logic wafer sees 15 to 25 EUV passes, so EUV contributes ~$2,000–3,500 to a wafer that sells for $20,000 to $30,000, roughly a tenth. Expensive, and still cheaper than the SAQP flows it replaced: Module 07's comparison put one tight metal layer at ~$200–250 by SAQP against ~$120–170 by single-exposure EUV.

## Photon Physics and Stochastics

### Very few photons

Here is the number that shapes all EUV process development. A 13.5 nm photon carries 92 eV, versus 6.4 eV for an ArF photon. For the same energy dose in millijoules per square centimetre, EUV delivers 14 times fewer photons.

> **Intuition:** Photons arrive independently and at random, like raindrops on a paving stone. If the average number landing on a patch is N, the actual count varies from patch to patch by about √N; this is a **Poisson** process, and the spread is a law of nature, not a flaw in the source. The relative fluctuation, √N/N = 1/√N, is what hurts: 10,000 raindrops fluctuate by 1%, 100 by 10%. Engineers call the spread σ (**sigma**, the standard deviation) and quote tolerances as **3σ**, a band wide enough to include 99.7% of cases. EUV's problem is that its N is small.

> **Worked example: photons per square nanometre at 30 mJ/cm².** One photon at 13.5 nm has energy E = 1240/13.5 = 91.8 eV = 91.8 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.47 × 10⁻¹⁷ J. A dose of 30 mJ/cm² is 0.030 J spread over 10¹⁴ nm², i.e. 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ J/nm². Dividing, the dose corresponds to ~20 photons per nm² incident on the resist; a 20 mJ/cm² dose, which is where resist suppliers would love to operate for throughput, is ~14 photons/nm². Now account for what is actually absorbed. Resist absorption is quoted in **µm⁻¹**, an absorption per micrometre of path: it is simply the reciprocal of the absorption length defined under Everything absorbs EUV, so 5 µm⁻¹ means the intensity falls by a factor of e every 1/5 µm = 200 nm. A chemically amplified resist absorbs ~5 µm⁻¹ at 13.5 nm, so a 30 nm film, 0.15 absorption lengths thick, captures only 1 − e^(−0.15) ≈ 14% of the incident photons, roughly 3 absorbed photons per nm². A 16 nm × 16 nm pixel at the edge of a contact hole, the region that decides whether the hole is open or closed, is defined by ~750 absorbed photons at 30 mJ/cm². The Poisson standard deviation of that count is √750 ≈ 27, a 3.6% fluctuation in exposure from a source that is otherwise perfectly stable. On a 1 nm-wide strip along a line edge the count is a few dozen photons and the fluctuation is 15 to 20%. Compare ArF immersion: at the same 30 mJ/cm², 290 photons/nm² incident, and several tens absorbed in a typical 100 nm film; photon shot noise is not on the list of ArF problems.

### Consequences: stochastics

Because the photon count per feature is small and random, the chemical result of exposure varies from feature to feature even when the optical image is identical. The industry term is **stochastics**, and it manifests as a defect population with no deterministic cause: a contact hole that fails to open (a **missing contact**), two adjacent holes that merge (a **kissing contact**), a line that breaks, or a bridge between lines. The probability of any single feature failing may be 10⁻⁹ or 10⁻¹²; a chip with 10¹⁰ contacts on a critical layer then has a defect somewhere with a probability that decides the yield. Stochastic failure rates rise exponentially as the feature is pushed smaller or the dose is lowered, which is why EUV process windows are described not just in terms of CD and focus but with a **stochastic failure cliff** on each side.

> **Worked example: the stochastic cliff.** Treat the photon count as **Gaussian** (the bell curve, which a Poisson distribution approaches once the mean is in the hundreds) and say, for illustration, that a contact fails to open if its edge pixel receives 20% fewer photons than average. At 30 mJ/cm² the mean is 750 and σ ≈ 27, so a 20% shortfall (150 photons) is 150/27 ≈ 5.5σ below the mean; the Gaussian tail beyond 5.5σ is ~2 × 10⁻⁸. Halve the dose: the mean is 375, σ ≈ 19, and the same 20% shortfall (75 photons) is only 75/19 ≈ 3.9σ out, a tail of ~5 × 10⁻⁵. Halving the dose has multiplied the failure rate by thousands; that is the cliff. The real chemistry is more complicated (and less forgiving) than a Gaussian, but the shape is right. Now count: a layer with 10¹⁰ contacts at a failure rate of 10⁻⁹ has ~10 dead contacts per chip, and unless the design has redundancy, every one of those chips is scrap. That is why contact and via layers run at 60 mJ/cm² and eat throughput to do it.

**Line-edge roughness (LER)**, the wobble of one edge along a line, and **line-width roughness (LWR)**, the resulting variation in the width between two edges, are the continuous version of the same statistics, and they are quoted as 3σ so that the number covers nearly every point along the line. EUV lines at 13 nm half-pitch have LWR of ~2 to 4 nm (3σ) in the best resists, a large fraction of the linewidth, and roughness at line edges transfers into transistor variability and interconnect resistance variation.

The photon shot noise is compounded by the resist's own chemistry. Recall the resist: a **chemically amplified resist (CAR)** is a polymer whose protecting groups (the bulky side groups of the Before-you-start list) make it insoluble; a **photoacid generator (PAG)** releases an acid molecule when hit; during the **post-exposure bake (PEB)** each acid hops around and strips hundreds of protecting groups (**deprotection**), making that region soluble, while a **quencher** base mops up stray acid so the edge stays sharp. Each absorbed 92 eV photon does not expose one molecule; it ejects a photoelectron that scatters in the film, generating a cascade of ~4 to 8 **secondary electrons** with energies of a few to tens of eV over a radius of a few nanometres, and it is those electrons that decompose the photoacid generator (in a CAR) or **cross-link** (form chemical bonds between neighbouring molecules so that they become one insoluble network) the metal clusters of a metal-oxide resist, the alternative resist platform, built from tin-oxide clusters rather than a polymer, described in the next section. That **secondary electron blur** of ~2 to 4 nm sets a floor on the resolution and adds its own randomness (how many secondaries, where they went, which molecules they hit). Then in a CAR the acid diffuses during the post-exposure bake, each acid catalyzing a random number of deprotection events, and the quencher neutralizes acids at random. Every stage is a counting process and every stage adds variance.

The design response is captured in the **RLS triangle**: Resolution, Line-edge roughness, and Sensitivity (dose) cannot all be improved at once. A resist can be made faster (lower dose) by increasing acid yield or diffusion, but then fewer photons decide each edge and diffusion blurs it; it can be made smoother by adding quencher and shortening diffusion, but then it needs more dose. The empirical **Z-factor**, Z = R³ × L² × S, where R is the resolution (half-pitch), L the LWR and S the sensitivity (dose to print), has stayed roughly constant across a resist generation, and each new platform tries to lower it. The equation says that resolution is the expensive term: shrinking R by a little requires a lot more dose or a lot more roughness. To print 8 nm instead of 13 nm with the same resist technology, R³ falls by (13/8)³ ≈ 4.3, so L² × S must rise 4.3×: either 4.3× the dose at the same roughness, or 2.1× the roughness at the same dose, or some mixture. That is the High-NA resist problem in one line. Doses that were expected to be 10 to 15 mJ/cm² when EUV was planned in the 2000s are in practice 30 to 60 mJ/cm² for critical layers because that is what the stochastics demand, and the source power roadmap has had to make up the difference.

> **What can go wrong:** A stochastic defect rate of 10⁻⁹ cannot be seen by looking at a few hundred contacts under a microscope. Fabs find these defects by very-large-area electron-beam inspection (Module 13), scanning millions to billions of contacts on test wafers and reporting failures per billion, and by the electrical test structures that show a via chain open. The levers, once the rate is known, are: raise the dose (and pay in throughput); switch to a higher-absorption resist so more of the incident photons count; tune the underlayer to add secondary electrons from below; use stochastic-aware OPC that enlarges the features most likely to fail; and design rules that keep contacts away from the cliff by forbidding the tightest pitches on the layers that cannot afford a 10⁻⁹ failure rate.

> **Why it matters downstream:** Roughness does not stay in the resist. A 3 nm (3σ) LWR on a 13 nm gate becomes a spread of gate lengths across a chip, and gate length sets a transistor's threshold voltage, so LWR becomes threshold-voltage variation and, through it, timing and leakage variation between nominally identical transistors (Module 11). On interconnect layers, roughness in a via's diameter becomes a spread in via resistance, and the slowest via on a signal path sets the clock (Module 12). Both are why LER is a yield and performance number, not a cosmetic one.

## EUV Resists

### Chemically amplified resists at EUV

Module 07's chemically amplified resist (CAR) platform, a polymer with acid-**labile** (easily removed) protecting groups, a photoacid generator (PAG), and a quencher, was extended to EUV by **JSR**, **TOK**, **Shin-Etsu**, **Fujifilm**, and **DuPont**, with JSR and TOK holding the largest EUV share. The adaptations: thinner films (~20 to 35 nm, versus 80 to 120 nm for ArF immersion) because tall, narrow resist lines suffer **capillary collapse** during development (as the rinse water dries, surface tension pulls neighbouring lines together, and the taller and thinner the line the worse it is); polymer-bound PAGs to limit acid diffusion; higher PAG loading; and, in the newest formulations, metal-containing sensitizers (tin, iodine, or fluorine, elements with a high 13.5 nm **absorption cross-section**, the effective target area an atom presents to a photon) to absorb more of the sparse photons, moving absorption from ~4 to ~6 µm⁻¹ (the unit defined in the photon worked example: 5 µm⁻¹ is an absorption length of 200 nm, so a 30 nm film catches ~14% of the light). CARs remain dominant for lines and spaces at N5/N3 because of their maturity, their compatibility with the existing track and with **TMAH** aqueous develop (tetramethylammonium hydroxide, the standard water-based developer), and their low defectivity.

Underneath the resist is an **underlayer** (spin-on carbon or a thin organic adhesion and secondary-electron-emitting layer, a few nanometres thick) that improves adhesion, reduces pattern collapse, and in some designs adds secondary electrons back into the resist from below. Thin EUV resists are typically transferred first into a **hardmask** stack (a tougher film that takes the pattern from the resist and survives the real etch): **spin-on carbon (SOC)** plus a SiON (silicon oxynitride) or metal-oxide hardmask, the **tri-layer** stack of Module 07, because 25 nm of resist cannot survive a 100 nm dielectric etch.

The EUV resist process on the track (TEL's CLEAN TRACK LITHIUS Pro Z or SCREEN's equivalent, docked to the scanner), in order:

1. **Underlayer coat and bake.** A few nanometres of adhesion and secondary-electron layer are spun on over the hardmask stack and baked to cross-link.
2. **Resist coat.** ~20–35 nm of CAR (or ~15–25 nm of metal-oxide resist) is spun on; at this thickness the film is thinner than the wavelength of visible light.
3. **Soft bake.** Solvent is driven off on a hot plate so the film is a solid.
4. **Exposure.** The wafer travels through the load lock into vacuum and receives its 30–60 mJ/cm² in ~16 s (this module).
5. **Post-exposure bake.** For a CAR, the acid deprotects hundreds of sites per photon; for a metal-oxide resist the bake finishes the condensation of the exposed clusters.
6. **Develop.** TMAH for a CAR (exposed areas dissolve, positive tone) or an organic solvent for a metal-oxide resist (unexposed areas dissolve, negative tone).
7. **Rinse and dry.** Water rinse and spin dry; this is where capillary collapse happens if the lines are too tall.
8. **Inspect.** After-develop inspection (a **CD-SEM**, a scanning electron microscope that measures critical dimensions, for size and roughness; overlay; defect scan) and, if the wafer fails, strip and rework (Module 13).
9. **Transfer.** The pattern is etched into the hardmask and then into the film below (Module 09).

### Metal-oxide resists

The alternative platform is the **metal-oxide resist (MOR)**, pioneered by **Inpria** of Corvallis, Oregon (acquired by JSR in 2021 for ~$514 million). Inpria's resist is a solution of tin-oxo clusters, roughly 1 to 2 nm particles of butyl-tin oxide hydroxide, spin-coated to ~15 to 25 nm. Each cluster is a cage of about a dozen tin and oxygen atoms with four-carbon butyl groups on the outside that keep it dissolved in the coating solvent. A 92 eV photon and its secondary electrons strip the butyl groups off, the bare cages cross-link into a tin-oxide network, and that network no longer dissolves, so the exposed areas stay and the developer washes away the unexposed ones: a **negative-tone** process, developed in organic solvent. Tin's 13.5 nm absorption is among the highest of any element, so the film absorbs ~15 to 20 µm⁻¹, three to four times a CAR, and a 20 nm film captures ~30% of incident photons rather than ~10%. There is no acid diffusion step; the "pixel" is the cluster size. The result is lower LER at a given dose and a higher etch resistance (it is essentially SnO₂ after exposure) that lets the resist be very thin. The cost is a different track chemistry, sensitivity to ambient humidity and to delay between coat and exposure, and a defectivity learning curve that took most of a decade. Memory makers (Samsung and SK hynix are widely reported) qualified MOR for **DRAM** (dynamic random-access memory, Module 15) contact layers first; logic makers use it selectively for contacts and vias.

> **Worked example: absorbed photons, CAR versus MOR.** At 30 mJ/cm² about 20 photons arrive per nm². A 30 nm CAR at 5 µm⁻¹ absorbs 1 − e^(−0.15) ≈ 14% of them, ~3 photons per nm². A 20 nm metal-oxide resist at ~17.5 µm⁻¹ absorbs 1 − e^(−0.35) ≈ 30%, ~6 photons per nm². Same dose, same photons in the beam, twice as many photons actually deciding each edge, and √6/6 is smaller than √3/3. The MOR can therefore print the same feature with lower roughness at the same dose, or the same roughness at a lower dose, which is exactly the trade the RLS triangle says a CAR cannot make.

**Lam Research** took the metal-oxide idea one step further with a **dry resist**: the **organotin** (tin bonded to carbon groups) film is deposited from the vapour (the film grows from a gas in a chamber, like the **chemical vapour deposition (CVD)** and **atomic layer deposition (ALD)** processes of Module 06, rather than being spun from a liquid), exposed, and then developed with a dry plasma or thermal step rather than a solvent, all in vacuum-compatible chambers. Dry deposition gives a more uniform, thinner (sub-20 nm) film with no solvent and no **edge bead** (the thick rim a spun liquid leaves at the wafer edge), and better dose efficiency, and dry development avoids the capillary forces that collapse wet-developed lines. Lam's dry resist, branded **Aether**, was developed with imec and SK hynix (partnership announced 2022) and in January 2025 was selected as production **tool of record** (the tool a fab has qualified and committed to for a production step) for advanced DRAM by a leading memory maker (as of 2025); it is one of the leading candidates for High-NA, where the **depth of focus** (the range of focus over which the image is still sharp, worked out under High-NA) mandates films of 20 nm or less.

| | Chemically amplified (CAR) | Metal-oxide (MOR) | Dry resist |
|---|---|---|---|
| Film thickness | ~20–35 nm | ~15–25 nm | Sub-20 nm |
| Absorption at 13.5 nm | ~4–6 µm⁻¹ | ~15–20 µm⁻¹ | ~15–20 µm⁻¹ (organotin) |
| Tone | Positive (exposed areas dissolve) | Negative (exposed areas stay) | Negative |

> **What can go wrong:** Four failure modes recur. Pattern collapse: above an aspect ratio (height to width) of roughly 2–3, an approximate threshold that depends on the rinse and the resist, the rinse pulls lines over, seen on the CD-SEM as paired, leaning or missing lines, and fixed by a thinner film, a surfactant rinse (a wetting agent that lowers the water's surface tension), or dry development. Metal-oxide sensitivity to humidity and to the delay between coat and exposure, which shifts the **dose-to-size** (the dose that prints a feature at its target width) from lot to lot until the track's environment and queue times are locked down. Outgassing: a formulation that passes its witness-sample outgassing test in the lab but contaminates optics in production is a very expensive discovery, so qualification is conservative. And the defectivity learning curve: every new platform starts with particle and residue counts that only years of formulation and filtration work bring down, which is why fabs change resist platforms only when the stochastics leave them no choice.

## Resolution Limits and Multipatterning

Rayleigh's criterion from Module 07, HP = k1·λ/NA, where HP is the half-pitch, λ the wavelength, NA the numerical aperture and k1 a process factor for how cleverly the light is used, sets the single-exposure limit. k1 cannot go below 0.25 because at least two diffracted beams must fit inside the optics' cone, and at that floor the image has zero contrast margin.

> **Worked example: resolution and depth of focus at 0.33 NA.** With a practical k1 of ~0.32: HP = 0.32 × 13.5/0.33 ≈ 13.1 nm, a **minimum pitch of ~26 to 28 nm** for dense lines in a single exposure. At the theoretical floor, k1 = 0.25: HP = 0.25 × 13.5/0.33 ≈ 10.2 nm, but at that k1 the contrast and the stochastics make yield impossible. The **depth of focus (DOF)**, the range of focus over which the image is still sharp, is ≈ λ/NA² = 13.5/0.33² ≈ 124 nm, about the thickness of the wafer's own local height variation plus the resist, which is why the level sensor maps every wafer. For two-dimensional features such as contact and via arrays the practical single-exposure pitch is larger, ~30 to 36 nm.

This maps onto the nodes as follows (node names are generation labels, and the pitches and pass counts are reconstructions by analysts and by **TechInsights**, a reverse-engineering firm that buys finished chips, cuts them open and measures them, not foundry disclosures):

| Node | Year | Tightest metal pitch | Scheme | Approximate EUV passes per wafer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Samsung 7LPP, TSMC N7+ | 2019 | ~40 nm | Single EUV exposure replacing DUV LELE or LELELE on a few layers | A handful |
| TSMC N5 | 2020 | ~28–30 nm | Single EUV exposure on every EUV layer | ~14 |
| TSMC N3 | ~2023 | ~23–24 nm | EUV LELE or SALELE on the two or three tightest metal layers | ~20 (estimate) |
| TSMC N2, Intel 18A | ~2025–2026 (as of mid-2026) | Below N3, not disclosed | EUV double patterning on more layers | Mid-20s |

Below the ~26 nm single-exposure pitch, TSMC began **EUV double patterning** at N3. **EUV LELE** (litho-etch-litho-etch), in order:

1. **First exposure.** Print every second line at a relaxed pitch of 2p, which a single 0.33 NA exposure can resolve.
2. **First etch.** Transfer those lines into the hardmask so they survive the next coat.
3. **Recoat.** Coat a fresh resist over the patterned hardmask.
4. **Second exposure.** Print the other half of the lines, shifted by p, overlaid on the first set.
5. **Second etch.** Transfer, leaving lines at pitch p, each line's neighbours defined by the other exposure.

**SALELE** (self-aligned LELE) adds a spacer so the second exposure does not have to land as precisely. The idea is that once every first-exposure line is flanked by spacers of exactly controlled width, the only places left that can become lines are the gaps between the spacers, and those gaps are already the right width and in the right place; the second exposure merely chooses which gaps are used. In order:

1. **First exposure and etch.** Print and etch the first set of lines (call them the A lines) into the hardmask; they will serve as the **mandrel**, the pattern that the spacers are built around.
2. **Spacer.** Deposit a **conformal** film (equal thickness on every surface, including vertical sidewalls) by ALD over the mandrels, then etch it back anisotropically so that it is removed from the flat tops and floors but stays on the sidewalls: every A line is now flanked on both sides by a spacer whose width is the ALD thickness, controlled to a few tenths of a nanometre (Module 06).
3. **Second exposure.** Coat resist and print the second pattern (the B lines, or the cuts and blocks). The resist opening only has to expose the gap between two spacers; its edges land on top of the spacers, and anywhere on a spacer is fine, because the spacer, not the resist edge, will define where the etch stops. The second exposure's placement tolerance is therefore roughly half a spacer width, several nanometres, instead of a fraction of a nanometre.
4. **Etch and strip.** Etch the exposed gaps into the layer below (the B lines), then strip the spacers. The A–B spacing everywhere on the wafer is the spacer width, set by ALD, not by the overlay between two exposures.

> **Worked example: the space budget, LELE versus SALELE.** Take a 23 nm metal pitch: 11.5 nm lines and 11.5 nm spaces. In plain LELE the space between an A line and its B neighbour is 11.5 nm minus whatever the two exposures are misaligned by, minus half of each line's width error. Overlay of 1.8 nm (3σ) and line-width errors of ±1 nm (3σ) per line, i.e. ±0.5 nm per edge (illustrative but typical values), combine as the square root of the sum of the squares: √(1.8² + 0.5² + 0.5²) ≈ 1.9 nm (3σ). So the space is 11.5 ± 1.9 nm, a ±17% swing, and the narrowest spaces on the wafer are ~9.6 nm: that is where line-to-line leakage and dielectric breakdown (Module 12) start, and the same swing appears in the capacitance between neighbouring wires. In SALELE the A–B space is the spacer width, and ALD sets that to within a few tenths of a nanometre across the wafer, so the space budget drops from ~1.9 nm to a few tenths. The overlay error of the second exposure moves to where the resist edge lands on the spacer, and an 11.5 nm spacer gives it about ±5 nm of room against ~2 nm of overlay. What SALELE does not remove is line-width variation: the A width comes from the first exposure and the B width from the gap between spacers, which inherits the A width error with the opposite sign. And it adds an ALD deposition, a spacer etch-back and a spacer strip, each with its own cost and defects.

> **What can go wrong:** In plain LELE the space between a line from exposure A and a line from exposure B depends directly on the overlay between two EUV exposures, ~1.5–2 nm, which is a large fraction of the ~11–12 nm space in a 23 nm pitch, as the worked example shows; a lot that drifts by a nanometre has shorts on one side and opens on the other. SALELE removes the spacing from the overlay budget but adds deposition and etch steps and their own defectivity: a spacer that is too thin by a few tenths of a nanometre narrows every space on the wafer at once, and a spacer etch-back that leaves residue at the foot of a mandrel narrows or breaks the B line beside it, a defect that repeats wherever the residue does. Either way, every extra EUV pass costs ~$100 or more and about a day of cycle time, and the cost is paid on the two or three most expensive layers of the chip.

> **Why it matters downstream:** EUV multipatterning is exactly the cost spiral that EUV was supposed to end. If N2-class chips need double-patterned EUV on a growing number of layers, then a tool that prints those layers in one pass, even at $380 million, can pay for itself, and that is the entire demand case for High-NA in the next section.

## High-NA and Hyper-NA

### The EXE platform

The **TWINSCAN EXE:5000** (research and development tool, first shipped to Intel in December 2023 in 250 crates and completed in April 2024 at Intel's Hillsboro, Oregon D1X fab, with a second in the joint ASML-imec High-NA lab in Veldhoven) and the **EXE:5200B** (the high-volume model, first shipped to Intel in April 2025 and accepted at Intel's Oregon site in December 2025 ahead of Intel's 14A node, with TSMC, Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron following, as of mid-2026) raise the numerical aperture from 0.33 to **0.55**, a half-angle of 33° at the wafer instead of 19°. By Rayleigh that lowers the single-exposure half-pitch from ~13 nm to ~8 nm (ASML quotes 8 nm resolution), which is enough to return the N2/A16-class metal layers to single exposure and to extend to the 14A / A10 generations.

Raising NA on a reflective system with an angled reticle creates a geometric problem, and the numbers show why the solution had to be unusual.

> **Worked example: why the optics are anamorphic.** Reticle-side NA is wafer-side NA divided by the reduction ratio, so the half-angle of the cone at the reticle is asin(NA/reduction). At 4× and NA 0.55 that is asin(0.55/4) = asin(0.1375) ≈ 7.9°, larger than the 6° chief ray angle: the incoming and outgoing cones would overlap, part of the reflected light would go back into the illuminator, and the absorber shadowing at those angles would be intolerable. At 8× it is asin(0.55/8) ≈ 3.9°, comfortably inside 6°. The costs of the higher NA follow from the same two equations as before. Half-pitch: 0.32 × 13.5/0.55 ≈ 7.9 nm. Depth of focus: 13.5/0.55² ≈ 45 nm, down from ~124 nm at 0.33, a factor of 2.8. And field size: 8× reduction in both directions would shrink the printable field from 26 × 33 mm to 13 × 16.5 mm, a quarter of the area; 8× in one direction only halves it, to 26 × 16.5 mm.

The straightforward fix, 8× reduction, would quarter the field area and quarter throughput. Zeiss and ASML instead made the optics anamorphic, as previewed in the reticle section: **8× reduction in the scan direction and 4× in the cross-scan direction**, a different magnification in the two directions. In the direction where the angular problem is worst, the reticle-side cone is halved; in the other direction the field size is preserved. The same 6-inch reticle now prints a **half-field of 26 mm × 16.5 mm** at the wafer, half the 26 × 33 mm of every scanner since the 1990s, and the pattern on the reticle is drawn stretched, 8× in one axis and 4× in the other.

A half-field is fine for a memory die or a mobile **system-on-chip (SoC)**, the single chip at the heart of a phone. It is a real problem for a reticle-limited die like an NVIDIA GPU or a large data-centre CPU, which fills the full 26 × 33 mm (Module 19's ~800 mm² dies). Those designs must either be split into two **chiplets** (separately made dies wired together in one package, Module 17) at the half-field boundary or **stitched**: printed as two half-field exposures whose patterns cross the boundary with sub-nanometre overlay, which ASML and the foundries have demonstrated but which adds overlay budget and edge-placement error exactly where a design would prefer none. The industry's move to multi-die packages (Module 17) makes this less painful than it would have been in 2015.

The rest of the EXE is scaled accordingly. The projection optics have larger mirrors, up to about a metre across, with a central **obscuration** in the pupil: as in a reflecting telescope, one mirror sits in the light path and blocks the centre of the cone, so the pupil is a ring with a hole in it, which simplifies the mirror geometry but means OPC has to allow for the missing central angles. The reticle stage moves at 8× the wafer speed in scan, so its accelerations are the highest in the machine. The 45 nm depth of focus drives resist thickness to ~20 nm or less (hence toward MORs and dry resist) and tightens flatness, chuck, and focus specs. The 8 nm half-pitch features are close to the secondary-electron blur, which is why High-NA resist development is the pacing item. Throughput needs care to read, because ASML quotes the two models at different reference doses: the EXE:5000 at more than 185 wph at 20 mJ/cm², and the EXE:5200B, the production tool, at 175 wph at 50 mJ/cm² (ASML's product page states the 50 mJ/cm² condition), a dose 2.5 times higher; at equal dose the EXE:5200B is the faster machine by a wide margin, and ASML targets 220 wph for it. The half-field is the reason High-NA throughput is quoted at doses and figures that look modest: every wafer needs twice as many field scans and turnarounds as on an NXE, which is exactly why the EXE stages accelerate at 8 g and 32 g. The EXE:5200B sells for on the order of €350 million (~$380 million) and needs higher cleanroom ceilings and a heavier floor. ASML shipped its first ten or so High-NA systems through 2024–2025 and planned around ten in 2027 (as scheduled in 2026).

Intel's strategy is to use High-NA in earnest at 14A (**risk production**, the first low-volume runs of a new node, ~2027), where it expects replacing EUV double patterning with single exposure to pay for itself; as a first step, ASML and Intel announced in July 2026 that certain Intel 18A layers (Core Ultra Series 3, "Panther Lake") had been dual-qualified (qualified to run on either the NXE or the EXE tools) on the EXE platform and were running in **high-volume manufacturing (HVM)** on High-NA with yields matched to the NXE tools (as of mid-2026). TSMC has said it will not use High-NA at A16, has said A14 will not need it, and is evaluating later nodes (its plans beyond that are not public), betting that low-NA multipatterning is cheaper for a few more years (as of mid-2026). Which is right depends on how many layers are double-patterned and on the resist stochastics at 8 nm.

> **What can go wrong:** Four High-NA-specific problems. Stitching: two half-field exposures that share a boundary must overlay to a fraction of a nanometre along that seam, and any error there is a systematic defect line down the middle of every die, caught by overlay metrology on stitch marks. Secondary-electron blur: at 8 nm half-pitch the ~2–4 nm blur is a large fraction of the line, so the resist, not the optics, sets the achievable roughness. Obscuration: the hole in the pupil removes the central illumination angles, so pupil shapes and OPC models built for the NXE do not transfer and every layer's illumination has to be re-optimized. And the building: the tool is heavier and taller than an NXE, so a fab designed in 2018 may need a reinforced floor and a higher ceiling before the first crate arrives.

### Hyper-NA

Hyper-NA is a side note in this module: it is a design study, not a machine, so one paragraph is all it gets. ASML and Zeiss have discussed a further step to **NA 0.75**, "hyper-NA," on a roughly 2030 horizon. At that NA the mirrors approach two metres, and polarization becomes a problem. Light's electric field oscillates across its direction of travel; at large angles the fields of two interfering beams whose oscillation lies in the plane of incidence (**p-polarized**) are nearly perpendicular to each other and can neither cancel nor reinforce, so the fringes wash out unless only the other orientation (**s-polarized**, field perpendicular to the plane of incidence) is used, which throws away half the light. The reticle-side angular problem also returns even with anamorphic optics. Whether it is built depends on whether the cost per transistor still improves; as of mid-2026 ASML has framed hyper-NA as an option to be decided in the next year or two, not a commitment.

An NXE:3800E contains on the order of 100,000 parts from ~5,000 suppliers, weighs ~180 tonnes, and ships in about 40 freight containers on ~20 trucks or three fully loaded Boeing 747s. It is assembled and tested in Veldhoven, Netherlands, then disassembled into modules for shipment and reassembled at the customer. Installation and qualification of a low-NA tool takes months; the first EXE:5000 at Intel took about six months from first crate (December 2023) to first light (April 2024).

## Who Has EUV, and Who Is Not Allowed To

EUV has never shipped to China. SMIC ordered an NXE:3400 in 2018 and the Dutch government, under US pressure, never issued the licence; EUV has been formally export-controlled since 2019. The US October 2022 and 2023 rules and the Dutch September 2023 (NXT:2000i and above) and September 2024 (NXT:1970i/1980i) rules then restricted the immersion DUV tools that would be used to multi-pattern around the lack of EUV. SMIC nonetheless produced 7 nm-class logic (Huawei Kirin 9000S, 2023) and "5 nm-class" parts by DUV quadruple patterning (the SAQP of the Before-you-start list), at lower yield and higher cost.

China's domestic response: **SMEE** (Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment) is the DUV champion, with 90 nm tools in production and a 28 nm-class immersion tool (SSA800) that has been "about to ship" for years. On EUV, the most public program is the **laser-induced discharge plasma (LDP)** source at Harbin Institute of Technology under Zhao Yongpeng, in which a laser vaporizes tin from an electrode and a high-voltage discharge through the vapour forms the plasma; reported in-band power was ~100 to 150 W in 2025 (as reported, 2025), versus ASML's 500 to 600 W, and discharge sources are hard to scale (Ushio's XTREME group, which took over Philips' LDP program in 2010, reached ~30 W at the intermediate focus in 2011 and gave up on high-volume manufacturing sources a few years later). A prototype scanner, reportedly assembled with former ASML engineers, was said to be under test in 2025 with a target of ~2028 (as reported, 2025). Tsinghua's **steady-state microbunching (SSMB)** source is a longer-shot alternative: it starts from a synchrotron, the ring accelerator introduced under the Mo/Si mirror section, whose circulating electrons normally emit light incoherently and spread across the spectrum, and SSMB would make the electrons bunch so that they emit coherently at 13.5 nm, an approach still on paper at wafer-scale power. As of 2026 no Chinese EUV tool has printed a production wafer; the source gap is years, and the Zeiss-class optics are the harder problem behind it.

The 2010s were a decade of delays. The 2008 roadmaps had EUV in production at 22 nm in 2012; the source delivered a tenth of the promised power with poor availability, and mask blank defects and resist stochastics were unsolved. ASML's response was financial and structural: buy the source company, have the three biggest customers pay for the R&D through the 2012 co-investment, buy into the optics supplier, and grind through the collector, droplet-generator and availability problems one at a time until 250 W at 90% availability arrived in 2017–2018. From first image to first product took 33 years.

## Summary

- EUV exists to collapse three or four DUV passes into one by using 13.5 nm light, 14 times shorter than 193 nm, on the ~15–25 layers per wafer where 193 nm cannot print the pitch in a single exposure.
- 13.5 nm is set by the Mo/Si multilayer mirror (~40–50 bilayers at ~6.9–7.0 nm, ~70% reflectance, ~2% system passband), not by the source; highly charged tin ions happen to radiate there.
- Nothing transmits EUV, so the machine is all-reflective and in vacuum; ~11 reflections at ~67% each leave ~1–2% of the in-band light, so every mirror is argued over.
- The light comes from 27 µm tin droplets, 50,000 a second, flattened by a pre-pulse and heated to ~30–40 eV by a 25–40 kW CO₂ laser; conversion efficiency ~5–6%; 250 → 600 W at the intermediate focus, 1,000 W demonstrated in 2026.
- Hydrogen at ~100 Pa slows the tin ions and etches tin off the collector as SnH₄; debris and availability, not resolution, delayed EUV by a decade.
- Zeiss's six-mirror 0.33 NA optics are figured to ~50 pm RMS; every mirror is a 30% tax, so every design decision is a light budget.
- The wafer is scanned through a fixed 26 × 2–3 mm slit at ~0.6–0.8 m/s (reticle at 4×, on stages accelerating at several g), so each point's dose is a sum over ~150–250 pulses; of the ~16 s per wafer only ~4 s is exposure, and past ~500 W the turnarounds and wafer exchange, not the source, set throughput.
- The reticle is a mirror lit at 6°, so its 60–70 nm absorber casts shadows (~1.6 nm at wafer scale) and shifts patterns through focus; OPC and thinner absorbers fight this, and anamorphic High-NA optics exist because of it.
- At 30 mJ/cm² only ~20 photons/nm² arrive and ~3 are absorbed in a CAR; √N counting noise plus secondary-electron and chemical randomness produce stochastic defects, and doses ended up 2–4× higher than planned.
- Resists moved thinner (20–35 nm), higher-absorbing (metal-oxide, ~15–20 µm⁻¹) and toward dry deposition; RLS and Z = R³L²S say you cannot win resolution, roughness and dose all at once.
- 0.33 NA prints ~26–28 nm pitch in one shot; N3 and N2 already double-pattern with EUV, which is the case for High-NA.
- High-NA (0.55) reaches ~8 nm half-pitch with anamorphic 4×/8× optics and a 26 × 16.5 mm half-field; ~$380 million per tool, at Intel since 2024, in HVM on some 18A layers as of mid-2026.
- One supplier chain (ASML, Zeiss, Trumpf, Cymer, Hoya/AGC, Lasertec, Mitsui, JSR/TOK), ~350 tools, none in China; an EUV pass costs ~$100–150 all-in and a leading wafer takes 15–25 of them.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"EUV is just a shorter-wavelength laser, like KrF to ArF."** → There is no EUV laser. The light comes from a tin plasma at ~500,000 K created by a CO₂ laser 785 times longer in wavelength; the machine is an all-reflective vacuum system with no lenses, no transmissive mask, and no air.
- **"13.5 nm was chosen because it is the shortest wavelength that could be produced."** → It was chosen because Mo/Si multilayer mirrors have their reflectance peak there. Shorter wavelengths (6.7 nm with La/B mirrors) have been studied; the mirror and the tin emission band, not the source alone, fix the wavelength.
- **"More source power means a smaller feature."** → Source power sets throughput at a given dose, not resolution. Resolution comes from wavelength and NA; power buys the ability to run higher doses (which reduce stochastic defects) without losing wafers per hour.
- **"EUV resolution problems are optical, like DUV."** → The dominant EUV yield limiter is stochastic: with only ~20 photons per nm² incident at 30 mJ/cm², and a few absorbed, photon counting noise plus secondary-electron and chemical randomness create missing contacts and line breaks with no deterministic cause.
- **"EUV ended multi-patterning."** → It ended it for one node. At N3 and N2, the tightest metal layers are below the ~26 nm single-exposure pitch of 0.33 NA and are double-patterned with EUV LELE or SALELE. High-NA exists to end it again.
- **"High-NA just prints smaller; everything else is the same."** → The optics are anamorphic (4× / 8×) to keep the reticle-side angles workable, which halves the field to 26 × 16.5 mm. Reticle-limited dies such as large GPUs must be stitched or split into chiplets.
- **"The hydrogen in the tool is there to make a better vacuum."** → It is a deliberately added gas at ~100 Pa in the source and a few Pa in the scanner: it slows tin ions before they sputter the collector, its radicals turn tin and carbon deposits into gases the pumps remove, and it costs only ~10% of the light per metre at 100 Pa.
- **"China has, or is about to have, its own EUV."** → As of 2026 the strongest Chinese source (Harbin LDP) is reported at ~100–150 W versus ASML's 500–600 W in production and 1,000 W demonstrated; no Chinese tool has printed a production wafer, and the Zeiss-class optics are the larger unsolved problem.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 07 delivered the resist chemistry, the track, and the DUV scanner that still exposes the majority of layers on any wafer; this module is the small set of critical layers (typically 15 to 25 of the ~80 to 100 lithography passes on an N3/N2 wafer, at ~$100–150 per EUV pass all-in) where only 13.5 nm light can print the pattern in one or two exposures. The inputs consumed here are the photomask blanks and finished EUV reticles from Module 04 (Hoya and AGC blanks, written and inspected in the foundry's mask shop with Lasertec actinic tools), EUV resists and underlayers from JSR, TOK, Shin-Etsu, Fujifilm, and Inpria/Lam, ultra-high-purity hydrogen, tin, and the ~1 MW of power and vacuum infrastructure in the sub-fab; the machine itself is the product of Zeiss, Trumpf, Cymer, VDL, and ~5,000 other suppliers integrated in Veldhoven. The output is a patterned resist layer, ~25 nm thick, with features at 13 nm half-pitch (8 nm with High-NA), that immediately goes to Module 09 for etch transfer into the hardmask and the underlying film, and whose CD, overlay, and stochastic defect populations are what Module 13's metrology and inspection tools spend most of their time measuring.
