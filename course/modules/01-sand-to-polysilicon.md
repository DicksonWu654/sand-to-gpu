# Module 01: Sand to Polysilicon

This stage has a clear goal: take silicon dioxide, the most abundant compound in the Earth's crust, and turn it into elemental silicon in which fewer than one atom in a billion is anything other than silicon.

The amount of silicon is not the difficulty. The difficulty is controlling which foreign atoms remain among its atoms, because different impurities change electrical behaviour in different ways. A **dopant** is an impurity added on purpose: boron has one electron fewer than silicon and makes **p-type** silicon, in which current is carried by mobile electron vacancies called holes; phosphorus has one electron more and makes **n-type** silicon, in which current is carried by the spare electrons. A transistor uses carefully arranged regions of semiconductor to control current, so the wafer maker introduces a controlled dopant concentration of perhaps 10^15 atoms per cm³ and needs any residual, uncontrolled boron and phosphorus to be at least a thousand times lower than that. Boron and phosphorus are dangerous precisely because they are dopants: a stray one changes the switch's behaviour.

Metals cause a different problem: an atom of iron, copper, or nickel lodged in the lattice acts as a trap that swallows the free electrons the switch relies on, or as a short through the **gate oxide**, the insulating layer only a few atoms thick that separates the silicon from the transistor's control electrode (its **gate**, the terminal whose voltage turns the switch on and off).

The product of this stage is **polycrystalline silicon (polysilicon)**: a solid made of millions of small silicon crystals in random orientations, which is what you get when silicon deposits everywhere at once on a hot rod. A wafer needs one continuous crystal; turning polysilicon into that is Module 02's job.

Nature does not hand out silicon in that condition. The Si-O bond is one of the strongest in geochemistry (~450 kJ/mol per bond, stronger than the carbon-carbon single bond that holds a hydrocarbon chain together (~350 kJ/mol) and a little stronger than a C-H bond (~410 kJ/mol), and every silicon in quartz has four of them), which is why quartz survives billions of years of weathering, and it is why breaking it costs a submerged-arc furnace (an electric furnace, Section 3) running at ~2,000 °C and roughly 12 MWh of electricity per tonne. The silicon that comes out of that furnace is ~98–99% pure, which is fine for aluminum alloys and silicones and hopeless for a wafer (Section 6 defines the grades). Going from 99% to 99.9999999% (nine nines, or **9N**) is a chemistry problem, not a metallurgy problem: convert the silicon into a volatile liquid, distill the liquid until the contaminants are at parts-per-trillion levels, and then decompose the liquid back into solid silicon inside a sealed reactor at ~1,100 °C. That is the **Siemens process**, invented at Siemens in the late 1950s and still, seven decades later, the way essentially all electronic-grade silicon is made.

This module walks the full chain: the rock, the furnace, the chlorosilane chemistry, the deposition reactor, the competing fluidized-bed route, how purity is actually measured, how **chunks** (the fist-sized broken pieces of rod in which polysilicon ships) get to a crystal grower, who makes what, and what it costs. It closes with the two other ultra-pure feedstocks that enter the supply chain at this same stage and that the crystal grower in Module 02 cannot work without: the fused-quartz crucible and the graphite hot zone.

## Before you start

- **Atoms, moles and Avogadro's number.** One mole is 6.022 × 10^23 particles, and an element's atomic mass in grams is one mole of its atoms, so a solid's density tells you how many atoms sit in a cubic centimetre; this module counts impurities that way.
- **Parts-per notation.** ppm, ppb and ppt are parts per million, billion and trillion. Counting by weight (ppmw, ppbw) and by atoms (ppma, ppba, ppta) give different numbers for the same sample, because atoms of different elements weigh different amounts.
- **Crystal, polycrystal, glass.** A **single crystal** is one continuous, ordered lattice; a **polycrystal** is many small crystals (grains) in random orientations; a **glass** (an amorphous solid) has no long-range order. This module makes polycrystals and uses glass (fused quartz) to hold molten silicon; Module 02 makes single crystals.
- **Dopants and resistivity.** Boron (one electron fewer than silicon) makes p-type silicon; phosphorus (one more) makes n-type. More dopant means more mobile charge carriers and lower **resistivity**, measured in ohm-centimetres (Ω·cm). Module 10 goes deep.
- **Heat of reaction.** A reaction's enthalpy change ΔH is the heat it absorbs (positive) or releases (negative). Its entropy change ΔS describes the change in how widely matter and energy can be distributed; producing gas often increases it. The balance is ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, where T is absolute temperature in kelvin and ΔG is the Gibbs free-energy change. A negative ΔG says the reaction is thermodynamically favoured under the stated conditions. It does not say how fast it happens; temperature must also make the reaction fast enough to be useful.
- **Boiling points and distillation.** Liquids with different boiling points can be separated by boiling and condensing, because the vapour is richer in the lower-boiling component. Doing that once is school chemistry; this module explains how a column does it a hundred times over.
- **Thermal radiation.** Every hot surface radiates power proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature (the Stefan-Boltzmann law, Section 4.3). At 1,100 °C this dwarfs every other heat loss.
- **Units.** 1 MWh = 1,000 kWh; 1 bar is roughly atmospheric pressure; 1 tonne (t) = 1,000 kg, 1 kt = 1,000 t, 1 Mt = a million tonnes; 1 µm is a thousandth of a millimetre. Temperatures are in °C except where a T⁴ law needs kelvin (K = °C + 273).
- **The chain (Module 00).** Quartz → metallurgical silicon → polysilicon → single crystal (an **ingot**, a cylindrical log of one crystal) → **wafer** (a thin disc sliced from it) → fab. This module covers the first two arrows, plus the crucible and graphite the third arrow needs.

## 1. Why Purity Is the Whole Game

Crystalline silicon contains **5.0 × 10^22 atoms per cm³** (density 2.33 g/cm³, atomic mass 28.09 g/mol; 2.33 / 28.09 × 6.022 × 10^23 = 4.99 × 10^22). A cubic centimetre is a sugar cube, and one part per billion of that sugar cube is still fifty trillion atoms. That number is the yardstick for everything in this module, so it is worth internalizing.

Purity for semiconductor silicon is usually quoted in "nines": 6N is 99.9999% (1 part per million impurity), 9N is 99.9999999% (1 part per billion), 11N is 99.999999999% (10 parts per trillion). Because the interesting impurities are counted as atoms, the industry uses **ppma** (parts per million atomic), **ppba** (parts per billion atomic) and **ppta** (parts per trillion atomic) for dopants, and **ppbw** (parts per billion by weight) for metals, which are usually measured by **mass spectrometry** (an instrument that turns the atoms in a sample into charged ions and sorts them by mass, so that each element is counted separately; Section 7 describes the version the industry uses) after dissolving the sample. The conversion matters: 1 ppbw of iron (atomic mass 56) is 0.5 ppba, because an iron atom weighs twice a silicon atom.

Three more definitions before the arithmetic. **Resistivity**, in ohm-centimetres (Ω·cm), is how hard it is to push current through a 1 cm cube of the material; more dopant atoms mean more carriers and lower resistivity, so wafer makers quote dopant content as a resistivity rather than as a count. **Czochralski (CZ)** growth pulls a single crystal out of a melt held in a crucible; **float-zone (FZ)** growth melts a rod one short zone at a time, using a **radio-frequency (RF) induction coil** and no crucible at all, so nothing but the feedstock's own impurities enters the crystal (Module 02 covers both). The coil is a water-cooled copper ring, a few turns wide, carrying alternating current at ~MHz frequency; its rapidly reversing magnetic field induces circulating currents in the silicon inside the ring, and those currents heat the silicon by its own resistance, so the coil melts a slice of the rod without ever touching it, like an induction hob heating a pan. And an impurity is **electrically active** if it dopes: boron, aluminum, gallium (one column to the left of silicon in the periodic table) each remove one electron, and phosphorus, arsenic, antimony (one column to the right) each contribute one, so every one of their atoms changes the resistivity. Iron and copper do not dope; they trap.

> **Worked example: what 9N means in atoms.**
> Silicon has 5 × 10^22 atoms per cm³. A total impurity content of 1 ppb (9N) means 5 × 10^22 × 10^-9 = **5 × 10^13 impurity atoms per cm³**. That sounds like a lot of atoms (fifty trillion in a sugar-cube volume), but compare it to the dopants a wafer maker adds on purpose. A standard p-type 300 mm CZ substrate (the wafer as a physical base for transistors) at ~10 Ω·cm has about 1.3 × 10^15 boron atoms per cm³ (about 27 ppba; the conversion from resistivity to atom count comes from the standard published resistivity-to-dopant curves, SEMI MF723, and Section 7 explains how the resistivity itself is measured). So a 9N feedstock carries roughly 25× fewer total foreign atoms than the deliberate doping level, and the fraction that is electrically active (B, P, Al, As, Sb) is far lower still. The electronic-grade specification for boron is typically ≤ ~0.02–0.05 ppba, i.e. ≤ 1–2.5 × 10^12 atoms per cm³, a thousand times below the intentional dopant level. At the other end, a 6N solar feedstock whose entire 1 ppm impurity budget were boron (a worst case; real 6N silicon carries a mix of impurities, but it makes the scale clear) would contain 5 × 10^16 boron atoms per cm³, giving a resistivity of ~0.3–0.5 Ω·cm before the grower adds anything at all; that is why 6N is a solar number and not an electronics number. And a high-resistivity float-zone wafer for radio-frequency or power devices at 5,000 Ω·cm needs net electrically active impurities below ~3 × 10^12 atoms per cm³, which is only reachable because the feedstock's boron and phosphorus are each at the 10^11–10^12 atoms per cm³ level.

> **Intuition:** sea ice is fresher than the sea it froze from, because the orderly ice lattice has no room for salt ions and pushes them back into the water. Freezing silicon does the same thing. A misfit atom such as iron or copper, the wrong size and with the wrong bonding habits, is shoved into the liquid as the crystal front advances. Boron is different: it is small, it bonds to four neighbours the way silicon does, and it fits into a silicon lattice site almost comfortably, so the crystal accepts most of it. The analogy breaks only in degree: sea ice rejects salt by a factor of a few, while silicon rejects iron by a factor of a hundred thousand.

That picture is quantified by the **segregation coefficient** k of each impurity: the ratio of its concentration in the freshly frozen solid to its concentration in the liquid it froze from. Iron has k ≈ 8 × 10^-6, aluminum ~2 × 10^-3, copper ~4 × 10^-4: crystal growth rejects them into the melt almost completely, so a polysilicon feedstock can tolerate a little metal that the grower will clean up. Carbon has k ≈ 0.07, so a tenth of it stays. Oxygen is a special case: the feedstock carries almost none, and the crucible adds it during growth (Section 10 and Module 02). Boron has k ≈ 0.8 and phosphorus k ≈ 0.35. Boron in particular goes straight into the crystal. It cannot be removed by crystal growth, by **zone refining** (sweeping a molten zone along a rod so that impurities that prefer the liquid ride along to the end), or by any metallurgical trick with reasonable yield. It has to be removed at the chemical stage, in the distillation columns, and that single fact shapes the design of the entire Siemens process.

> **Worked example: one freezing pass.**
> The rule is reusable: the first solid to freeze carries k times the melt concentration. For boron, k ≈ 0.8, so the crystal keeps ~80% of what was in the melt (and as the melt shrinks and its boron concentrates, later parts of the crystal carry even more). For iron, k ≈ 10^-5, so the crystal keeps one part in a hundred thousand: a melt at 1 ppm of iron gives a crystal at ~10 ppt. One pass of crystal growth is a superb iron filter and a useless boron filter.

> **Why it matters downstream:** the boron in the feedstock sets a floor under everything grown from it. A float-zone wafer for a radio-frequency switch or a high-voltage power device needs several thousand Ω·cm, which means boron and phosphorus at the 10^11–10^12 atoms per cm³ level, and downstream steps are not a substitute for a qualified low-impurity feedstock. Crystal segregation can redistribute impurities, and zone refining can remove some; neither makes feedstock purity irrelevant.

## 2. The Rock: Quartz and Quartzite

### 2.1 Why not beach sand

Start with why the furnace cares about the shape of its rock. The reaction in Section 3 strips both oxygens off every silicon atom and turns them into two molecules of carbon monoxide gas, and that gas has to escape upward through a pile of rock and coal several metres deep (the **charge**, the batch of raw materials loaded into the furnace, which sits as a **packed bed**, a heap of lumps with gas-filled gaps between them). If the pile is not porous, the gas does not leave quietly. The "sand to chip" phrase is a nice story and it is essentially wrong. Beach and desert sand is a poor feedstock for three reasons, all of them mechanistic.

First, grain size. Fine sand (0.1–1 mm) packs into an impermeable mass. The trapped gas then either **channels** (finds one path and blasts through it, leaving the rest of the pile unreacted) or **blows** (a pocket of trapped gas erupts through the surface), and the furnace becomes unstable and dangerous. Furnaces need lumpy material, typically **10–150 mm** pieces, with fines screened out.

Second, chemistry. Beach sand contains shell fragments (CaCO₃), heavy minerals (ilmenite FeTiO₃, zircon, monazite, magnetite), feldspar (Al, Na, K), and clay. Iron, aluminum, titanium, calcium, and especially boron and phosphorus (from apatite, tourmaline, and organic residues) all end up in the silicon. Boron and phosphorus are the ones the downstream chemistry hates most.

Third, thermal behavior. At 573 °C the atoms in quartz snap from one arrangement (α-quartz) into a slightly different one (β-quartz), with an abrupt volume jump of roughly 1%; a lump heated quickly cracks along the front where that jump is happening. Some quartz **decrepitates** (shatters into fines) on rapid heating through that transition and again near its ~1,700 °C softening point, which recreates the permeability problem inside the furnace. Producers test candidate ore for **thermal strength** and pick deposits that stay lumpy.

So the furnace feed is **lump quartz** or **quartzite** (a metamorphosed quartz sandstone) mined from hard-rock quarries, crushed and screened to size, and specified at roughly ≥ 98–99.5% SiO₂, with Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃ each in the 0.05–0.5% range, and B and P in the low-ppm range. Major sources are Norway, Spain, France, Brazil, Canada, Australia, South Africa, Turkey, and many quarries in China. A silicon smelter uses ~2.5–2.7 tonnes of quartz per tonne of silicon. The theoretical figure is 2.14: SiO₂ weighs 60.08 g/mol and silicon 28.09 g/mol, and 60.08 / 28.09 = 2.14. The rest is lost as silica fume (Section 3.1) and **slag**, a molten oxide layer that floats on liquid metal and carries away the oxides you want gone.

> **Worked example: how much boron a quarry delivers.**
> Suppose a quartz lot carries 10 ppm of boron by weight, at the top of the "low-ppm" specification. At 2.5–2.7 t of quartz per tonne of silicon, that is ~25–27 g of boron per tonne of silicon. If all of it stayed, the silicon would contain ~25 ppmw of boron, squarely inside the 5–40 ppmw measured in real metallurgical silicon (Section 3.3); in practice some boron leaves with the fume and the coal ash adds some back. The point stands: whatever boron the quarry delivers, the chlorosilane plant in Section 4 must remove, and nothing in the furnace will do it for you.

### 2.2 High-purity quartz: a different material for a different job

There is a second quartz stream in the supply chain, and it is much rarer, much more expensive, and much more geographically concentrated. **High-purity quartz (HPQ)** is quartz with total impurities below roughly 50 ppm, in the best grades below ~10 ppm, with particular attention to aluminum, titanium, and the alkalis (Na, K, Li). These sit inside the quartz lattice as **substitutional** ions (taking a silicon atom's place) or **interstitial** ions (squeezed into a gap between atoms), and cannot be washed off. HPQ is not fed to furnaces. It is the raw material for the **fused-quartz crucibles** in which single crystals are grown (Section 10 and Module 02), for quartz furnace tubes and wafer boats, for lamp envelopes, and for the silica used in some optical and telecom applications. A crucible cares about those particular impurities for a concrete reason: alkalis make the glass flow and crystallize at melt temperature, aluminum and titanium dissolve into the melt and end up in the crystal, and any of them weakens the bubble-free inner layer that keeps the crucible from shedding particles (Section 10.1).

The world's benchmark deposit is the **Spruce Pine** pegmatite district in Mitchell County, North Carolina. A **pegmatite** is a very coarse-grained igneous rock; this one is an alaskite (a feldspar-quartz-mica granite) in which the quartz crystallized unusually clean of trace elements, and which, crucially, can be liberated from the feldspar and mica by crushing, froth flotation, magnetic separation, and acid leaching. Two companies mine it: **Sibelco** (Belgian, whose Spruce Pine operation descends from Unimin and sells the IOTA family of grades; IOTA-STD is on the order of 20 ppm total impurities, ~14 ppm of it aluminum, and the premium grades, IOTA-4 and above and the crucible-optimized IOTA-CG, bring the total down to roughly 8–10 ppm, with aluminum at ~8 ppm, the floor the geology sets; Section 10.1 and the Key Numbers table reuse these figures) and **The Quartz Corp** (a joint venture of Norway's Norsk Mineral and France's Imerys), which mines at Spruce Pine and does its final purification at Drag in northern Norway. Between them they supply the large majority of the world's crucible-grade quartz, and in the tightest grades effectively all of it. Other HPQ sources exist but are second-tier or politically awkward: Kyshtym in Russia's Urals (sanctions-affected), deposits in India, Brazil, Australia, Mauritania, and China's Donghai county in Jiangsu, home of Jiangsu Pacific Quartz, the Chinese leader in both natural HPQ processing and synthetic quartz.

The purification flowsheet defines the price:

1. **Crush** the rock to sand-sized grains, so that each grain is mostly one mineral and the quartz can be separated from the feldspar and mica physically.
2. **Float**: **froth flotation** adds chemicals that make one mineral's surface water-repellent, so that air bubbles blown through the slurry carry it to the top; this rejects feldspar and mica.
3. **Magnetic separation** at high field strength pulls out iron-bearing minerals, which are weakly magnetic.
4. **Acid leach** in hot hydrochloric and hydrofluoric acid dissolves the impurities sitting on grain surfaces and along grain boundaries.
5. **Hot chlorination**, for the top grades only: the sand is heated to ~1,000–1,200 °C in chlorine or HCl gas, which converts alkalis near the lattice surface and some aluminum and iron into volatile chlorides that boil away.

What remains is dominated by aluminum substituting for silicon deep in the lattice, which no step removes; that is why the geology matters more than the plant.

### 2.3 The Hurricane Helene scare

A side note, because it is the episode most readers will have heard of. On 26–27 September 2024 Hurricane Helene dumped historic rainfall on western North Carolina, destroying Spruce Pine's roads, rail spur, power and water; both Sibelco and The Quartz Corp had halted operations ahead of the storm, and for about two weeks the press ran the headline that the world's chip supply depended on one flattened mountain town. Sibelco announced production and shipments restarting on 10 October 2024; The Quartz Corp resumed later, as the local infrastructure was rebuilt, without publishing a single restart date. Three things kept the scare from becoming a shortage: crucible and wafer makers hold months of HPQ inventory precisely because the source is concentrated, and quartz does not degrade in a warehouse; the mines themselves were intact, the damage being to the town and its transport links; and a crucible needs ultra-clean quartz only in its inner few millimetres, for which synthetic quartz (Section 10.1) is a qualified if pricier substitute. The lasting effect was to accelerate second-source qualification of Chinese synthetic quartz and alternative natural deposits; the episode is now the standard example of a single point of failure that is neither a chip nor a tool.

## 3. Carbothermic Reduction: The Submerged-Arc Furnace

### 3.1 The chemistry

The overall reaction is

SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO

with ΔH ≈ +690 kJ per mole of silicon. That number comes from **formation enthalpies**, the heat released when a compound forms from its elements: SiO₂ releases 911 kJ/mol when it forms, CO releases 110.5 kJ/mol, and the reaction's ΔH is the products' total minus the reactants', 2 × (−110.5) − (−911) = +690 kJ/mol. The positive sign means heat must be supplied: the reaction is strongly **endothermic**. It is electric rather than fuel-fired for a simple reason: no flame can hold the required temperature inside a bed of carbon that would consume the flame's oxygen.

> **Intuition:** picture a pit ~10 m across filled with quartz lumps and coal, with three carbon columns as thick as tree trunks plunged into it from above. Around each column tip the electric arc hollows out a gas-filled cavity (the **crater**) at ~2,000 °C, and the pile above the crater is cooler the higher you go. Gas rises, solids sink, so the furnace is a counter-current trap: half-finished product rising as gas must be caught by the carbon it meets on the way up and dragged back down as a solid. The half-finished product is **silicon monoxide, SiO(g)** (the "(g)" means gas), a molecule that exists only at these temperatures. Any that escapes the trap is lost.

> **Worked example: why 2,000 °C.**
> Whether a reaction goes is decided by the Gibbs free energy change ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, where ΔH is the heat absorbed (+690,000 J per mole of silicon here), T is the absolute temperature in kelvin, and ΔS is the entropy change, the gain in disorder. This reaction turns two solids into a solid plus two molecules of gas, and gas is far more disordered than solid. Using the standard-table entropies: the two moles of CO gas made carry 2 × 198 ≈ 395 J/K; subtract the ~53 J/K that the solids consumed had (quartz ~42 J/K per mole, plus two moles of carbon at ~6 J/K each); add the ~19 J/K of the mole of solid silicon made; net ΔS ≈ 395 − 53 + 19 ≈ **+360 J per mole of silicon per kelvin**. The reaction becomes favourable when TΔS exceeds ΔH, i.e. above T ≈ 690,000 / 360 ≈ 1,900 K, about 1,650 °C, for the overall equation. That is an estimated crossing using constant standard-state enthalpy and entropy, not a rigorous furnace-equilibrium temperature. The furnace runs hotter because the overall equation never happens in one step: the final step that actually makes liquid silicon, SiO(g) + SiC → 2Si + CO(g) below, needs the crater at or above ~1,800 °C to run at a useful rate, which is why the hot zone sits at ~1,900–2,000 °C and no hotter, since every degree of margin costs power and quartz.

The real chemistry is staged, and understanding the stages explains most of the furnace's design and most of its losses. In the hot crater around each electrode tip:

SiO₂ + C → SiO(g) + CO(g)
SiO₂ + Si → 2SiO(g)
SiO(g) + SiC → 2Si + CO(g)

Higher up in the bed, where the charge is cooler (~1,500–1,800 °C), the rising silicon monoxide gas is captured by the carbon:

SiO(g) + 2C → SiC + CO(g)

and the silicon carbide then descends into the crater to be consumed by the third reaction above. In other words, silicon carbide is the working intermediate, and gaseous SiO is the carrier that has to be trapped on its way up. Any SiO that reaches the top of the charge oxidizes in air to a cloud of **amorphous** (glass-like, with no crystal order) SiO₂ nanoparticles called **silica fume** or microsilica, which is bag-filtered and sold to the concrete industry. For the stated 2.5–2.7 t quartz feed, a simple balance assuming pure SiO₂ gives 2.139/(2.5–2.7), or about 79–86% recovery of feed silicon into metal. Industrial yield figures can use different feed and recycle boundaries; they should not be equated automatically with this balance. Unrecovered silicon includes material in fume and slag. A furnace that is run too hot, too fast, or with too little reactive carbon in the upper bed loses more.

### 3.2 The furnace

A modern silicon furnace is a steel shell lined with **refractory** (a ceramic that stays solid and inert at very high temperature), typically 8–12 m in diameter and several meters deep, open at the top (or fitted with a semi-closed hood for gas capture), rated at **10–45 MW**. Three **Söderberg electrodes**, self-baking carbon columns 1.2–2 m in diameter, hang from above and descend into the charge. Söderberg electrodes are made continuously: a steel casing is filled with carbon paste (anthracite, a hard coal, plus coal-tar pitch, a tarry binder) at the top, and as the column is slipped downward the paste bakes into solid electrode in the heat of the furnace. The three electrodes carry **three-phase alternating current** (the standard form of heavy industrial power: three alternating currents, one per electrode, offset from one another by a third of a cycle, so that at every instant current is flowing through the charge between some pair of electrodes) at low voltage and enormous current, and the tips sit in gas-filled craters below the charge surface, hence "submerged arc." The numbers: a 40 MW furnace splits its power roughly three ways, ~13 MW per electrode, and the voltage between electrode and **hearth** (the furnace floor, a carbon-lined basin on which the molten silicon pools) is only ~100–150 V because the charge and the arc offer little resistance, so the current is 13,000,000 W / ~130 V ≈ 100 kA per electrode. A domestic kettle draws ~10 A; one Söderberg column carries the current of ten thousand kettles, which is why it is a metre or two thick and why the busbars (the heavy conductors feeding it) are water-cooled copper. The heat is a mixture of arc and resistive heating through the charge and the molten silicon. Electrode consumption is on the order of 100 kg per tonne of silicon and is itself a carbon input to the reaction.

The charge is dosed by weight from bins: quartz lumps plus a carefully balanced reductant mix. The reductants are chosen for **reactivity toward SiO** (how well they capture the gas on the way up), for **porosity** (a permeable charge that lets gas out and heat in), and for **ash chemistry** (ash is the mineral residue left when carbon burns, and it is where the Fe, Al, Ca, Ti, B, and P come from). The typical mix is:

- **Coal**, usually low-ash bituminous (ordinary soft) coal from Colombia or similar, the main carbon source;
- **Charcoal**, more reactive and lower in ash than coal, especially valued in Brazil and Norway, and the reason some smelters advertise a partly renewable carbon footprint;
- **Petroleum coke**, the dense, low-ash carbon left over from oil refining, low in reactivity and used sparingly;
- **Wood chips**, which contribute little carbon but keep the charge open and porous, prevent crusting, and improve SiO capture in the upper bed. A smelter consumes wood chips by the truckload, on the order of ~1 t of chips per tonne of silicon (an order-of-magnitude figure that varies with the recipe), and a shortage of clean, dry chips is a real operating constraint.

The furnace is stoked continuously: a stoking machine (or, in older plants, workers with long steel poles) breaks the crust that forms at the surface, pushes fresh charge toward the electrodes, and keeps the craters from collapsing. Molten silicon (melting point 1,414 °C) pools on the hearth and is **tapped** every 1–2 hours: a tap hole in the side wall is opened with an oxygen lance or a drilling machine, the liquid metal runs out, and the hole is closed again with a clay plug.

> **What can go wrong:** the furnace is a slow, hot, partly blind process, and most of its failure modes are about the bed. A hard **crust** of sintered charge (lumps fused together by heat without melting) and condensed SiO forms at the surface and traps gas beneath it until it **blows**, an eruption of hot gas and charge that can injure the stoking crew; the cure is more wood chips and more stoking. An electrode whose paste baked too fast or too slowly cracks, a metre or more of it drops into the crater, and the furnace loses a third of its power for days while the column is re-baked and lowered. Silicon carbide accumulates on the hearth if the bed is too carbon-rich, raising the furnace bottom until the electrodes cannot form a proper crater; the remedy is to run "under-coked" for a while to consume it. The tap hole blocks with SiC and slag and must be lanced open. And a quartz lot high in boron or titanium cannot be fixed by anything at the smelter, so tap chemistry drifts until the lot is used up. Operators read the bed through electrode position and electrical resistance (a rising electrode means a shrinking crater), off-gas temperature and fume rate, and the chemistry of every tap.

### 3.3 Refining, casting, crushing

The liquid silicon runs into a **ladle** (a large refractory-lined bucket), where it is **oxidatively refined**: a mixture of oxygen and air is blown through the melt beneath a lime-silica slag (Section 2.1: a molten oxide layer floating on the metal that absorbs the oxides you want gone). Which elements the oxygen removes is set by their appetite for oxygen, which runs Ca > Al > Si > Fe. Aluminum and calcium oxidize preferentially (their oxides are more stable than SiO₂) and **report to the slag**, the metallurgist's phrase for "end up in the slag layer rather than in the metal"; a typical refining step takes Al from ~0.5% to ~0.1% and Ca from ~0.3% to below ~0.05%. Iron is not removed by this step, because Fe is more **noble** than Si (less eager to grab oxygen), so blowing oxygen would burn silicon before it touched the iron; iron control therefore has to be done at the raw-material end. Boron and phosphorus are essentially untouched by ladle refining.

The refined silicon is cast into shallow iron or graphite moulds, or onto a casting bed, as slabs ~10 cm thick. Slow cooling segregates the remaining Fe, Al, Ca, and Ti into **intermetallic** phases (compounds of two or more metals with their own crystal structure, here FeSi₂ and Al-Ca-Si compounds) at the **grain boundaries**, the surfaces where neighbouring silicon crystals meet. This matters later: the intermetallics react with HCl at a different rate from silicon and their aluminum and calcium can shift the product split in the fluidized bed of Section 4.1, which is why some polysilicon makers specify the cooling rate and grain structure of the silicon they buy (practice varies by plant, and the effect is second-order next to the boron and phosphorus content). The slabs are crushed to the customer's size, typically 10–100 mm lumps for chemical customers.

The product is **metallurgical-grade silicon (MG-Si)**, also called silicon metal, at **98.5–99.5% Si**. A representative analysis: Fe 0.2–0.6%, Al 0.1–0.4%, Ca 0.02–0.2%, Ti 0.01–0.05%, B 5–40 ppmw, P 10–50 ppmw, plus carbon at saturation (a few hundred ppm as dissolved C and SiC). Polysilicon makers specify a "chemical grade" with tighter limits, especially on Ti, B, and P, and pay a premium for it.

> **Worked example: energy and efficiency of the arc furnace.**
> The reaction enthalpy is +690 kJ per mole of Si, and one mole is 28.09 g, so the simplified reaction-enthalpy requirement is 690 / 28.09 = 24.6 MJ/kg = 6.8 kWh/kg, or **6.8 MWh per tonne** of silicon, before counting the energy to heat the reactants to 2,000 °C, the latent heat of melting (the heat needed to melt a solid without raising its temperature), and the heat carried out by ~2 tonnes of CO gas per tonne of Si. Real furnaces consume **11–13 MWh/tonne** (the best large furnaces are near 11; small or old ones exceed 13), so the reaction-to-electricity efficiency is on the order of 55–60%, which for a 2,000 °C high-temperature process is respectable. At an industrial electricity price of $0.05/kWh, the power bill alone is ~$600/tonne, roughly a quarter to a third of the ~$1,500–2,500/tonne price of chemical-grade MG-Si (Chinese domestic prices at the low end, Western contract prices at the high end, as of 2025); that arithmetic is why silicon smelters cluster where hydro (Norway, Quebec, Yunnan, Sichuan) or cheap coal (Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia) power sits. The CO₂ footprint from the carbon reductant alone is ~4–5 tonnes CO₂ per tonne Si, and it can be built up in three steps. **Stoichiometry** (the fixed atom ratios of the balanced equation) says two moles of carbon per mole of silicon: 2 × 12.01 g of carbon per 28.09 g of silicon, i.e. 0.855 t of carbon per tonne of silicon. Every 12 g of carbon becomes 44 g of CO₂ once the CO burns in the off-gas (CO₂ carries two oxygens of 16 g each), so the stoichiometric minimum is 0.855 t × (44 / 12) = 3.1 t of CO₂ per tonne of silicon. Real furnaces use more carbon than that: ~1.2 t of **fixed carbon** per tonne of silicon, where fixed carbon is the carbon left in coal, charcoal or coke after its moisture and volatile matter (tars and gases) are driven off, i.e. the part that actually reacts (ordinary coal is only ~55–70% fixed carbon, which is why a smelter's coal tonnage is larger than these figures). The excess over 0.855 t goes to the SiO that escapes as fume after carbon was spent making it, and to the electrode paste, which is a carbon input too. So the real footprint is 1.2 t × (44 / 12) = 4.4 t of CO₂ per tonne of silicon.

### 3.4 Who makes MG-Si

Global silicon-metal production is on the order of 4.5–5 million tonnes a year, and China produces roughly 80–85% of it; the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries put 2024 world output at ~4.6 Mt with Chinese output at ~3.9 Mt (USGS counts silicon content; Chinese industry data, which count gross tonnage and include **secondary silicon**, scrap and off-grade silicon re-melted and resold rather than freshly smelted, run higher). The largest single producer is **Hoshine Silicon** in Xinjiang, with well over a million tonnes of capacity. Outside China, **Ferroglobe** (Spain, France, Canada, the US, South Africa; ~350 kt/yr silicon-metal nameplate, the largest non-Chinese producer, with actual output lower in 2024–25) and **Elkem** (Norway, Iceland, Canada, Paraguay, China; Norwegian-listed and majority owned by China National Bluestar) dominate, with **Dow** (Brazil), **RIMA** and **Liasa** (Brazil), **Simcoa** (Australia), and **Mississippi Silicon** (US) as smaller players. Wacker smelts part of its own requirement at the Holla plant in Norway (Wacker Chemicals Norway, acquired in 2010) and buys the rest of its chemical-grade MG-Si under long-term contracts from Elkem, Ferroglobe, and Brazilian producers, which is partly why supply free of Xinjiang-origin material (under a US law described in Section 9) matters to it.

Demand splits roughly into thirds: aluminum alloys; silicones (the rubbers, sealants and oils made by reacting MG-Si with methyl chloride at Dow, Wacker, Momentive, Shin-Etsu, Elkem, and Chinese producers); and polysilicon, which has been the growth engine: solar polysilicon alone consumed on the order of 2 Mt of MG-Si in 2024.

## 4. The Siemens Process

The Siemens process changes silicon's form so it can be separated from its contaminants, then changes it back. First, **metallurgical-grade silicon (MG-Si)** reacts with hydrogen chloride (HCl) to make **trichlorosilane (TCS, SiHCl₃)**. Next, distillation separates this volatile compound from other compounds. Finally, it decomposes on hot silicon rods, leaving purified solid silicon behind. These are the three **unit operations**, meaning the main kinds of processing, in the plant.

The plant also recovers and reuses chemicals. Vent-gas recovery, the silicon tetrachloride converter and the HCl absorber close the **mass balance**: accounting for where each input goes, so chlorine and hydrogen can circulate rather than being discarded after one pass. The loop, in the order the silicon experiences it:

1. **Grind and chlorinate** (Section 4.1). MG-Si powder reacts with HCl, or with silicon tetrachloride and hydrogen, in a fluidized bed to make crude TCS: a rock becomes a liquid that boils at 32 °C, and a liquid can be distilled.
2. **Condense** (Section 4.1). The hot reactor gas is chilled so the chlorosilanes become liquid while hydrogen and most of the HCl stay gas and go to recovery.
3. **Distill** (Section 4.2). A train of columns strips boron, phosphorus and metals down to parts per trillion; this is where the purity is made.
4. **Deposit** (Section 4.3). Pure TCS and hydrogen flow over silicon rods at ~1,100 °C, where the TCS decomposes and grows the rods from pencil to log over several days.
5. **Recover the off-gas** (Section 4.4). 80–90% of the mass fed leaves unreacted; the chlorosilanes are condensed, the HCl absorbed, the hydrogen purified and sent round again.
6. **Convert the byproduct** (Section 4.4). The silicon tetrachloride made in the reactor is turned back into TCS, so chlorine stays in the loop.
7. **Harvest** (Section 4.5). The rods are broken, etched, and bagged in a cleanroom.

### 4.1 Making trichlorosilane: two routes

MG-Si is ground to a powder of roughly 50–300 µm and fed to a **fluidized-bed reactor (FBR)**, a vertical steel vessel in which upward-flowing gas suspends the powder like a boiling liquid, giving intimate gas-solid contact and superb heat transfer. (The same kind of vessel, a fluidized bed, appears again in Section 5 doing a different job, growing silicon granules; the industry uses the acronym FBR for both.) Two variants are in use, and modern plants run both:

**Direct chlorination** (the original Siemens route):

Si + 3HCl → SiHCl₃ + H₂ (ΔH ≈ −220 kJ/mol, strongly exothermic)

at ~**300–350 °C** and 1–5 bar (1 bar ≈ atmospheric pressure), often with a small copper addition as **catalyst** (a substance that speeds a reaction without being consumed). The negative ΔH means the reaction releases heat (it is **exothermic**), so the reactor is cooled, not heated; runaway temperature drives the side reaction to **silicon tetrachloride (STC, SiCl₄)**, Si + 4HCl → SiCl₄ + 2H₂, which also becomes dominant above ~400 °C. **Selectivity** (the fraction of the silicon that reacts which ends up as the product you want) to TCS is typically 85–90%, the rest STC and a few percent dichlorosilane (SiH₂Cl₂, **DCS**).

**Hydrochlorination** (also called STC hydrogenation or the "cold converter" route), developed by Union Carbide and now the backbone of low-cost Chinese plants:

3SiCl₄ + Si + 2H₂ → 4SiHCl₃

at ~**500–600 °C** and **20–35 bar**, with copper catalyst. It is "cold" only by contrast with the 1,000 °C-plus thermal converter of Section 4.4 that does the same job. This reaction consumes the STC that the deposition reactor produces (Section 4.3), converting it back to TCS in the presence of fresh MG-Si. **Per-pass conversion** (the fraction of the feed converted in one trip through the reactor; the rest is separated and sent round again) is only ~20–30%, so the STC recirculates many times, but it means the plant needs far less HCl make-up and disposes of no STC. It is the reason modern integrated plants describe themselves as "closed loop."

In both variants, the impurities in the MG-Si react too. Iron, aluminum, calcium, and titanium form FeCl₃, AlCl₃, CaCl₂, TiCl₄; boron forms **BCl₃**; phosphorus forms **PCl₃** (and some PH₃ and phosphorus hydrochlorides); carbon comes through as methylchlorosilanes and hydrocarbons. Unreacted silicon, metal chlorides that are solid at reactor temperature, and fines are removed in **cyclones** (spinning gas separators that fling particles to the wall) and filters. The crude product is a hot gas of TCS, STC, DCS, HCl, H₂, and every volatile impurity chloride, which is condensed to a liquid at ~−30 to +10 °C, with H₂ and most HCl going off as gas to recovery.

**Scale.** The chlorination plant is larger than the polysilicon tonnage suggests, because most of what it makes is recycled rather than kept. Section 4.4 derives that ~14–19 kg of TCS are consumed for every kilogram of silicon deposited, so a 100 kt/yr polysilicon plant must synthesize on the order of 1.5–2 Mt/yr of TCS, roughly 200 t/h, and with only ~20–30% of the STC converting per pass the gas actually passing through the hydrochlorination reactors is several times that (the figures in this paragraph are derived from the module's own numbers, not quoted; producers do not publish reactor dimensions). That duty is shared among several reactors: vertical pressure vessels ~2–4 m in diameter and ~10–20 m tall, each turning over ~50–100 t/h of chlorosilane vapour (~), with the gas spending only seconds in the bed while a silicon particle sits in it for hours to days before it is consumed, its surface constantly renewed by attrition. Because both reactions run hot in a corrosive gas, the vessels are lined or built in nickel alloys, the bed is cooled through internal tubes or an external jacket, and the whole train is designed to run bone-dry: any moisture turns HCl into hydrochloric acid and chlorosilanes into silica.

> **What can go wrong:** the fluidized bed has four characteristic upsets. **Agglomeration**: the direct-chlorination reaction is strongly exothermic, so a region of the bed that is poorly cooled, or a slug of fines that reacts all at once, runs hot enough to fuse the powder into clumps that the gas can no longer lift; the bed **defluidizes**, channels, and its heat transfer collapses, which drives the temperature higher still. Operators watch the pressure drop across the bed (a fluidized bed has a steady, characteristic pressure drop; a fall means channelling, a rise means fines or clumps) and the temperature profile up the wall. **Runaway to STC**: any excursion above ~400 °C flips the selectivity toward silicon tetrachloride, so a cooling-water problem shows up within minutes as a rising STC:TCS ratio on the product analyzer, and the plant has to convert that STC back at a cost. **Copper and fines carry-over**: the copper catalyst and the finest silicon and metal-chloride dust ride out with the gas; if the cyclones and filters do not catch them they foul the condensers and load the columns with metals. Aluminum chloride is a particular nuisance, because it is a gas at reactor temperature and a solid below ~180 °C, so it desublimes as a crust exactly where the gas is first cooled and blocks pipework. **Corrosion**: hot HCl attacks ordinary steel, and wet HCl attacks almost everything, so a leaking seal or a wet nitrogen purge causes pinholes and metal pickup in the product; the plant is monitored by the iron and nickel readings on the crude chlorosilane and by wall-thickness checks at every turnaround.

**Who builds the equipment.** The reactors are custom-fabricated pressure vessels rather than catalogue items. GT Solar (later GT Advanced Technologies) sold hydrochlorination reactors into the 2008–2012 build-out, Poly Plant Project and Centrotherm offered complete TCS synthesis and distillation plants, and Wacker, Hemlock and Tokuyama design their own; the Chinese producers now have theirs built by domestic engineering contractors or in-house (as of ~2025, non-exhaustive).

### 4.2 Distillation: the purity step

The condensed crude chlorosilane is roughly 99.9% pure, which is nowhere near enough. It is now sent to a train of **fractional distillation columns**, typically **three to six columns** in series, each 30–60 m tall and fitted with 60–100 trays, or packed with **structured packing** (folded, corrugated metal sheets that give vapour and liquid a large surface to meet on, doing the same job as trays).

> **Intuition:** a column is a ladder of boil-and-condense steps stacked in one tower. Vapour rises from a heater at the bottom, liquid trickles down from a condenser at the top, and on every tray they meet: the hot vapour re-boils the liquid, and the vapour that leaves upward is a little richer in the lower-boiling component than the vapour that arrived. Each rung pushes the light component one step further up and the heavy one further down. Four terms carry the subject. **Relative volatility α** is the ratio of the two components' vapour pressures at column conditions; α ≈ 1 means they boil alike and are nearly inseparable, α = 3 is an easy separation. A **theoretical stage** is one perfect boil-and-condense equilibrium; a real tray achieves ~0.5–0.7 of one, because vapour and liquid never quite reach equilibrium in the second they spend together. **Reflux** is condensed overhead liquid pumped back down the column to give the rising vapour something to wash against; the higher the reflux ratio (the fraction sent back rather than drawn off), the sharper the separation and the more energy it takes. The **reboiler** is the steam heater at the bottom that drives the whole thing.

Distillation exploits the difference in boiling points among the components:

| Compound | Formula | Boiling point at 1 atm | Where it goes in the train |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silane | SiH₄ | −112 °C | Overhead with the lights (rare in a TCS plant) |
| Dichlorosilane (DCS) | SiH₂Cl₂ | 8.3 °C | Overhead in the lights column; recycled or converted |
| Boron trichloride | BCl₃ | 12.5 °C | Overhead in the lights column, if it behaves |
| Trichlorosilane (TCS) | SiHCl₃ | 31.8 °C | The product, drawn between the lights and the heavies |
| Silicon tetrachloride (STC) | SiCl₄ | 57.6 °C | Bottoms of the heavies column; to the converter |
| Phosphorus trichloride | PCl₃ | 76 °C | Bottoms, with the STC |
| Titanium tetrachloride | TiCl₄ | 136 °C | Bottoms |
| Aluminum chloride | AlCl₃ | sublimes ~180 °C (solid at column conditions) | Never leaves the bottoms; removed as a solid |
| Iron(III) chloride | FeCl₃ | ~315 °C (solid) | Never leaves the bottoms; removed as a solid |

The "lights" column takes overhead the DCS, dissolved HCl, and low boilers; the "heavies" column leaves STC, PCl₃, TiCl₄, and all the metal chlorides in the bottoms. The metal chlorides are easy: AlCl₃ and FeCl₃ are essentially non-volatile at 30–60 °C and never make it up a column. Phosphorus is moderately easy: PCl₃ boils 44 °C above TCS and follows the STC into the bottoms, and the STC stream is itself re-distilled before recycling.

**Boron is the hard one.** BCl₃ boils only 19 °C below TCS, so the relative volatility between them is small and separating them to the ppt level requires many theoretical stages at high reflux ratio, which means tall columns and a lot of reboiler energy per kilogram of product.

> **Worked example: how many trays to strip boron.**
> Where a column strips a light impurity out of a heavier product, each theoretical stage lowers the impurity-to-product ratio in the descending liquid by roughly a factor α relative to the stage above. So the minimum number of theoretical stages N_min needed to change that ratio by a factor F, at total reflux (everything condensed at the top sent back down, nothing drawn off), is N_min ≈ ln F / ln α. This is the Fenske equation, N_min = ln[(x_top / (1 − x_top)) · ((1 − x_bottom) / x_bottom)] / ln α, where x_top and x_bottom are the mole fractions of the light component at the top and bottom of the column; for a trace impurity the bracket collapses to the ratio of the two concentrations. Take α ≈ 2 for BCl₃ over TCS, estimated from the two vapour pressures. Why a 19 °C gap means about two-fold: for light liquids like these, vapour pressure roughly doubles every ~15–20 °C. That is the **Clausius-Clapeyron relation**, which says the logarithm of a liquid's vapour pressure falls linearly with 1/T, with a slope set by its heat of vaporization (~25 kJ/mol for these chlorosilanes); at ~300 K that slope works out to a doubling every ~20 °C (25,000 / (8.314 × 300²) ≈ 0.033 per kelvin, and ln 2 / 0.033 ≈ 21 K). Two liquids with similar heats of vaporization whose boiling points differ by 19 °C therefore have vapour pressures about 2× apart at any temperature in the column, so treat α as "~2". Crude TCS carries boron at ~10 ppm (~10^-5); the product needs ~10 ppt (~10^-11); F = 10^6. Then N_min ≈ ln(10^6) / ln 2 = 13.8 / 0.69 ≈ 20 theoretical stages. That is the floor at total reflux, where no product leaves; at a practical reflux ratio a column needs roughly double, ~40 stages. Real trays are ~0.5–0.7 of a stage each, so ~60–80 real trays, which is why the columns above carry 60–100 trays or the equivalent height of packing. The honest caveat: this assumes all the boron is BCl₃ and that BCl₃ behaves ideally, and neither is true, which is why the chemistry list below exists.

Worse, boron chemistry is not just BCl₃: boron forms hydride-chloride and Si-B species, and BCl₃ interacts with trace moisture and with the column's own materials, so a small fraction of the boron travels as species whose volatility is close to that of TCS. Since boron has a segregation coefficient of 0.8 in silicon (Section 1), whatever survives distillation ends up almost entirely in the wafer, with nothing downstream able to fix it. The industry's responses, layered on top of the columns:

1. **Complexing.** The chlorosilane passes over or through a **Lewis base**, a molecule with a spare pair of electrons (amines, amine-carrying resins, or in older plants simply partially hydrolyzed silica surfaces). The boron atom in BCl₃ is electron-hungry and latches onto that pair, forming an **adduct**, a heavy combined molecule that will not boil, which then leaves in the bottoms.
2. **Adsorption.** The liquid flows through **guard beds**, fixed beds of **adsorbent** (a porous solid such as activated carbon, silica gel, or ion-exchange resin that holds molecules on its surface) as a final polish, replaced when they saturate.
3. **Partial hydrolysis.** A controlled trace of water converts BCl₃ to boric-acid species that are non-volatile, at the cost of also converting some TCS to **siloxanes** (molecules with Si–O–Si links, the first step toward silica) that must be removed.
4. **A generous lights cut.** The first TCS column is run with a deliberately large "lights" draw so that every trace of BCl₃ goes overhead with the DCS and is destroyed or vented rather than allowed near the product draw, at the cost of some TCS yield.

The exact sequence and the adsorbent chemistry are among the most closely held pieces of know-how at Wacker, Hemlock, and Tokuyama. What is public is the result: electronic-grade TCS leaving the last column carries boron and phosphorus at the low-ppt level (below about 10 ppta, i.e. below ~5 × 10^11 atoms per cm³ in the eventual silicon), and total metals below 1 ppbw. The chlorosilane at this point is arguably the purest bulk chemical made by industry.

> **What can go wrong:** the column train has three characteristic upsets. Moisture ingress, from a leaking seal or wet nitrogen, hydrolyzes chlorosilanes to siloxanes that foul the packing and converts boron into species with the wrong volatility, so the boron spec fails without any change in the feed. A guard bed reaches **breakthrough** (it is full, and boron passes straight through) quietly, because nothing visible changes. And a column upset (a reboiler trip, meaning an automatic shutdown, a reflux pump failure, a surge of DCS) lets a slug of light material carry BCl₃ toward the product draw. Detection is by online analyzers on the product stream and, definitively but slowly, by the trend in float-zone test ingots grown from each lot (Section 7), which is why producers hold product in quarantine tanks.

Carbon deserves a note. TCS is a hydrocarbon-free molecule, but the MG-Si is carbon-saturated and the reductant ash brings hydrocarbons; the carbon comes through as CH₃SiCl₃ (methyltrichlorosilane, boiling point 66 °C, so it goes to the heavies), CH₃SiHCl₂ (41 °C, awkwardly close to TCS), and light hydrocarbons. Electronic-grade specs limit carbon in the final silicon to ≤ ~0.1–0.3 ppma, tighter than most solar specs.

> **Why it matters downstream:** CZ wafers deliberately contain ~10^18 oxygen atoms per cm³, dissolved from the crucible, and the wafer maker later heat-treats the wafer so that some of that oxygen precipitates as tiny oxide particles deep in the bulk, where they trap metals away from the transistors (Module 02). Carbon atoms act as unplanned seeds for those precipitates. At ~0.1 ppma and above, uncontrolled carbon makes the precipitation unpredictable, so a carbon excursion in the chlorosilane plant shows up months later as a wafer lot that precipitates oxygen in the wrong places.

### 4.3 The deposition reactor

The purified TCS goes to the **Siemens reactor**, a bell-jar chamber for **chemical vapor deposition (CVD)**: a gas carries the atoms you want, a hot surface makes the gas react and leave those atoms behind as a solid layer, and the cold parts of the chamber stay clean. The base plate, a thick stainless-steel disc with water-cooling channels and gas inlets and outlets, carries pairs of electrical **feedthroughs** (insulated connections passing through the sealed plate). Into each pair of sockets is set a **slim rod**: a 7–10 mm diameter, 2–3 m long rod of ultra-pure silicon (itself cut from previous polysilicon by float-zone growth or sawn from rods), with a short horizontal bridge across the top joining two vertical legs into an inverted **U**. A modern reactor holds **24, 36, 48, or 72 rod pairs** (i.e. 48–144 legs); older designs had 8–18 pairs. Over the base plate is lowered the **bell jar** itself: a double-walled stainless-steel dome, water- or oil-cooled, whose inner surface is polished and often silver- or gold-plated to reflect thermal radiation back at the rods. The wall is kept below ~300 °C so that no silicon deposits on it.

**Startup.** Silicon at room temperature is a poor conductor (**intrinsic**, meaning undoped, silicon has a resistivity of ~2 × 10^5 Ω·cm), so the slim rods cannot be heated by ordinary current.

> **Intuition:** metals get more resistive when hot, and silicon does the opposite, for a reason worth having. In intrinsic silicon every electron is tied up in a bond between neighbours; there are almost no free carriers. Heat shakes a few electrons loose, and the number shaken loose rises exponentially with temperature, so a drop of three or more orders of magnitude in resistivity between 25 °C and 400 °C is normal. A metal's electrons are already free at any temperature; heating only makes the vibrating lattice get in their way. This is also why a thin, hot leg of silicon is a hazard (see the failure box below): hotter means lower resistance, which at constant voltage means more current and more heat.

Either a high-voltage (several kV) supply forces a small current through the cold, highly resistive rods, which warms them, lowers their resistance and lets more current flow, a deliberately controlled version of the hotter-means-more-current feedback described above, or the rods are preheated by radiation from an auxiliary heater; either way they are brought to ~400 °C, where their resistivity has fallen by orders of magnitude and the main low-voltage, high-current supply takes over. From then on the rods are **Joule-heated** (heated by their own resistance to the current passing through them, at a power of current squared times resistance), with optical **pyrometers** (thermometers that read temperature from the colour and brightness of the glow, needed because nothing can touch the rod) reading the rod surface through sight glasses to control the current.

**Deposition.** A mixture of hydrogen and TCS (molar ratio H₂:TCS in the range ~3:1 to 10:1, most plants toward the lower end today) is fed at a total pressure of ~5–6 bar (old reactors ran near atmospheric; pressure raises deposition rate). The rod surface is held at **~1,050–1,150 °C**, well below the 1,414 °C melting point. The principal reactions at the hot surface are

SiHCl₃ + H₂ → Si + 3HCl (hydrogen reduction)
2SiHCl₃ → Si + SiCl₄ + 2HCl (disproportionation)

**Disproportionation** means two identical molecules trade atoms so that one ends up richer and one poorer, here in chlorine. (A third pathway runs through the short-lived intermediate SiCl₂ and gives the same products.) Read the two equations as limits, not as a recipe: pure hydrogen reduction would consume 1 mol of TCS per mol of silicon deposited, pure disproportionation 2 mol. In the real reactor the disproportionation does not stop there, because the STC-rich mixture keeps trading atoms, and the fully disproportionated net reaction the industry quotes is

4SiHCl₃ → Si + 3SiCl₄ + 2H₂

four TCS per silicon deposited, three of them leaving as STC. A real run sits between the limits; one balanced blend of the pathways is 3SiHCl₃ + H₂ → Si + SiCl₄ + SiH₂Cl₂ + 3HCl. The silicon atoms that do not land on the rod are not lost: they leave the reactor as STC and DCS, which Section 4.4 returns to the loop. The net effect is that for each mole of silicon deposited, on the order of 3–4 moles of TCS are consumed, with 1–2 moles ending up as STC and the balance as DCS and other chlorosilanes that are recycled to the columns. Only ~10–20% of the TCS entering the reactor converts to solid silicon per pass; the rest leaves as unreacted TCS, STC, HCl, DCS, and hydrogen. The gas velocity and injection nozzle pattern are engineered to sweep the rods uniformly; poor distribution gives fat rods near the inlet and thin ones elsewhere.

The rods grow radially at roughly **0.5–1 mm of radius per hour**. Over a run of **60–120 hours** each leg grows from ~8 mm to **120–200 mm** in diameter, and the U-pairs end up as heavy, dull-grey, faintly nodular columns. As a consistency check, a radius going from 4 mm to 60–100 mm in 60–120 h is 0.5–1.6 mm/h, which matches the stated rate (the fastest combination is a solar recipe). Process engineers walk a line: raising surface temperature and TCS **partial pressure** (the share of the total pressure contributed by one gas, i.e. its concentration in the mix) speeds growth but promotes powder and popcorn, described below. Electronic-grade rods are grown slower and smoother than solar rods for exactly this reason.

> **What can go wrong:** a Siemens run is 100 hours at 1,100 °C, and five things go wrong regularly. **Popcorn** is the classic: at high temperature and high TCS partial pressure the surface grows a dendritic (tree-like), cauliflower texture whose closed voids trap process gas and adsorb contaminants, and popcorn chunks release that gas violently when they melt in a CZ crucible, splashing silicon onto the crucible wall and heater. **Gas-phase nucleation** (silicon particles forming in the gas rather than on the rods; **nucleation** is the first appearance of a new solid) makes powder that fouls the off-gas system and lands on the rods as a loose skin. A **thin-leg runaway** follows from the intuition above: a leg thinner than its neighbours has less cross-section, so at the same current it runs hotter, which lowers its resistance and makes it grow faster and hotter still, until it melts through. An **air leak** oxidizes the rods and makes an explosive hydrogen-air mixture. A **power dip** lets the rods cool and crack from thermal shock. Detection is by the pyrometers and the current-voltage trend of each pair (a leg heading for runaway shows as a falling resistance), by off-gas analysis, and after the run by visual and optical sorting of the chunk, with popcorn material downgraded.

**Power.** Nothing about the chemistry demands much energy; the TCS reduction is only mildly endothermic. The cost is radiation. Every hot surface radiates power proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature, the **Stefan-Boltzmann law**: P = σεT⁴ per square metre, where σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant (5.67 × 10^-8 W per m² per K⁴), **emissivity** ε is how close the surface is to a perfect radiator (0 to 1; silicon at these temperatures is ~0.7, and a surface with ε < 1 is called a grey body), and T is the temperature in kelvin. Doubling the temperature multiplies the loss by 16. A grey body at 1,373 K (1,100 °C) with emissivity ~0.7 radiates about σεT⁴ = 5.67 × 10^-8 × 0.7 × (1,373)⁴ ≈ **140 kW per square meter** of rod surface. What that means: a hand-sized patch of rod, ~100 cm², radiates ~1.4 kW, about as much as a kitchen hob ring. A single fully grown 150 mm × 2.5 m leg has ~1.2 m² of surface. Rods facing each other re-absorb much of one another's radiation and the reflective wall returns some, but the reactor still dumps megawatts into its cooling water for the whole run.

> **Worked example: from 140 kW/m² to kWh/kg.**
> A fully grown leg with ~1.2 m² of surface radiates ~170 kW at 140 kW/m². A 36-pair reactor has 72 legs, ~86 m² of hot surface, so ~12 MW of gross radiation at the end of the run. The reactor actually draws 3–5 MW at that point, so most of the radiation never reaches the cooling water: it lands on a neighbouring rod and is re-absorbed, or is bounced back by the plated wall. Average over the run, since the rods are thin for the first half: ~2.5–3.5 MW × ~100 h ≈ 250–350 MWh per batch. A 36-pair batch is ~6 t of silicon (~5–7 t depending on final diameter), so 250–350 MWh / ~6 t ≈ 40–60 kWh/kg, the stated range for a modern reactor. In an older 8–18-pair reactor, fewer and thinner rods see more cold wall and less of each other (the **view factor**, the fraction of one surface's radiation that lands on another, is worse), the wall was unplated steel, and the batch was only 1–2 t, so the energy came out at 100–150 kWh/kg.

So Siemens reactors of 1980s–2000s design consumed **100–150 kWh per kg** of silicon (some plants still run them); the best modern reactors, with large rod counts, high pressure, optimized H₂:TCS, and reflective walls, are in the **40–60 kWh/kg** range for the CVD step alone; and the all-in figure for a modern plant (distillation reboilers, converters, compressors, cooling) is ~50–65 kWh/kg, against ~100+ kWh/kg for older complete lines. That electricity is 30–40% of the cash cost of solar polysilicon, and it is the reason the industry migrated to coal-powered Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia at $0.03–0.04/kWh.

**Batch size.** A 12–24 pair reactor of 1990s design yields **1–2 tonnes** per batch; the 36–72 pair reactors built in China since ~2018 yield several tonnes, with the largest reported designs approaching 10 tonnes. A 100,000 t/yr solar plant thus runs on the order of 100–150 reactors in staggered cycles, each with its own power supply, **gas manifold** (the branching pipework and flow controllers that meter TCS and hydrogen into the reactor's nozzles) and **off-gas header** (the collecting pipe that carries the exhaust to the recovery system of Section 4.4).

### 4.4 Off-gas recovery and the STC loop

The reactor exhaust, 80–90% of the mass fed, is the part of the plant that decides whether the operation is economic and legal. The **vent-gas recovery (VGR)** system compresses and chills the stream; chlorosilanes (TCS, STC, DCS) condense at around −40 to −60 °C and go back to distillation; HCl is absorbed into cold chlorosilane or water and re-used; hydrogen is purified over activated-carbon adsorbers and recycled to the reactors. The hydrogen loop has its own purification and monitoring because of what it can carry: moisture and oxygen make siloxanes and oxide on the rods, hydrocarbons bring carbon, and boron or phosphorus species that slipped past the condensers go straight to the hot surface. The recycled hydrogen is therefore held to roughly the standard of a fresh electronic-grade gas: moisture and oxygen at the ~ppb to low-ppm level, and chlorosilane, boron and phosphorus carry-over below what the online analyzers can see (~), because the hydrogen is fed at several times the molar rate of the TCS, so even a part-per-million impurity in it is delivered to the rods many times over. The recycle stream is, in short, treated as a product stream.

The **STC** stream is the largest byproduct; an open-loop Siemens plant makes something like 10–20 kg of STC per kg of polysilicon. Three fates are available:

1. **Hydrochlorination** back to TCS with MG-Si and H₂ (Section 4.1), the dominant modern route;
2. **Thermal hydrogenation**, SiCl₄ + H₂ → SiHCl₃ + HCl, at ~1,000–1,250 °C in a graphite-heater "hot converter," an older, energy-hungry approach (~1–2 kWh per kg of STC converted) that some Western plants still run;
3. **Sale** to the fumed-silica industry (Cabot, Evonik, Wacker's own silica business; **fumed silica** is the feathery SiO₂ made by burning SiCl₄ in a hydrogen flame, used as a thickener and filler) and to optical-fiber **preform** makers (Corning, Prysmian, Sumitomo Electric; a preform is the thick glass rod an optical fibre is drawn from), for whom STC is the raw material. Wacker Burghausen is famous for being a chemical complex in which the polysilicon plant's STC feeds the silica plant, which in turn feeds silicones, so that "waste" is a product.

> **Worked example: the closed loop per kilogram of silicon.**
> One kilogram of silicon is 1,000 / 28.09 = 35.6 mol. At 3–4 mol of TCS consumed per mol of silicon deposited, that is 107–142 mol, or (at 135.5 g/mol) ~14–19 kg of TCS consumed per kg of silicon. But only 10–20% of the TCS entering the reactor becomes solid silicon per pass, so the reactor must be fed 5–10 mol of TCS for each mol of silicon it keeps: on the order of **25–35 kg of TCS circulates through the reactor per kg of silicon** at the better end of the conversion range, up to ~50 kg at the worse end. Of the TCS consumed, 1–2 mol per mol of silicon leaves as STC (169.9 g/mol): 36–71 mol, i.e. **~6–12 kg of STC per kg of silicon** from the deposition reactor alone. The 10–20 kg/kg quoted above for an open-loop plant is larger because it also counts the STC made in the direct-chlorination reactor (10–15% of the silicon reacted there goes to STC rather than TCS) and converter losses. Each kilogram of STC sent to hydrochlorination converts at only 20–30% per pass, so it circulates three to five times before it is all TCS. Add the hydrogen, recycled at many times the amount the reactions consume, and the HCl (3 mol formed per mol of silicon deposited, 3 mol consumed per mol of TCS made), and the plant's steady state is tens of kilograms of chlorosilane in motion for every kilogram of silicon that leaves. That is why compressors, condensers and columns, not reactors, dominate the plant's footprint and much of its capital cost.

### 4.5 Harvest, breaking, etching, packaging

When the target diameter is reached the power is ramped down, the reactor is purged (flushed) with nitrogen, the bell jar is lifted by crane, and the rods are removed. For electronic grade, everything from this moment on happens in a **cleanroom** with the same logic as a fab: the polysilicon is now pure enough that a fingerprint, a steel tool, or ambient dust would be the dominant contamination. A cleanroom's class counts airborne particles of 0.5 µm or larger: under the old US Federal Standard, Class 1,000 means ~1,000 such particles per cubic foot of air, against roughly a million in ordinary office air; the ISO scale counts per cubic metre, and polysilicon harvest rooms are typically ISO Class 6–7, i.e. Class 1,000–10,000.

The U-rods, each weighing 100–250 kg, are broken into chunks: historically by operators with tungsten-carbide or silicon-headed hammers, now for electronic grade by automated crushers with tungsten-carbide or silicon-lined jaws followed by optical sorting; solar grade uses ordinary jaw crushers. The output is screened into size classes (typically chips < 10 mm, then 10–45, 45–90, 90–160 mm, and lumps > 160 mm), because a crystal grower needs a particular size distribution to pack the crucible densely (Module 02).

Breaking contaminates the surface with tool metal and particles. For electronic grade, the chunks are therefore **etched** in HF/HNO₃ (nitric acid oxidizes the silicon surface, hydrofluoric acid dissolves the oxide), removing a few microns of surface and the embedded metal with it, then rinsed in 18.2 MΩ·cm ultrapure water and dried in filtered nitrogen. That resistivity figure is the purity gauge for water: perfectly pure water at 25 °C has a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ·cm from its own faint self-ionization, and any dissolved ion lowers it, so a reading of 18.2 means nothing else is there. Etched electronic-grade chunk carries surface metals below ~1 ppbw total; "as-broken" chunk can be 10–100 × higher. Solar grade is mostly sold unetched, "as-is," one reason it is cheaper.

> **What can go wrong:** the harvest is where good silicon gets dirty. Crusher jaws shed metal; a worn tungsten-carbide liner shows up as tungsten and cobalt on the surface assay. Etch baths carry over: acid trapped in a crack, or a rinse cut short, leaves fluoride and nitrate residues that read as contamination at the customer. Bags tear on pallets. Cold chunk brought into a warm room sweats condensation, the moisture problem of a leaking column seal transferred to the product's surface. All of it is caught by the surface-metal extraction and particle counts of Section 7, which is why every lot is sampled.

Chunks are weighed into **double polyethylene bags** of 5–10 kg, heat-sealed, packed in cartons on pallets, and shipped by truck or container. A 300 mm fab's silicon supply chain thus literally begins with a cardboard box of grey rocks arriving at a Shin-Etsu or SUMCO crystal-growing plant.

**Who builds the equipment.** Siemens reactors and converters were historically supplied by GT Solar (later GT Advanced Technologies), Centrotherm, MSA Apparatus and Poly Plant Project, and are now largely built by Chinese fabricators or designed in-house at Wacker, Hemlock and Tokuyama; distillation columns and vent-gas recovery come from standard chemical-engineering contractors (as of ~2025, non-exhaustive).

## 5. FBR Granular Polysilicon: the Silane Route

The Siemens process's two structural weaknesses are that it is a batch process and that it spends its energy heating a surface that faces a cold wall. The **fluidized-bed reactor (FBR) granular** process attacks both.

### 5.1 Silane

Most FBR plants use **monosilane, SiH₄**, rather than TCS. Silane is made from TCS by catalytic **redistribution**, in which the molecules swap hydrogen and chlorine atoms among themselves until some carry all four hydrogens (the Union Carbide process, developed under a 1970s US DOE program and commercialized by Union Carbide at Moses Lake, later REC Silicon, and by MEMC/SunEdison at Pasadena, Texas, whose technology now belongs to GCL):

2SiHCl₃ ⇌ SiH₂Cl₂ + SiCl₄
2SiH₂Cl₂ ⇌ SiH₃Cl + SiHCl₃
2SiH₃Cl ⇌ SiH₄ + SiH₂Cl₂

over **ion-exchange resin** catalysts (plastic beads carrying reactive chemical groups on their surface, here amines that catalyse the swap) in a cascade of reactors and columns. The equilibria are unfavorable at each step, so the system runs as a reactive-distillation loop (reaction and distillation in the same circuit) that keeps stripping off the volatile silane (boiling point −112 °C) and recycling the chlorides; net, about 4 moles of TCS become 1 mole of silane and 3 moles of STC, which is hydrochlorinated back to TCS. Silane is pyrophoric (ignites spontaneously in air), which makes its handling the main safety feature of these plants.

**Conditions and energy.** The redistribution reactors themselves are mild: the amine resin has a plastic backbone that degrades above roughly 100 °C, so they run at ~50–100 °C (~) and a few to a few tens of bar, and the swap reactions are nearly thermally neutral. The cost sits in the separations around them. Each equilibrium above lies far to the left (only a few percent of the mixture is the more hydrogen-rich product at any moment), so the loop must distil and recycle the chlorosilanes many times over, and the silane column is the awkward one: silane boils at −112 °C and cannot be liquefied at all above its **critical temperature** (the temperature above which a gas cannot be condensed however hard it is compressed) of −3.5 °C, so its condenser runs at tens of degrees below zero under ~10–20 bar (~), which is refrigeration duty. Feeding the loop is the hydrochlorination reactor of Section 4.1 at ~500–600 °C and 20–35 bar, which turns the 3 moles of STC back into TCS. Producers do not publish the loop's energy separately, but it can be bracketed from the module's own figures: GCL's claimed total of under 20 kWh/kg for granular silicon, less the 5–15 kWh/kg of the deposition step (Section 5.2), leaves ~5–15 kWh/kg for making and purifying the silane (hydrochlorination, redistribution, distillation, compression and refrigeration). That is the number the FBR-versus-Siemens comparison in Section 5.2 silently depends on: the granular route only wins if the silane loop costs less than the ~25–55 kWh/kg it saves at the reactor.

> **What can go wrong:** three failure modes belong to the silane loop specifically. **Moisture** is worse here than in a TCS plant: a wet purge gas hydrolyzes chlorosilanes to siloxanes and silica that foul the resin beds and columns, and it poisons the amine resin, whose activity is watched through the per-pass conversion of each reactor; a resin that has been overheated or poisoned is replaced, which means a shutdown. **Premature decomposition**: silane begins to decompose on hot surfaces at a few hundred degrees, so compressor discharge temperatures and any hot spot in the pipework grow a silicon skin and, worse, shed silicon dust into the product; the loop is designed with cool compression and no hot dead-legs. **Leaks** are the safety problem rather than a quality one: silane ignites on contact with air, and a leak announces itself as a flame or, if the gas accumulates before igniting, as an explosion, so silane plants are built with open-air structures, gas detectors on every flange, and remote-operated isolation valves, and the silane inventory is kept as small as the process allows.

Silane has a chemical advantage beyond process economics: it contains no chlorine and its purification by distillation is different in character. Boron and phosphorus **hydrides** (compounds with hydrogen: B₂H₆, PH₃) boil at −93 °C and −88 °C respectively, far from silane's −112 °C, and metal hydrides are essentially non-existent as volatiles, so silane can be distilled and adsorber-purified to a level that makes it also the standard silicon precursor inside the fab for CVD and for **epitaxy** (growing a new single-crystal silicon layer on top of a wafer, Module 06).

### 5.2 The reactor

Silane decomposes cleanly and completely:

SiH₄ → Si + 2H₂

at **600–750 °C**, much lower than TCS, with no HCl byproduct and near-100% single-pass conversion. In the FBR, a bed of silicon **seed granules** (~0.3–1 mm, made by grinding product granules in a clean mill) is fluidized by a mixture of silane and hydrogen entering through a **distributor plate** (a perforated plate that spreads the gas evenly across the bed) at the bottom. Silane decomposes on the granule surfaces; each granule grows layer by layer until it is **1–3 mm** across and heavy enough to be withdrawn from a bottom port while fresh seed is added at the top. The process is continuous, running for weeks to months between shutdowns.

The central engineering problem is heating the bed without depositing silicon on the heater.

> **Intuition:** think of a hot-air popcorn popper. The airflow holds the kernels up and tumbles them; now imagine that every hot surface in the pot, not only the kernels, grows a skin of popcorn. In an FBR every surface at reaction temperature grows silicon, and the heater, by definition, is the hottest surface in the vessel. Whatever heats the bed must either stay below deposition temperature itself or be kept out of contact with the gas.

Three approaches are used. Wall heaters put the heat through the reactor wall, which then runs hotter than the bed and crusts up over weeks. Microwave heating couples energy directly into the granules, so the wall can be cooler than the bed. Induction heating drives current in a conductive sleeve around the bed or in the bed itself. **Liners** of quartz or silicon carbide between the steel wall and the bed reduce metal contamination, but quartz and SiC conduct heat poorly and crack under steep temperature gradients, so a lined reactor cannot be heated as aggressively through its wall.

Two mechanisms limit the process. First, **homogeneous nucleation**: silane will happily decompose in the gas phase into amorphous silicon nanoparticles, or "fines," instead of on the granules; fines **elutriate** (are carried out of the bed by the gas) with the hydrogen and are lost or, worse, deposit on downstream surfaces. Keeping the silane partial pressure low and the gas residence time short suppresses this but limits throughput. Second, **contamination from the reactor**: every granule is in constant collision with every other granule and with the wall. Steel walls put iron, chromium, and nickel on the granule surfaces; the liners above improve this at the cost of heating. Granular product also traps **hydrogen** (from incompletely dehydrogenated growth layers, typically tens of ppm), which comes out as bubbles when the granules melt and can splash silicon in a CZ crucible unless the material is degassed or melted carefully. Dust on the granule surface increases the oxygen and carbon load in the melt.

> **What can go wrong:** fines production climbs when the silane feed is pushed for throughput, and the fines then plug filters and coat the off-gas train. A heater crust grows until the heat flux into the bed falls and the reactor must be shut down and chipped clean. Wall metals put iron, chromium and nickel on every granule's surface, which is why granular product is specified by surface metals as much as bulk. Trapped hydrogen makes granules spit and splash in the crucible, coating the hot zone with silicon droplets. And a contaminated seed batch (a dirty mill, a wrong-grade lot) propagates through weeks of continuous product, because every granule grows around a seed. Detection is by surface-metal extraction, hot-extraction hydrogen analysis (Section 7), and by the customer's ingot yield.

> **Worked example: energy per kilogram, FBR versus Siemens.**
> The deposition step uses 5–15 kWh/kg in an FBR against 40–60 kWh/kg in a modern Siemens reactor, roughly 4–8× less. At a Chinese industrial tariff of ~$0.035/kWh, the saving of ~25–55 kWh/kg is worth roughly $1–2 per kg of silicon (an order-of-magnitude figure), a tenth to a third of a ~$5–8/kg selling price (as of 2025). That is the whole commercial case for granular silicon, and it is why GCL kept spending for five years to make the process work.

Beyond the deposition energy, FBR needs no rod harvesting, breaking, or etching, and GCL claims a total energy consumption for its granular product below 20 kWh/kg including silane production, versus ~50 for its former Siemens lines. FBR reactors are not bought from an equipment vendor; GCL and REC each built their own, and the reactor design is the core of the know-how.

### 5.3 Who runs FBR and how it is used

**GCL Technology** is the world's largest FBR producer by a wide margin: it bought SunEdison's silane-FBR technology in 2017, spent five years fixing it, and by 2025 had converted its entire ~480 kt/yr **nameplate** capacity (what the plants are designed to make, not what they make; GCL produced ~269 kt in 2024) to granular silicon, about a quarter of Chinese output in the first half of 2025 by the company's own account. **REC Silicon** ran silane-FBR at Moses Lake, Washington, for two decades, mothballed it in 2019, restarted it in 2023–24 with Hanwha as anchor customer, and announced its shutdown in December 2024 (production ceased in early 2025) after the granular material failed Hanwha's qualification test on impurity levels and ingot yield; REC still makes silane gas at Butte, Montana, for the electronics industry, and Korea's Hanwha, its largest shareholder since 2022, bought out the remaining shareholders through a 2025 offer and took the company private, delisting it from the Oslo exchange in 2026 (as of mid-2026). **Wacker** has run a small (~650 t/yr) TCS-based FBR at Burghausen since 2009 as a supplement to its Siemens lines.

The way the market uses granular polysilicon reveals its status. Solar crystal growers blend granules at ~20–50% with Siemens chunk: the granules fill the gaps between chunks, raising crucible packing density from ~55–60% to ~70%, and they enable **continuous Czochralski (CCz)**, in which granules are metered through a feed tube into the crucible during the pull (Module 02); chunk cannot be fed that way. For electronic grade, granular polysilicon has never won a significant share, because the surface-metal and hydrogen issues remain hard to close at 300 mm.

## 6. Grades: Electronic Grade vs Solar Grade

**SEMI** (Semiconductor Equipment and Materials International) is the industry's standards body, and its **SEMI PV17** specification defines four grades of solar polysilicon feedstock; wafer makers write their own, tighter specs for electronic grade.

One row of the table below needs a concept the earlier sections have not used. In p-type silicon the holes are the majority carriers and electrons the minority (in n-type the reverse). Light, or a transistor in operation, injects extra minority carriers, and the **minority-carrier lifetime** is how long such an extra electron survives, on average, before a defect or a metal atom swallows it. A lifetime of 1,000 µs (a millisecond) means a very clean crystal; a single interstitial iron atom per 10^12 silicon atoms shortens it measurably, which is why lifetime is the device engineer's favourite proxy for metal contamination. Rough boundaries, as of ~2025:

| Property | Electronic grade (EG) | Solar grade, SEMI PV17 Grade I–II (n-type / high-efficiency) | Solar grade, PV17 Grade III–IV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal purity | 9N–11N | ~7N–8N | 6N–7N |
| Boron | ≤ 0.02–0.05 ppba | ≤ 0.1–0.3 ppba | ≤ 1–3 ppba |
| Phosphorus | ≤ 0.05–0.1 ppba | ≤ 0.3–1 ppba | ≤ 3–10 ppba |
| Total bulk metals (Fe, Cr, Ni, Cu, Zn, …) | ≤ ~0.1–1 ppbw | ≤ ~10–100 ppbw | ≤ 100–1,000 ppbw |
| Carbon | ≤ 0.1–0.3 ppma | ≤ 0.5–1 ppma | ≤ a few ppma |
| Surface metals (etched chunk) | ≤ ~1 ppbw | as-is | as-is |
| Minority-carrier lifetime of FZ test ingot | ≥ 1,000 µs | ≥ ~100 µs | not specified |

The Chinese solar industry's switch in 2023–2025 from an older solar-cell design built on boron-doped (p-type) wafers to a newer, more efficient one built on phosphorus-doped (n-type) wafers tightened the solar spec sharply, because n-type wafers are sensitive to stray boron and to the metals that limit lifetime; the good "N-type grade" solar polysilicon of 2025 (B ≤ ~0.1 ppba, P ≤ ~0.3 ppba, ~8N) is close to what was considered electronic grade in the 1990s.

The electronic-grade supply is dominated by a handful of incumbents (all as of 2025):

- **Wacker**: Burghausen and Nünchritz, Germany; Charleston, Tennessee.
- **Hemlock Semiconductor**: Hemlock, Michigan; owned by Corning with a Shin-Etsu Handotai minority stake since Dow's exit in 2020.
- **Tokuyama**: Tokuyama, Yamaguchi, Japan.
- **OCI**: Gunsan, Korea, converted to electronic grade only in 2020, plus Samalaju, Malaysia.
- **High-Purity Silicon Corp.**: Yokkaichi, Japan, with a US plant in Alabama; the former Mitsubishi Materials polysilicon business, sold to the wafer maker SUMCO in 2023.
- **REC Silicon**: silane gas, and historically FBR and Siemens polysilicon at Moses Lake and Butte.

So why do these six hold the market when Chinese producers make twenty times the tonnage? Four reasons.

1. **It is a small market.** Electronic-grade demand is on the order of 40–50 kt/yr, perhaps 3% of global polysilicon (~1.5–2 Mt/yr), and it grows with wafer starts at a few percent a year, not with solar's 30%. A single 100 kt solar line would double the world's EG supply; no one builds for that.
2. **Qualification is slow and sticky.** A wafer maker qualifying a new feedstock source for 300 mm grows test crystals, runs full wafer characterization (resistivity, lifetime, oxygen precipitation behavior, defect density), then ships wafers to fab customers who run their own qualification lots; the cycle is one to two years and the fab customer sees no benefit, only risk. Once a source is qualified it stays qualified for a decade under long-term contract.
3. **The last decade of purity is expensive in ways solar economics cannot justify**: extra distillation columns, resin guard beds, slower and smoother rod growth, cleanroom breaking, etching, and a much heavier analytical laboratory. Chinese producers have repeatedly announced "electronic grade" output (Xinte, Daqo, and Tongwei have all sold material into the 200 mm and discrete (single-function chips such as power transistors) market and have programs for 300 mm), and as of ~2025 they do supply part of China's domestic wafer industry, but the top-tier 300 mm wafer makers (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic, SK Siltron) still buy overwhelmingly from the incumbents.
4. **Traceability and geopolitics** (Section 9) now cut in the incumbents' favor: US and Japanese customers want a supply chain that is not exposed to Xinjiang or to export-control retaliation.

## 7. How Purity Is Measured

You cannot buy a 9N assay off the shelf. Verifying polysilicon purity is a multi-technique exercise, and a producer's analytical lab is a significant part of its capital and know-how.

> **Intuition:** you cannot count atoms at 10^11 per cm³, one in every five hundred billion. What you can do is measure what those atoms do: to a spectrometer, if you can first concentrate them and strip away the silicon; or to the electrons in a test crystal, which a few boron atoms in a trillion will visibly change. Every method below is one of those two moves.

**Metals.** For bulk metals, a sample of chunk is dissolved in HF/HNO₃, the silicon is driven off as volatile SiF₄ by evaporation, and the residue is taken up in dilute acid and analyzed by **ICP-MS** (inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry): the solution is sprayed into an argon plasma hot enough, ~6,000 K and above, to strip an electron from every atom, and the resulting ions are sorted by mass, so each element is counted separately down to sub-ppbw (often ppt) detection limits for Fe, Cr, Ni, Cu, Zn, Al, Na, K, Ca, and the rest. Surface metals are measured separately by extracting only the outer skin of the chunk in an HF/HNO₃ or HF/H₂O₂ bath and analyzing the extract; the difference between as-broken and etched chunk (Section 4.5) shows up here. **GDMS** (glow-discharge mass spectrometry) skips the dissolution: argon ions knock atoms off the solid sample (**sputtering**) and the same mass sorting gives a full-periodic-table survey at ~ppb levels. **Neutron activation analysis** puts the sample in a nuclear reactor, where neutrons make a fraction of each impurity's atoms radioactive; the gamma rays (high-energy photons) they then emit have energies characteristic of each element, and counting them was the historical gold standard at ppt levels; it is still used for referee measurements. Instrument makers, as examples rather than a list: Thermo Fisher and Agilent for ICP-MS.

**Boron and phosphorus** are too dilute for chemistry at 10^11–10^12 atoms per cm³; instead the industry measures them electrically and optically in a test crystal. The standard method (SEMI MF1723, descended from ASTM F1723) is to grow a **float-zone (FZ) test ingot** from the polysilicon: a rod is melted zone-by-zone by a radio-frequency induction coil without any crucible (Section 1 and Module 02), so that nothing but the feedstock's own impurities enters the crystal. The FZ ingot's **resistivity** is then measured with a **four-point probe** (SEMI MF84): current is passed through two outer needles and the voltage is read across two inner ones, which removes the contact resistance of the needles from the answer. The resistivity is converted to a net **donor** (phosphorus-type, electron-giving) or **acceptor** (boron-type, electron-taking) concentration using the standard published curves relating resistivity to dopant density (SEMI MF723). Because boron and phosphorus **compensate** each other (one boron and one phosphorus cancel electrically, so resistivity sees only the difference), a single resistivity number is ambiguous, so two further steps are used: a "type test" (whether the FZ ingot comes out p- or n-type) and, for the definitive number, **low-temperature FTIR** or **photothermal ionization spectroscopy** on the FZ sample at ~10 K. At that temperature each dopant's electron is frozen back onto its own atom, and it absorbs infrared light at an energy unique to that element, a fingerprint, so B, P, Al, As, and Sb can each be quantified individually down to ~10^11 atoms per cm³ (a few ppta). The two techniques are variants of one idea: FTIR reads the absorbed infrared directly, as a dip in the light transmitted through the slice; photothermal ionization spectroscopy detects the same absorption electrically, because an electron lifted off its atom by the absorbed photon and then shaken fully free by the lattice's thermal vibration briefly raises the sample's conductivity, and measuring that conductivity jump is more sensitive than measuring a tiny dip in a light beam. Some labs instead process the FZ ingot through a multi-pass zone refine to concentrate the impurities before measurement. That is not a contradiction of Section 1: many passes do push boron slowly toward one end of the rod (k = 0.8 moves ~20% of it per pass), which is useless as purification because it costs most of the rod and dozens of passes, but fine for an assay, where yield does not matter and a known concentration factor at the enriched end makes a trace easier to see.

> **Worked example: from an FZ test ingot to ppta.**
> Section 1 said that 10 Ω·cm p-type silicon holds ~1.3 × 10^15 boron atoms per cm³. Resistivity is roughly inversely proportional to dopant density (roughly, because carrier mobility also shifts a little with doping), so a p-type FZ test ingot reading ~1,000 Ω·cm holds ~1.3 × 10^13 boron atoms per cm³ (an estimate good to a few tens of percent). Divide by the atom density: 1.3 × 10^13 / 5 × 10^22 ≈ 2.6 × 10^-10, which is 0.26 ppba or ~260 ppta. That lot would fail the electronic-grade boron specification of ≤ 0.02–0.05 ppba by a factor of five to ten. A passing lot must give an FZ ingot of several thousand Ω·cm, meaning boron and phosphorus each in the 10^11–10^12 atoms per cm³ range; at that level a resistivity reading alone cannot say how much of each is present, because they compensate, and only the cold-FTIR line intensities can separate them.

**Carbon and oxygen** are measured by room-temperature **FTIR** (Fourier-transform infrared) absorption: infrared light is shone through a polished slice and the wavelengths it absorbs are recorded. Substitutional carbon absorbs at 605 cm⁻¹ (SEMI MF1391) and interstitial oxygen at 1,107 cm⁻¹ (SEMI MF1188); the unit is the **wavenumber**, the number of waves per centimetre, so 605 cm⁻¹ is light of wavelength 1 / 605 cm = 16.5 µm. Detection limits are ~10^15 atoms per cm³ (a few tens of ppba) at room temperature and better at cryogenic (very low) temperature. Oxygen in polysilicon is low anyway; it is the CZ crucible that adds oxygen (Module 02). Carbon in the FZ test ingot is also a check on the chlorosilane purification. Bruker and Thermo are typical FTIR suppliers (examples).

**Lifetime.** Because what a device engineer ultimately cares about is whether the metals kill minority-carrier lifetime (Section 6), the FZ test ingot is also measured by **µ-PCD** (microwave photoconductance decay: a light flash creates carriers, and a microwave beam reflected from the sample watches how fast they vanish) or by **QSSPC** (quasi-steady-state photoconductance, the same idea with a slow flash), after **surface passivation** (coating the surface so that carriers do not die there, which would otherwise mask the bulk). Electronic-grade feedstock should give ≥ 1,000 µs lifetime, sensitive to interstitial iron at the 10^10 atoms per cm³ level, a sensitivity no chemical method matches. Semilab and Sinton Instruments are the usual names for lifetime tools (examples).

> **What can go wrong:** at ppt levels the measurement is usually dirtier than the sample. The **blank** (the result of running the whole procedure with no sample) dominates: a beaker, an acid lot, a glove or a lab's air can each read as a dirty polysilicon lot, and a producer's metals lab spends more effort on its acids and its plasticware than on its spectrometers. Two labs will disagree on the same chunk for exactly that reason, so producer and customer agree on **referee methods** (a named procedure and instrument for settling disputes, often neutron activation or an FZ ingot grown by a third party) and run **round-robin** tests, the same split sample measured by several labs, to keep their numbers aligned.

**Hydrogen** in granular product is measured by **hot extraction**: a weighed sample is heated under vacuum, or melted in a graphite crucible, until the hydrogen dissolved in it escapes as gas, and the gas released is measured by a thermal-conductivity detector or a mass spectrometer; the same principle is used for hydrogen in steel. **Surface particles** on chunk are counted by rinsing the chunk and passing the rinse water through a liquid-particle counter. Every batch ships with a certificate of analysis; the wafer maker repeats a subset on incoming inspection and grows its own test crystals from new lots.

## 8. How Much Polysilicon a Wafer Needs

Where does the polysilicon go? For electronics, to the five wafer makers that account for ~90% of the 300 mm market (Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic, SK Siltron) plus a long tail (Wafer Works, Ferrotec, NSIG/Zing in China, and the FZ specialists Topsil GlobalWafers and Siltronic). For solar, to the ingot-and-wafer giants LONGi, TCL Zhonghuan, JinkoSolar, Trina, JA Solar, Gokin, and Shuangliang, essentially all in China. The crystal grower loads a fused-quartz crucible (Section 10) with a chosen mix of chunk sizes and, for solar, granules, and pulls a single crystal (Module 02).

> **Worked example: how much polysilicon is in one 300 mm ingot, and how many wafers is that?**
> A modern 300 mm Czochralski ingot has a usable cylindrical body about **2 m** long and is grown at ~305–310 mm diameter so that it can be ground to a precise 300 mm. Take 2.0 m × 306 mm: volume = π × (15.3 cm)² × 200 cm = 735 cm² × 200 cm = 147,000 cm³; at 2.33 g/cm³ that is **~343 kg** of single-crystal body. Add the **crown** (the cone-shaped shoulder where the crystal widens from its thin neck to full diameter, ~10 kg), the **tail** (the matching cone at the end, where the crystal is narrowed before it is lifted out of the melt, ~10 kg), the **Dash neck** (the thin neck grown first to shed crystal defects, Module 02; negligible mass), and the residual melt left in the crucible at the end of the pull, the **pot scrap** (a few percent of the charge, ~15–25 kg, deliberately left so that the impurity-enriched last liquid does not enter the crystal), and the initial **charge is ~400 kg** of polysilicon; a 32-inch (810 mm) crucible holds about that. Slicing: a finished 300 mm wafer is 775 µm thick, but the diamond-wire saw takes a **kerf** (the width of crystal turned to dust by the cut) of ~120–170 µm per cut, and **lapping** (grinding both faces flat with an abrasive slurry), etching, and polishing remove another ~80–120 µm, so the pitch per wafer along the ingot is roughly 1.0–1.1 mm. A 2 m body therefore yields **~1,800–2,000 wafers** (before rejecting the ends and out-of-spec slices, say ~1,700 shipped). Per shipped wafer, the polysilicon consumed is ~400 kg / 1,700 ≈ **0.23 kg**, of which only 127 g (π × 15² × 0.0775 × 2.33) is actually in the wafer; the rest is kerf, grinding swarf (dust), crown, tail, and pot scrap, some of which is recycled as **remelt** (clean scrap fed back into a crucible). Scaling up: the world starts on the order of ~7–8 million 300 mm wafers a month (starts, not capacity), so 300 mm alone consumes roughly 7–8 × 10^6 × 12 × 0.23 kg ≈ **~20–22 kt of electronic-grade polysilicon a year**, and with 200 mm, 150 mm, and FZ material, total EG demand lands in the 40–50 kt/yr range quoted in Section 6, consistent with the incumbents' known capacities.

The same numbers as a budget per shipped 300 mm wafer (all derived from the example above, ~):

| Item | kg per shipped wafer | Where it ends up |
|---|---|---|
| Polysilicon charged to the crucible | ~0.23 | Everything below |
| Crystal body (343 kg / 1,700) | ~0.20 | Sawn into wafers, including the end rejects |
| Finished wafer | ~0.127 | Shipped to the fab |
| Kerf, lapping and polishing loss | ~0.07 | Silicon dust and slurry; partly recovered |
| Crown, tail, pot scrap | ~0.03 | Partly remelted, partly sold down to solar |

## 9. Economics, the China Boom, and Xinjiang

### 9.1 Price

Solar polysilicon is a commodity with a brutally cyclical price. On the **spot market** (lots traded for immediate delivery at today's price, as opposed to multi-year contracts) it peaked near **$400/kg** in 2008, when demand outran a Siemens capacity base built for electronics; collapsed to ~$15–20/kg by 2012 as Chinese capacity arrived; touched ~$7/kg in 2020; spiked to ~**$38–40/kg** (RMB ~300/kg; RMB is the Chinese yuan) in late 2022; and then crashed as the 2022–2024 capacity wave landed. As of 2025 the Chinese domestic price was **~$5–8/kg** (RMB 35–60/kg), and by mid-2026 it had drifted to the low end of that range and below (~RMB 30–35/kg, as of August 2026).

Why that price hurts is a matter of the cost stack. The **cash cost** is what it costs to keep the plant running, excluding repaying its construction; **depreciation** is the construction cost spread over the plant's life; and their sum is the **full cost**. For a tier-1 Chinese Siemens plant (the largest, lowest-cost class, in Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia or Sichuan), the stack per kilogram of polysilicon builds up as follows, from the module's own numbers (all ~, as of 2025):

| Cost item | Basis | $/kg of polysilicon |
|---|---|---|
| MG-Si | ~1.1 kg per kg of polysilicon (i.e. ~90% of the silicon fed survives the chlorosilane loop as product) at ~$1.5–2/kg | ~1.7–2.2 |
| Electricity | ~50 kWh/kg all-in (Section 4.3) at $0.03–0.04/kWh | ~1.5–2.0 |
| Consumables, labour, maintenance | HCl and hydrogen make-up, slim rods, resin and adsorbent beds, graphite, packaging, wages, repairs | ~0.8–1.8 |
| **Cash cost** | | **~4–6** |
| Depreciation | a plant built for on the order of $10–20 per kilogram of annual capacity (~), written off over ~10 years | ~1–2 |
| **Full cost** | | **~5–8** |

Two things follow. First, MG-Si and power together are roughly three quarters of the cash cost, which is why the industry sits next to the cheapest coal and hydro in China and why MG-Si integration (Tongwei, Hoshine's customers) matters. Second, at ~RMB 30–35/kg (~$4–5/kg) the price covers cash cost only for the best plants and full cost for none. That is what Chinese **nameplate** capacity of over 3 Mt/yr against global demand of ~1.5–2 Mt/yr (as of 2025) produces. Beijing's response was the 2025 **"anti-involution"** campaign, a push against self-destructive price wars ("involution" is the Chinese term for competition that exhausts everyone and benefits no one). Its sequel, in brief:

| Date | Event | Effect on price |
|---|---|---|
| 2025 | Anti-involution campaign; industry proposes buying out and retiring older capacity | Sub-cost prices persist |
| December 2025 | Beijing-registered platform company, ~RMB 3 billion of capital from ten producers, to buy and mothball capacity | Stalls under antitrust scrutiny |
| August 2026 | Eight producers holding over ~90% of effective capacity pledge not to sell below an agreed full-cost figure (~RMB 45–48/kg, consistent with the ~$5–8/kg full cost above) | Not yet taken hold as of September 2026; spot ~RMB 30–35/kg |

Outside China, polysilicon documented free of Xinjiang content traded at **~$19–22/kg** through 2025 and 2026 (the Global Polysilicon Marker published by **OPIS**, a price-reporting agency, sat near $19–20/kg), a premium created entirely by policy.

Electronic-grade prices are not published; they are set in multi-year contracts between half a dozen producers and five wafer makers. Reasonable estimates put EG at **~$20–40/kg**, two to four times the solar price (as of 2025). At 0.23 kg per 300 mm wafer, the polysilicon in a $150–200 wafer is worth ~$5–10, and in a $20,000 leading-edge processed wafer it is a rounding error, which is precisely why electronics buyers pay for purity and traceability rather than shopping on price.

### 9.2 The China boom

In 2005 China made under 5% of the world's polysilicon; in 2024 it made ~93–95%, and nine of the ten largest producers were Chinese. **Tongwei** (originally an animal-feed company; ~910 kt/yr in Sichuan, Inner Mongolia, and Yunnan), **GCL Technology** (~480 kt/yr, all granular), **Daqo New Energy** (~300–350 kt/yr, Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia; NYSE-listed), **Xinte Energy** (a TBEA subsidiary, ~300 kt/yr), **East Hope**, and **Asia Silicon** lead (nameplate capacities as of 2025); the four largest held about 65% of global output in 2024. Their advantages were not primarily technological (they adapt Western reactor designs, and GCL's FBR is SunEdison's) but scale, 18-month construction cycles, sub-$0.04/kWh coal or hydro power, cheap local MG-Si, and a domestic wafer industry that absorbs everything. Wacker's ~80 kt/yr and OCI Malaysia's ~35 kt/yr are the only non-Chinese solar-relevant capacity of note (Hemlock's output is mostly electronic grade, with some solar).

### 9.3 Xinjiang and the UFLPA

Xinjiang's coal is the cheapest in China, and by 2020 about 40–45% of Chinese polysilicon and a larger share of MG-Si (Hoshine) came from the region. In June 2021 the US Commerce Department added Hoshine Silicon, Xinjiang Daqo, Xinjiang East Hope, Xinjiang GCL, and the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps to the **Entity List** (a US export blacklist), and **CBP** (US Customs and Border Protection) issued a **Withhold Release Order** (an order to hold a company's goods at the border) on Hoshine's products, on forced-labor grounds. The **Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA)**, signed December 2021 and enforced from June 2022, presumes that anything made wholly or in part in Xinjiang is made with forced labor and bars it from the US unless the importer proves otherwise; polysilicon was named a priority sector, and CBP detained thousands of module shipments in 2022–2023.

The mechanical consequence is that every exporter to the US must trace its silicon back through polysilicon, MG-Si, and quartz to the quarry, with **mass-balance accounting** at each step (paperwork proving that the tonnes that came out of a plant can be matched to tonnes of traced input). Chinese producers built "non-Xinjiang" lines in Inner Mongolia, Sichuan, Yunnan, and Qinghai, sourcing MG-Si from hydro-powered smelters in Yunnan and Sichuan or from Elkem and Ferroglobe; Wacker, OCI Malaysia, and Hemlock found their products newly valuable as anchors of compliant supply chains, and the EU's Forced Labour Regulation (applying from late 2027) extends the same logic. For electronic-grade silicon the issue is exposure rather than labor: a fab in Arizona or Kumamoto does not want its wafer supply to depend on a region that could be sanctioned or embargoed in either direction.

## 10. The Other Ultra-Pure Feedstocks: Crucibles and Graphite

Polysilicon is not the only material that must be ultra-pure at this stage. The crystal grower in Module 02 will melt 400 kg (electronics) to over 1,000 kg (solar, with **recharge**, refilling the crucible between pulls) of it in a vessel at 1,500 °C for days, in contact with a heater, and every atom that leaves the vessel or heater ends up in the crystal.

### 10.1 The fused-quartz crucible

The crucible is **fused silica** (amorphous SiO₂, quartz that has been melted into a glass), chosen because it is the only material that is both refractory enough (it softens at ~1,650 °C, adequate for a melt at ~1,420–1,450 °C, though the crucible does slowly sag and must be carried by a graphite **susceptor**, the cup that supports the softening crucible) and chemically compatible: it dissolves into the melt, but what it adds is oxygen and silicon, and the oxygen (at ~10^18 atoms per cm³ in the crystal) is actually engineered for, since oxygen precipitates in the wafer bulk act as **gettering** sites, places that trap metal atoms harmlessly away from the transistors (Module 02). Nothing else, no nitride, no graphite, no metal, comes close.

**Manufacture** is by **arc fusion** (spin casting):

1. **Load the mould.** A graphite mould, 32–36 inches (810–910 mm) in diameter for 300 mm ingots, spins on a vertical axis while quartz sand is poured in and pressed by the spin against the wall into a sand shell 15–30 mm thick; the inner few millimetres are laid down last from the highest-purity feed.
2. **Strike the arc.** Graphite electrodes are lowered into the cavity and an arc at several thousand degrees fuses the sand from the inside outward, because the inner surface is the one that will touch the melt and must be fused best.
3. **Pull vacuum.** Vacuum applied through the porous mould wall sucks gas out of the fusing layer, so the inner layer solidifies without bubbles.
4. **Let the outside stay bubbly.** The outer part fuses with its gas still in it, giving an opaque, bubble-filled layer that scatters heat and gives the crucible its strength.
5. **Cool, trim, inspect.** Every crucible is inspected for bubbles and inclusions in the inner layer, because a single flaw there can kill a crystal worth far more than the crucible.

The result is a two-layer structure: an **inner transparent layer** 1–3 mm thick, essentially bubble-free, and an **outer opaque layer** full of small bubbles. Bubbles at the inner surface would burst during the pull, releasing silica particles into the melt that nucleate **dislocations**, line defects where one plane of atoms has slipped past its neighbours; once one starts it multiplies, and the crystal is no longer "dislocation-free" (Module 02), which for electronic silicon means it is scrap. For the inner layer, the highest-purity feed is used: IOTA-grade Spruce Pine natural quartz, or **synthetic quartz** made by **flame hydrolysis** (burning purified SiCl₄, the same STC from the polysilicon plant, in a hydrogen-oxygen flame; the water made in the flame converts it to SiO₂ soot, which is collected and fused) or by a wet-chemical route, at Al < 1 ppm and alkalis < 0.1 ppm versus ~8–14 ppm Al in the best natural material (Section 2.2: IOTA-STD ~20 ppm total with ~14 ppm Al; the premium IOTA grades ~8–10 ppm total with ~8 ppm Al). Synthetic quartz is several times the price of natural HPQ, which is why crucible makers use it only where it counts (the inner layer, or the whole crucible for the most demanding FZ-quality and heavily doped applications). The outer layer can use lower natural grades.

Some crucibles receive a barium-doped or "crystallization-promoting" inner coating that deliberately **devitrifies** the inner surface: devitrification is a glass turning into crystals, here a fine, adherent skin of cristobalite (crystalline SiO₂) that forms on heating and resists erosion. The reason to do it on purpose is that it will happen anyway: fused silica held at 1,500 °C crystallizes wherever a speck of alkali or a scratch gives it a start, and a cristobalite skin shrinks abruptly on cooling, so a random, patchy skin flakes off into the melt and seeds defects during a long run, whereas a deliberately uniform skin stays put.

> **Worked example: how much crucible ends up in the melt.**
> The melt dissolves the inner wall at ~10 µm per hour, so a ~100 h electronic pull consumes ~1 mm of it. The wetted inner surface of a 32-inch crucible holding a 400 kg charge (a floor of ~0.5 m² and a melt ~0.3 m deep on a ~0.8 m diameter wall) is on the order of 1 m². So ~1 mm × ~1 m² ≈ 1,000 cm³ of fused silica, at 2.2 g/cm³ about **~2 kg of SiO₂**, dissolves into a 400 kg charge (all ~). If all of that oxygen stayed, the crystal would hold ~2 × 10^20 oxygen atoms per cm³ (2 kg of SiO₂ is ~4 × 10^25 oxygen atoms, spread through ~1.7 × 10^5 cm³ of silicon); the actual figure is ~10^18, so ~99% of the dissolved oxygen escapes from the melt surface as SiO gas before it can be frozen in, and it is that SiO that attacks the graphite in Section 10.2.

That dissolution is the origin of the crystal's oxygen and one reason a semiconductor crucible is **single-use**: it is cooled, cracks (fused silica and the cristobalite skin have mismatched thermal expansion), and is discarded, at a cost on the order of several thousand dollars per 300 mm crucible (more for synthetic-lined 36-inch parts). Solar crucibles, which are larger and run 300–400 hours with repeated recharging, are the crucible industry's volume business.

> **What can go wrong:** the crucible's failure modes are all about its inner millimetres. Bubbles in the inner layer too small to catch at inspection grow at 1,500 °C, open, and shed silica into the melt. Patchy devitrification, seeded by an alkali fingerprint or a scratch, flakes cristobalite into the melt. A crucible whose outer layer is too thin, or a run that is too long, sags against the susceptor and cracks on cooling. Sodium or potassium from a bad quartz lot lowers the glass's viscosity so it sags early. All of these end the same way, a **loss of structure** (the crystal grows a dislocation) that the grower sees as the disappearance of the **growth facets**, the flat ridges that run down the outside of a dislocation-free crystal and show through the viewport as bright lines along its length; when a dislocation appears they vanish within a few centimetres of further growth, and the operator knows the run is over; the run is stopped, the crystal remelted, and the crucible lot investigated.

**Suppliers.** For semiconductor crucibles, **Shin-Etsu Quartz Products** (Japan), **Momentive Technologies** (Strongsville, Ohio; the former GE Quartz, which in 2022 also absorbed the crucible business of CoorsTek, itself descended from Toshiba Ceramics), **Heraeus Covantics** (Germany; formed in January 2025 by merging Heraeus Conamic and Heraeus Comvance), and **Ferrotec** are the qualified names; Chinese crucible makers such as **OJing Technology** (Inner Mongolia, the first listed crucible maker) and a cluster of producers in Jinzhou (Liaoning) and Jiangsu dominate solar volume (as of 2025). Quartz ware for furnaces and wafer boats comes from the same firms plus Tosoh Quartz and QSIL. Above them sit the two HPQ miners, Sibelco and The Quartz Corp, and the synthetic producers (Heraeus, Shin-Etsu, and Jiangsu Pacific Quartz). The Helene episode is the reason every one of these companies now runs a formal second-source program.

### 10.2 Graphite hot-zone parts

Surrounding the crucible is the **hot zone**: the graphite susceptor, a graphite **heater** (a cylindrical resistance element slotted into a meander, a zig-zag path, carrying kilo-amperes), graphite **heat shields and reflectors**, and insulation of carbon-fiber felt or rigid carbon-bonded carbon fiber. All of it sits in argon (an inert gas) at ~1,500 °C within centimeters of the melt. Graphite is the only material that is machinable, thermally stable, electrically suitable for a heater, and does not melt or react catastrophically with silicon vapor and SiO gas. The heater's electrical design follows the same logic as the Siemens rods: graphite's resistance is low, so the heater runs at tens of volts and kilo-amperes, fed through water-cooled copper electrodes that pass through the furnace floor. The numbers for a 300 mm puller: the heater draws ~100–150 kW during body growth, at roughly 50–100 V, so its current is ~1.5–3 kA (150,000 W / 60 V = 2.5 kA), and the heater's total resistance is only a few tens of milliohms; solar pullers, with smaller charges and lighter insulation, draw less per kilogram.

> **Worked example: what the hot zone costs in power and parts.**
> Where does ~120 kW go, if the melt is already molten and the chemistry needs nothing? The same place as in the Siemens reactor: radiation. Liquid silicon at ~1,700 K has an emissivity of only ~0.3, but T⁴ is enormous: σεT⁴ = 5.67 × 10^-8 × 0.3 × (1,700)⁴ ≈ 140 kW/m², and the free melt surface in a 32-inch crucible is ~0.5 m², so ~70 kW leaves the melt surface as gross radiation; the heat shields and the crystal hanging above it intercept and return part of that, and the remainder of the heater's output makes up what leaks outward through the susceptor, shields and felt to the water-cooled steel chamber. Over a ~100 h pull that is ~120 kW × 100 h = 12 MWh, or ~30 kWh per kilogram of the 400 kg charge, comparable to the ~40–60 kWh/kg the polysilicon itself cost to deposit, and the reason hot-zone insulation design is a competitive matter. The parts: a heater lasting ~1,000–3,000 h (the "hundreds to a few thousand hours" below) is ~10–30 electronic pulls of ~100 h; at a hot-zone replacement cost of tens of thousands of dollars spread over that many pulls, the graphite adds on the order of $1,000 per pull, i.e. under a dollar per wafer, small next to the crucible, which is single-use.

The contamination pathway is carbon itself: SiO gas evaporating from the melt reacts with graphite, SiO + 2C → SiC + CO, and the CO diffuses back into the melt and dissolves as carbon, which at ~10^16 atoms per cm³ begins to disturb oxygen precipitation (Section 4.2) and at higher levels forms SiC **inclusions**, foreign solid particles frozen into the crystal. Hot-zone design therefore manages gas flow so that argon sweeps SiO away from the graphite, and the parts closest to the melt (the susceptor, the heat-shield lip) are increasingly made of **carbon-fiber-reinforced carbon (C/C)**, which is stronger and lasts longer, or are coated with **CVD silicon carbide** (a SiC skin deposited from gas, Section 4.3) to seal the surface. Ash content in the graphite (Fe, Al, Ca, V, Ti, B) is reduced from the ~100–500 ppm of ordinary electrode graphite to **< 5 ppm** (often < 2 ppm for semiconductor grade) by **halogen purification**: the machined parts are heated to > 2,000 °C in chlorine- or fluorine-bearing gas (the halogens are the chlorine family of elements), which volatilizes the metals as chlorides. Boron in graphite is a particular concern, for the usual segregation reason.

> **What can go wrong:** the same SiO that dissolves the crucible eats the heater. SiO erosion thins the heater's slots unevenly, so its resistance drifts upward and the hot spots move; the heater is replaced when the resistance drift crosses a limit. A SiC crust forms on the parts nearest the melt and spalls (flakes) off as particles. And boron in the graphite ash, or in a lot that was under-purified, reaches the melt through the gas phase and shows up as a p-type drift in the crystal's resistivity from run to run. Detection is by minority-carrier lifetime on test wafers, by the crystal's resistivity trend, and by the heater's resistance logged at every run.

The producers are the specialists in **isostatic graphite** (graphite pressed from fine powder with equal pressure from all sides, giving a fine, uniform, machinable material) that serve the rest of the fab (Module 04): **Toyo Tanso**, **Tokai Carbon**, **Ibiden** (Japan), **SGL Carbon** (Germany), **Mersen** (France), **Entegris/Poco**, and China's **Sinosteel** and Fangda, with C/C from SGL, Toyo Tanso, and Chinese entrants. A 300 mm hot zone costs tens of thousands of dollars, and heaters and susceptors are consumables replaced after hundreds to a few thousand hours.

## Summary

- Silicon has 5 × 10^22 atoms per cm³; 9N purity still leaves 5 × 10^13 foreign atoms per cm³, 25× below the boron a wafer maker adds on purpose, which is why 9N is the floor for electronics and 11N the target.
- Boron is the enemy because it is a dopant that fits the silicon lattice (k ≈ 0.8): crystal growth cannot reject it, so it must be removed chemically; iron (k ≈ 10^-5) is rejected almost completely by freezing.
- Furnace feed is lumpy quartz or quartzite, not beach sand: the pile must stay porous for CO to escape, must not shatter at 573 °C, and must be low in boron and phosphorus.
- High-purity quartz from Spruce Pine goes into crucibles, not into the silicon; two companies mine it, and Hurricane Helene (September 2024) showed the concentration risk without causing a shortage.
- The submerged-arc furnace runs SiO₂ + 2C → Si + 2CO at ~2,000 °C via SiC and SiO gas, uses 11–13 MWh per tonne against a 6.8 MWh theoretical minimum, and makes 98.5–99.5% metallurgical silicon; China makes ~85% of it.
- The Siemens process converts silicon to trichlorosilane (boiling point 31.8 °C), distils it to parts-per-trillion boron in 60–100-tray columns plus chemical guard beds, and redeposits it on 1,100 °C rods; it has been the electronic-grade route for seventy years.
- Boron trichloride boils only 19 °C below TCS (relative volatility ~2), so stripping six orders of magnitude of boron needs ~20 theoretical stages even at total reflux and chemistry on top; that is the most closely guarded know-how in the industry.
- The Siemens reactor's energy (40–60 kWh/kg modern, 100–150 older) is thermal radiation from hot rods to a cold wall, not reaction chemistry; rod count, pressure and reflective walls are the levers.
- Fluidized-bed granular polysilicon from silane uses 5–15 kWh/kg and runs continuously, but wall metals and trapped hydrogen keep it a solar blend material (GCL, ~480 kt/yr nameplate), not an electronic-grade feedstock.
- Electronic grade (boron ≤ 0.02–0.05 ppba, etched chunk, FZ test-ingot verification) is ~3% of a ~1.5–2 Mt/yr market and is supplied by a handful of incumbents (Wacker, Hemlock, Tokuyama, OCI) because qualification takes one to two years and the market is small.
- Purity is verified by ICP-MS for metals, float-zone test ingots plus resistivity and cold FTIR for boron and phosphorus, FTIR for carbon and oxygen, and minority-carrier lifetime as the device-relevant proxy; at ppt levels the blank is the enemy.
- A 300 mm wafer consumes ~0.23 kg of polysilicon (127 g ends up in the wafer); polysilicon is a few dollars of a $150–200 wafer, which is why electronics buys on purity and traceability, while solar polysilicon is a brutally cyclical commodity (~$5–8/kg in China, ~$19–22/kg outside, as of 2025–2026).
- The crystal grower also needs a single-use fused-quartz crucible (bubble-free synthetic or Spruce Pine inner layer, ~2 kg of which dissolves per pull) and halogen-purified graphite (< 5 ppm ash), because everything that touches the melt for 100 hours ends up in the crystal.

The reaction-enthalpy energy estimate and the estimated ΔG crossing use simplified thermochemical inputs. They are not rigorous minimum electrical-work or furnace-equilibrium calculations: temperature-dependent properties, phase changes and gas activities affect those results.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Chips are made from beach sand."** → Furnace feed is lumpy hard-rock quartz or quartzite chosen for size, thermal strength, and low B/P; beach sand is too fine and too dirty. The truly special "sand" in the chain is Spruce Pine high-purity quartz, and it goes into crucibles, not into the silicon.
- **"The arc furnace makes the pure silicon."** → The furnace makes 98–99% MG-Si, a commodity for aluminum and silicones. The ten additional nines come from converting silicon to trichlorosilane, distilling it, and redepositing it; the purification is chemistry, not metallurgy.
- **"Crystal growth cleans up the polysilicon, so feedstock purity is not critical."** → True for metals (Fe segregation coefficient ~10^-5), false for boron (0.8) and largely false for phosphorus (0.35) and carbon (0.07). Boron must be removed in the distillation columns or it is in the wafer forever.
- **"Solar polysilicon and electronic polysilicon are the same stuff at different prices."** → Modern n-type solar grade (~8N) is good material, but EG requires ~10–100 × lower B/P, far lower metals, etched surfaces, cleanroom handling, FZ test-ingot verification, and one-to-two-year customer qualification. That is why six companies supply almost all of it while China makes 95% of the tonnage.
- **"The Siemens process is energy-intensive because the chemistry is."** → The TCS reduction is only mildly endothermic. The energy goes into thermal radiation from 1,100 °C rods to cold, water-cooled walls, which is why rod count, pressure, reflective walls, and FBR alternatives are the levers.
- **"Hurricane Helene nearly stopped chip production."** → It stopped mining at Spruce Pine for about two weeks in October 2024. Months of inventory, intact mines, and qualified synthetic-quartz substitutes for the crucible inner layer meant no fab lost a wafer. The real lesson was concentration risk, not an actual shortage.
- **"Higher polysilicon purity always means better wafers."** → Past ~10N the feedstock is no longer the limiting input: the crucible dissolves ~2 kg of itself into the melt, the graphite feeds carbon and boron through the gas phase, and the crystal grower's own handling adds metals. Purity has to be matched across everything that touches the melt.
- **"Granular silicon is a cheaper drop-in for chunk."** → It is cheaper on energy, but trapped hydrogen, surface metals from the reactor wall, and dust limit it to a 20–50% blend with Siemens chunk in solar crucibles; no 300 mm electronic-grade line runs on it.

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

Module 00 mapped the chain from quartz to a GPU rack; this module is its first industrial step, the conversion of mined quartz (lump quartzite from quarries in Norway, Spain, Brazil, and China; high-purity quartz from Spruce Pine) into two ultra-pure products: 9N–11N electronic-grade polysilicon chunk in sealed polyethylene bags, and fused-quartz crucibles with graphite hot-zone parts. The inputs consumed are quartz, coal, charcoal, wood chips, electricity (~12 MWh/t for MG-Si, ~50–100 kWh/kg for polysilicon), HCl, hydrogen, and a great deal of analytical effort; the byproducts are silica fume, silicon tetrachloride (feeding fumed silica and optical fiber), and CO₂. The polysilicon ships from Wacker, Hemlock, Tokuyama, OCI, and High-Purity Silicon Corp. (and Tongwei, GCL, Daqo for solar) to the crystal growers, Shin-Etsu, SUMCO, GlobalWafers, Siltronic, and SK Siltron, who in Module 02 will load ~400 kg of it into a crucible from Shin-Etsu Quartz, Momentive or Heraeus Covantics, sitting in a Toyo Tanso or SGL graphite hot zone, and pull it into a 300 mm dislocation-free single crystal.
