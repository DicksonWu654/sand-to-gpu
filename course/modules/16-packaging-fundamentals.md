# Module 16: Packaging Fundamentals: From Wafer to Chip

A finished 300 mm wafer contains working circuits, but it is not yet a component you can install in a product. The transistors on it switch in picoseconds, but their terminals are aluminum or copper pads roughly 50 µm across (a **micrometer (µm)** is one-thousandth of a millimeter; a human hair is ~70 µm wide), spaced 100–150 µm apart, on a slab of silicon 775 µm thick that will shatter if you drop it, corrode if you breathe on it, and cook itself if you run it without a path for its heat to escape.

A **printed circuit board (PCB)**, the green fiberglass board that carries the chips in every product, can by contrast reliably solder to features no finer than ~0.4–1.0 mm and can only pull heat away through a few square centimeters of copper and a heatsink. **Packaging** is the discipline that bridges that gap: it takes a bare **die** (one rectangle cut from the wafer, carrying one chip) with thousands of micrometer-scale terminals and turns it into a component with millimeter-scale terminals that survives 20 years in a car engine bay or 5 years at 700 W in a data center, whether the chip is an 8-pin voltage regulator or a **system-on-chip (SoC)**, a single die that integrates processor cores, graphics, memory controllers and radios.

The problem is hard for one fundamental reason that will recur throughout this module. Every material grows when heated, by a fraction of its length per degree called its **coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)**, quoted in **ppm/K** (parts per million per kelvin: 2.6 ppm/K means the length grows by 2.6 millionths for every degree). Silicon expands at about **2.6 ppm/K**, while the organic laminate substrates and PCBs it must be joined to expand at **~15–17 ppm/K**. Warm a 30 mm die by 100 K and it grows ~8 µm; the organic substrate under it grows ~45 µm. Every heat-up and cool-down therefore **shears** the joints between them: one layer slides sideways over the other, like a deck of cards pushed from the top. Nearly every process, material, and design choice in a package is, at bottom, a strategy for managing that mismatch while still getting signals, power, and heat in and out.

By the end of this module you will be able to explain why underfill exists and what it actually does; why a GPU's substrate has 18–20 wiring layers and costs hundreds of dollars; why the 100 µm of thermal paste between die and lid is the hottest layer in a 700 W package; why four companies you may never have heard of (Disco, Ajinomoto, Ibiden, ASE) are chokepoints for the whole industry; and the difference between the package families, wire bond, flip chip, fan-out and ball grid array, that between them carry every chip made.

## Before you start

- **Wafer, die, scribe streets** (Modules 03, 05). A 300 mm wafer, 775 µm thick, carries hundreds of identical dies separated by ~50–100 µm wide lanes, the **scribe streets**, reserved for cutting them apart.
- **The BEOL and low-k dielectrics** (Module 12). On top of the transistors sit 10–15 layers of copper wiring, the **back end of line (BEOL)**, embedded in porous, mechanically weak insulators (**low-k dielectrics**); the top layer ends in ~50 µm aluminum or copper **bond pads** under a protective glass or polymer skin.
- **Sputtering and electroplating** (Modules 06, 12). Sputtering knocks atoms off a metal target with an argon **plasma** (a gas whose atoms have been split into ions and electrons by an electric field) so they coat the wafer; electroplating grows metal out of a solution by passing current. Both are reused in this module for bumps and substrates.
- **Lithography basics** (Module 07). Coat **photoresist** (a light-sensitive coating that hardens or softens where light hits it), expose a pattern, develop, then deposit or etch through the openings. Bumping and substrates use coarse, thick-resist versions of the same sequence.
- **Plasma etching and the Bosch process** (Module 09). Alternating a few seconds of SF₆ (sulfur hexafluoride) etching with a few seconds of C₄F₈ (a Teflon-like fluorocarbon) sidewall coating digs deep vertical trenches; plasma dicing reuses it.
- **Wafer sort and known-good die** (Module 14). After electrical probing, a wafer map records which dies passed; only those are worth the cost of packaging.
- **HBM stacks** (Module 15). **High-bandwidth memory (HBM)**: DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) dies thinned to ~30–50 µm and stacked on tiny solder bumps; packaging handles a finished stack as one unit.
- **School physics you will need.** Thermal expansion (materials grow with temperature; CTE in ppm/K). Stress and strain (force per unit area; deformation as a fraction of original size). Surface tension (liquids pull themselves into the shape of least surface area). Inductance (a conductor resists changes in its current; the voltage needed is V = L·dI/dt). Thermal conductivity (W/m·K: how readily heat flows through a material).
- **Vocabulary preview.** **Flip chip**: the die is turned face-down and its bumps are soldered straight to the substrate. **BGA** (ball grid array): a package whose underside is a grid of solder balls. **Substrate**: the miniature circuit board inside the package. **OSAT**: an outsourced assembly and test company that packages chips for others.

## What a Package Must Do

A package has five jobs, and they pull against one another.

**1. Electrical fan-out.** The package acts as a wiring adapter: it takes tightly spaced contacts on the die and spreads them into a pattern the board can accept. This is a change in connection spacing, not an amplification of the signals. The die's terminals sit at ~40–150 µm pitch (center-to-center spacing), either as bare **bond pads** or as **bumps**, small solder or copper terminals grown on each pad so the die can be soldered face-down. The board wants 0.4–1.0 mm pitch solder balls or leads. The package must redistribute several thousand connections across this ~10× change in scale without adding more resistance, inductance or crosstalk than the design can tolerate. **Inductance** is the property of a conductor that resists any change in its current (it stores energy in a magnetic field); a current changing at a rate dI/dt through an inductance L produces a voltage V = L·dI/dt, so long, thin wires make fast signals ring and sag. **Crosstalk** is signal on one wire inducing noise in its neighbor through their shared electric and magnetic fields. A modern data-center GPU package routes on the order of 5,000–10,000 signal and power connections; an 8-pin power-management IC routes eight.

**2. Power delivery.** The power connections must keep the voltage at the transistors steady, even when many circuits switch together. A 700–1,000 W GPU at ~0.8 V draws roughly 1,000 A. Low voltage is why the current is so large: delivering a given power at a smaller voltage requires more current. Two different effects constrain the wiring. Resistance causes a voltage loss while current flows; inductance causes an additional voltage disturbance when current changes. For steady current, V = I·R: 1,000 A through just 0.1 mΩ drops 0.1 V, an eighth of the whole 0.8 V supply, so the package's total resistance through bumps, planes and balls must be well under 0.1 mΩ. For changing current, V = L·dI/dt: when a GPU wakes thousands of cores at once its current can step by hundreds of amps per nanosecond, and even 10 pH (picohenries, trillionths of a henry) at 100 A/ns is 1 V, enough to collapse the core voltage. This is why a large fraction (often more than half) of all bumps on a high-performance die are power and ground rather than signal, why substrates contain **planes** (whole copper sheets reserved for power or ground), and why they embed **decoupling capacitors**, local charge reservoirs that supply the first nanoseconds of a current surge before the board can respond. Worked example WE-F in Step 6 shows where the resistance actually sits.

**3. Heat removal.** A cooler can remove heat only after that heat reaches it. Each material and contact between the transistors and the cooler adds an obstacle to that journey. Nearly all electrical power becomes heat in the transistor layer, within ~10 µm of the die's front surface. **Thermal conductivity**, in W/m·K, measures how readily heat flows through a material: copper ~400, silicon ~150, epoxy mold compound ~1, air ~0.03. **Thermal resistance** is the temperature rise per watt of heat flowing through a layer. For a uniform layer, R = t / (k·A), where t is thickness, k is thermal conductivity and A is the area carrying the heat. Greater thickness makes the path harder to cross; greater conductivity or area makes it easier. Thin, conductive, wide layers therefore have low resistance. In a flip-chip package the heat conducts through the ~300–775 µm of silicon backside, then through a **thermal interface material (TIM)**, a paste, gel or solder that fills the microscopic gaps between two solid surfaces, into a lid or cold plate. Every interface adds resistance, and the TIM layer, only 50–200 µm thick, is frequently the single largest resistance in the stack. Worked example WE-B, under Lids, shows why: at 700 W a 100 µm polymer TIM costs ~17 °C, the 500 µm of silicon under it ~3 °C.

**4. Mechanical and environmental protection.** Silicon and its dielectrics are brittle; the low-k dielectrics of the BEOL are the weakest part, cracking roughly as easily as window glass, about 50 times more easily than steel. The package must isolate the die from board flexure, handling and vibration, and must seal it against moisture and ionic contaminants (chloride, sodium) that cause corrosion and **electrochemical migration**: in a film of moisture under voltage, metal ions dissolve at one conductor, drift, and grow as dendrites toward the neighbor until they short it.

**5. CTE mismatch management.** Silicon: ~2.6 ppm/K. Copper: ~17 ppm/K. Solder (SAC305, the standard lead-free tin-silver-copper alloy): ~22 ppm/K. Organic build-up substrate (in-plane): ~12–17 ppm/K. Epoxy mold compound: ~7–12 ppm/K below its **glass transition temperature (Tg)**, the temperature above which a polymer changes from glassy and rigid to rubbery, and above which its CTE typically triples. A package is a **laminate**, a stack of these dissimilar layers bonded face to face, assembled at 150–260 °C and used at −40 to +125 °C. Managing the resulting **strain** (deformation divided by original size, a pure fraction), **warpage** (the whole package bowing into a dome or a bowl as its layers fight) and **fatigue** (failure from thousands of small stress cycles, each far below the breaking stress, like a paperclip bent back and forth) is the central engineering problem of packaging, and it is why the first worked example below is a strain calculation.

> **Intuition:** glue two rulers of different metals together at their centers only and warm them: the free ends slide past each other, because one ruler grows more than the other. Glue them along their whole length instead and neither can slide, so the pair curls like the bimetallic strip in an old thermostat. That curl is warpage, and converting sliding into curling is exactly what underfill does. Where the picture breaks: solder joints are not glue along a whole length; they are ~70 µm tall pillars spaced across the die, and each one must absorb its own share of the slide.

> **Worked example WE-A: CTE shear strain at a corner bump.**
> Consider a 30 mm × 30 mm flip-chip die (about the size of a large GPU compute die) on an organic build-up substrate, joined by solder bumps with a stand-off height of ~70 µm. Near the die center is the **neutral point**, where die and substrate expand together and nothing slides (the glued center of the rulers). The bumps that suffer most are at the corners, farthest from it. The **distance to neutral point (DNP)** of a corner bump is half the diagonal: 30 mm × √2 / 2 ≈ **21.2 mm**.
> The CTE difference is Δα = 15 − 2.6 ≈ **12.4 ppm/K**. Use ~217 °C, the SAC305 solidus, as an illustrative stress-free reference (the alloy solidifies across a range) and the assembly then cools to 25 °C, so ΔT ≈ 192 K for the initial cool-down.
> Relative displacement between the die corner and the substrate directly beneath it, if nothing constrained them, would be δ = DNP × Δα × ΔT = 21.2 mm × 12.4 × 10⁻⁶ /K × 192 K ≈ **50 µm**.
> Spread across a 70 µm tall solder joint, that is an engineering shear strain γ = δ/h ≈ 50/70 ≈ **0.7, or 70%** (the sideways slide divided by the joint height). Solder **yields**, that is, deforms permanently instead of springing back, at strains of a fraction of a percent. Even for a milder operating cycle (−40 to +125 °C, ΔT = 165 K), δ ≈ 43 µm and γ ≈ 60%.
> The **Coffin-Manson relation** for solder fatigue is N_f ∝ (Δγ)^(−n), where N_f is the number of cycles to failure, Δγ the strain range in each cycle, and n a fitted exponent (~1.5–2 for solder). It means life falls with roughly the square of strain: a joint seeing 60–70% strain would fail in a handful of cycles, and cutting the strain 14-fold, from 70% to 5%, buys roughly (70/5)² ≈ 200× more cycles. That is the underfill's whole job.
> This is why bare flip chip on organic substrates is impossible without **underfill**: an **epoxy** (a two-part resin that hardens by chemical cross-linking into a rigid, glue-like solid) that fills the gap between die and substrate mechanically couples the two so that the assembly bends together (warps) rather than shearing the joints. With underfill, the bump strain drops by more than an order of magnitude and the joint survives 1,000+ thermal cycles. It is also why very large dies were historically mounted on ceramic substrates (CTE ~7 ppm/K) or, today, on silicon **interposers** (slabs of silicon carrying only wiring; CTE 2.6, perfectly matched) as in **CoWoS** (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate, TSMC's interposer-based package), covered in Module 17.

> **Why it matters downstream:** the same DNP arithmetic is why CoWoS puts its dies on a silicon interposer, CTE-matched to the dies, and then fights the mismatch only once, at the interposer-to-substrate joint, and it is why Module 17's central manufacturing problem is the warpage of 80–100 mm packages during reflow.

## The Back-End Flow in Order

Four terms first. In **flip chip** the die is turned face-down and its bumps are soldered straight to the substrate. A **ball grid array (BGA)** is a package whose underside is a grid of solder balls; a **flip-chip ball grid array (FCBGA)** combines the two and is the package of essentially every CPU, GPU and high-end SoC. The industry calls the whole sequence below **assembly and test (A&T)** or simply **the back end**; the companies that do it for others are **outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT)** firms, while an **integrated device manufacturer (IDM)** such as Intel or Samsung designs, fabs and usually packages its own chips.

The back-end flow, in order, with the reason each step sits where it does. There are two branches, the FCBGA branch and the cheaper wire-bond/leadframe branch, and they differ only in how the die is attached and connected, so the list below carries both: Steps 4 and 5 belong to the leadframe branch, Step 6 is the FCBGA alternative to them, and a given die goes through one or the other, never both. Steps 1–6 each have their own subsection below, numbered the same way; Steps 7–11 are covered in the sections that follow them.

1. **Wafer-level bumping** (FCBGA branch only; Step 1 below). Copper or solder terminals are grown on every pad of every die while the wafer is whole. It comes first because it is lithography, and lithography wants a stiff, flat, full-thickness wafer. A wire-bonded die needs no bumps and skips this step.
2. **Backgrinding** (both branches; Step 2). The 775 µm wafer is ground from the back to its final thickness. It comes after bumping (the bumps are protected by tape) and before dicing, because you cannot grind loose dies.
3. **Dicing** (both branches; Step 3). The wafer is cut into dies along the scribe streets, on tape stretched over a metal frame so nothing scatters.
4. **Die attach** (leadframe branch; Step 4). Each good die is picked off the tape and glued face-up, by its backside, onto the metal paddle of a leadframe or onto a substrate.
5. **Wire bonding** (leadframe branch; Step 5). Hair-fine wires are welded one at a time from the die's pads to the leadframe's fingers.
6. **Flip-chip join and underfill** (FCBGA branch, in place of Steps 4–5; Step 6). Each good die is placed bumps-down on its substrate and the solder is melted (reflowed) or pressed and heated (thermocompression). Epoxy underfill is drawn under the die immediately afterwards, because the CTE stress of WE-A begins at the very first cool-down.
7. **Lid attach or molding.** A copper lid is glued on over a thermal interface (FCBGA), or the die and its wires are encased in mold compound (leadframe parts, and some FCBGAs). It comes before the balls because the balls would remelt in any later heating.
8. **Marking.** A laser writes the part number and lot code.
9. **Ball attach** (BGA packages only). Solder spheres are placed and reflowed onto the substrate's bottom pads. Nearly last, so they see only one reflow before the customer's.
10. **Singulation.** The substrate strip is sawn into individual packages; leaded parts are punched free and their leads bent to shape instead.
11. **Package test.** Every unit is tested electrically (Module 18). Last, because only a finished unit can be tested the way the customer will use it.

Laid out side by side, the two branches share the front of the flow, diverge after dicing, and reconverge at the finish:

| Stage | FCBGA branch | Leadframe / wire-bond branch |
|---|---|---|
| Wafer level | Bump (Step 1) → backgrind (Step 2) → dice (Step 3) | Backgrind → dice (no bumps needed) |
| Attach | Flip-chip join, bumps down (Step 6) → underfill | Die attach, backside glued to a leadframe paddle, face up (Step 4) → wire bond (Step 5) |
| Encapsulate | Lid over TIM, or mold | Transfer mold over die and wires |
| Finish (reconverge) | Mark → ball attach → singulate → test | Mark → trim and form leads (or saw apart a strip of **QFN**, quad flat no-lead, packages, the commonest leadframe type) → test |

Steps 2 and 3 (grind, dice) are done on the whole wafer and are shared; the leadframe branch skips bumping (Step 1), and the two branches part at Step 4.

### Step 1: Wafer-Level Bumping

The die's terminals as they leave the fab are flat aluminum or copper pads, and a flat aluminum pad cannot be soldered to: aluminum grows a skin of aluminum oxide within seconds of meeting air, and molten solder will not bond to that oxide. The die therefore needs a raised, solderable terminal of controlled height on every pad, thousands per die, all the same height to within a few micrometers so that every one touches its substrate pad at once. **Bumping** builds those terminals on every die while the wafer is still whole. It is performed in a fab-like cleanroom using lithography and plating tools, and it is the one back-end step that TSMC has always kept largely in-house (at its AP2 facility in Tainan, among others) because it directly interfaces with the BEOL; OSATs such as ASE, Amkor and PTI also run large bumping lines.

The starting point is the wafer's final **passivation**, the glass (silicon nitride) or **polyimide** (a heat-resistant polymer spun on as a liquid and patterned like resist) skin over the top wiring, opened only at the pads. The steps:

1. **Under-bump metallurgy (UBM) sputtering.** The wafer is sputter-cleaned (an argon plasma knocks off the native Al₂O₃, the aluminum oxide skin) and a stack of ~0.1–0.2 µm **titanium** (or titanium-tungsten, TiW) followed by ~0.2–0.5 µm **copper** is sputtered over the entire wafer. **Sputtering** means argon ions knock atoms off a metal target so they rain onto the wafer as a thin film (Module 06). Titanium adheres to the passivation and pad and acts as a **diffusion barrier**, a layer that stops one metal dissolving into another; the copper is the **seed**, the thin conductive film that will carry the plating current. The UBM has three jobs: adhere to the pad, provide a solderable surface, and stop the solder from consuming the pad metal.
2. **Photoresist patterning.** A thick photoresist (liquid or dry-film, 50–120 µm thick) is coated and exposed with a **stepper** (a projection tool that prints one die-sized field at a time, Module 07) or a **laser direct imager (LDI)**, which scans a laser to write the pattern straight from the design file with no mask, to open a well over each pad. The well diameter sets the bump diameter.
3. **Electroplating.** Current passed through an acidic copper sulfate bath deposits copper onto whatever is wired as the **cathode** (the negative electrode, where dissolved metal ions pick up electrons and become solid metal), here the seed at the bottom of every well. For a **copper pillar** bump, Cu is plated up the well to ~30–50 µm tall, followed by an optional ~2–3 µm nickel barrier and a ~15–25 µm cap of **SnAg** solder (typically Sn–1.8 to 2.5 wt% Ag, where **wt%** is percent by weight, melting ~221–226 °C depending on silver content). Baths contain organic additives that level and brighten the deposit; current density is ~1–5 A/dm² (amps per square decimeter of wafer). Wafer-level plating tools come from Lam Research, Applied Materials, ACM Research and EBARA, among others. For classic **C4 bumps** ("Controlled Collapse Chip Connection", IBM's 1960s invention), the entire bump is solder, plated as a mushroom over a small UBM.
4. **Resist strip and seed etch.** The resist is stripped and the exposed Ti/Cu seed between bumps is wet-etched, electrically isolating each bump.
5. **Reflow.** The wafer passes through a nitrogen convection furnace above the solder melting point; the plated solder melts and surface tension pulls it into a smooth spherical cap. **Flux**, a mild organic acid, or formic acid vapor strips the tin oxide so the solder can **wet**: molten solder spreading over and bonding to a clean metal, as water spreads on clean glass but beads on wax.
6. **Inspection.** Automated optical inspection (Camtek, Onto, KLA) checks bump height (**coplanarity**: all bump tops in one plane, target ±3–5 µm across a die), missing bumps and bridging.

> **Worked example WE-C: how long does a pillar take to plate?** Faraday's law says the mass deposited is proportional to the charge passed. Copper has a molar mass of 63.5 g/mol, needs 2 electrons per atom, and has a density of 8.96 g/cm³; one mole of electrons carries 96,485 C (coulombs; one ampere for one second is one coulomb). At 1 A/dm² (0.01 A/cm²), one minute delivers 0.6 C per cm², which is 0.6 / (2 × 96,485) = 3.1 µmol of copper, or 0.197 mg. Spread over 1 cm² at 8.96 g/cm³ that is 0.197 mg / 8.96 g/cm³ ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻⁵ cm, or **0.22 µm per minute per A/dm²** (assuming all the current deposits copper). At a typical 3 A/dm² that is 0.66 µm/min, so a 40 µm pillar takes ~60 min, and the solder cap adds more. A plating chamber holds one wafer, so a line that must bump a few thousand wafers a month runs dozens of plating cells side by side: bumping is slow chemistry, not fast lithography.

Why copper pillars replaced solder-only C4: a solder ball's height scales with its diameter, so at fine pitch the bumps get short and the gap for underfill closes. A tall, narrow Cu pillar keeps a ~50–70 µm stand-off at 40–100 µm pitch and carries 5–10× more **current density** (amps per square millimeter of cross-section) before **electromigration**: at high current density the stream of electrons physically pushes metal atoms along the conductor, opening voids at one end and piling up **hillocks** (small mounds of displaced metal) at the other; tin atoms move far more easily than copper atoms, so copper survives currents that would void a solder bump. (The 5–10× is the practical design allowance for the pillar joint as a whole, which still ends in a solder cap; the two metals' own limits are about a hundred times apart, as WE-F in Step 6 shows.) Copper also conducts heat better. Today, C4-style solder bumps at ~130–150 µm pitch survive on cost-sensitive or coarse-pitch parts; Cu pillar at 100–130 µm pitch is standard for high-performance FCBGA; Cu-pillar **microbumps** (the same structure scaled down: 36–55 µm pitch for die-to-interposer joints, ~25–55 µm between the DRAM dies inside an HBM stack) connect dies to an **interposer**, a slab of silicon or organic film with fine wiring that sits between dies and substrate (Module 17), and join the dies of an HBM stack to one another; and **hybrid bonding**, copper pads pressed together and annealed so they fuse with no solder at all (Module 17), is expected to replace HBM microbumps from the HBM4E/HBM5 generation (Module 15).

Historically, C4 used high-lead solder (Pb–5Sn, melting ~310 °C), which stays solid through a later ball-attach reflow; the EU's **RoHS** directive (Restriction of Hazardous Substances, 2006) pushed the industry to lead-free SnAg and **SAC** alloys (tin-silver-copper; **SAC305** is 96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper, the standard lead-free solder).

> **What can go wrong:** **Missing bumps and bump voids**, from resist scum in a well or hydrogen bubbles trapped during plating, are found by optical inspection and X-ray. **Non-wet after reflow**: the oxide was not fully removed, so the cap stays a dull plated stub instead of a shiny sphere. **UBM undercut**: the seed etch attacks the titanium sideways beneath the pillar, so the bump leans or lifts under the slightest shear. **UBM consumption**: every reflow grows a copper-tin **intermetallic** (a hard, brittle compound of two metals in fixed ratio, here Cu₆Sn₅), and after several reflows a thin UBM can be eaten through to the pad. **Coplanarity out of spec** (beyond the ±3–5 µm target): the tallest bumps hold the die off its pads and the shortest never touch, giving open joints at flip-chip. The bump line is run like a fab line for exactly this reason: every wafer is inspected before it leaves.

### Step 2: Backgrinding

A 300 mm wafer is 775 µm thick because the front end needs that stiffness to survive ~1,000 process steps and hundreds of thermal cycles without warping or breaking. The package does not want it: thinner dies conduct heat with less resistance, fit in thinner phones, and stack more layers per millimeter. **Backgrinding** (also "thinning") removes most of the wafer's thickness from the back.

> **Intuition:** **in-feed grinding**, the method every wafer grinder uses (the wheel is fed straight down into a slowly turning wafer), is not like sanding a board. The grinding wheel is a cup whose rim is slightly wider than the wafer's radius, positioned so the rim passes through the wafer's center. The wafer turns slowly underneath on a vacuum chuck, so every point of the surface passes under the rim once per revolution, while the wheel is fed straight down at a set rate. Thickness is set by how far the wheel is fed, not by how long you grind, and the wheel only ever touches a line across the wafer, which is why a ground wafer shows fine radial arcs rather than random scratches.

The sequence, dominated worldwide by **Disco Corporation** of Japan (~70–80% share in grinders and dicing saws as of 2025, a trade-press estimate, with Tokyo Seimitsu/Accretech a distant second):

1. **Backgrinding tape lamination.** A **UV-release adhesive tape**, whose glue loses its tack under ultraviolet (UV) light (Nitto Denko, Lintec, Furukawa Electric) is laminated over the bumped front side to protect the bumps and pads from grinding slurry. For bumped wafers, the tape's adhesive layer is 100–200 µm thick so that it conforms around 50–70 µm bumps without leaving voids.
2. **Coarse grinding (Z1).** (Z1 and Z2 are the grinder's two spindle stations, one carrying a coarse wheel and one a fine wheel; a rotary table carries the wafer on its chuck from one to the other.) The wafer is vacuum-chucked face-down on a rotating porous ceramic chuck. A cup-shaped diamond wheel with coarse **grit** (a mesh number; higher means finer diamonds: #320–#600, roughly 20–40 µm diamonds held in a **bond**, the glass ("vitrified") or resin matrix that grips them) spins at ~2,000–4,000 rpm and is fed into the back at ~3–5 µm/s, removing ~500+ µm in a couple of minutes: at 4 µm/s, 500 µm takes ~2 min. Deionized water floods the interface to carry away **swarf** (ground-off silicon dust) and heat.
3. **Fine grinding (Z2).** A fine wheel (#2000–#8000 grit, ~2–5 µm diamonds) at feed ~0.3–1 µm/s removes the last 20–40 µm (30 µm at 0.5 µm/s is ~1 min). Coarse grinding leaves a **subsurface damage layer** of microcracks and **dislocations** (lines of misplaced atoms in the crystal lattice) ~10–20 µm deep; fine grinding shrinks it to ~1–3 µm.
4. **Stress relief.** Even the fine-grind damage layer halves the die's fracture strength and is a crack-initiation site. It is removed by one of: **dry polishing** (Disco's proprietary abrasive-free polishing pad), **chemical mechanical polishing (CMP)**, the slurry-and-pad polish the fab uses to flatten every layer, wet spin etching (HF/HNO₃, a hydrofluoric/nitric acid mixture, removing ~5–10 µm), or a downstream plasma etch in SF₆ (sulfur hexafluoride, the fluorine-donating etch gas of Module 09). A properly stress-relieved die has a **three-point-bend strength** (support a strip of die at both ends, press down on the middle until it snaps, and report the stress at the break) of ~1–2 GPa versus ~400–800 MPa for a ground-only die (MPa and GPa are mega- and gigapascals, units of stress; 1 GPa ≈ 10,000 atmospheres of pressure; structural steel yields at ~0.3–0.5 GPa), and a mirror backside that also happens to be ideal for laser marking or for a backside metallization if the die will be soldered.
5. **Tape removal and transfer to dicing tape.** The wafer, now floppy, is mounted back-side down onto dicing tape on a metal frame (a "film frame"). The backgrinding tape is exposed to UV light, which cross-links its adhesive so it hardens and loses its tack, and is peeled; the wafer goes to dicing.

Target thicknesses: ~300–700 µm for large FCBGA dies (a thick die helps warpage control and thermal spreading), ~100–200 µm for wire-bond and **WLCSP** parts (wafer-level chip-scale packages, dies carrying their own solder balls), 50–100 µm for smartphone memory and power **MOSFETs** (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors, the basic switch of power electronics), and ~30–50 µm for the DRAM dies in an HBM stack (Module 15). Power devices want thin silicon for a different reason: their current flows vertically through the whole wafer thickness, so every extra 100 µm of silicon is extra **on-resistance**, the resistance of the switch when it is turned on. The physical limit is not grinding but handling: below ~100 µm a 300 mm wafer sags like paper and cannot be picked up by conventional robots.

Disco's answer is the **TAIKO** process: the grinding wheel thins only the interior of the wafer, leaving an unground ~2–3 mm ring at the edge at the full 775 µm. The ring acts as a stiffening frame; the wafer can be transported, etched and even implanted with far less warpage and breakage. This is standard for **IGBT** (insulated-gate bipolar transistor) and power MOSFET wafers, the switches in motor drives and chargers, which need ~50–100 µm of silicon for low on-resistance. The ring is cut off at dicing.

For very thin wafers (HBM, 3D stacks), the industry instead **temporarily bonds** the wafer face-down to a glass or silicon carrier with a **thermoplastic** (a polymer that softens again when heated, so the carrier can later be slid off warm) or UV-release adhesive before grinding, and de-bonds it after all backside processing; the tools come from EV Group, SUSS MicroTec and TEL.

> **What can go wrong:** **Breakage**: an edge chip left by an earlier step, or a void in the tape around a bump, concentrates stress and the wafer cracks on the chuck; in dicing-before-grinding flows, where the wafer is part-cut first, the half-cut streets are the weak points. **Total thickness variation (TTV)**: the ground wafer is thicker on one side than the other, from chuck or wheel misalignment; targets are a few µm across 300 mm (an estimate; each product sets its own), and a wedge-shaped die will later sit tilted in its package. **Warpage from an unrelieved damage layer**: the damaged skin is in compression and bows the whole wafer, so skipping stress relief on a thin wafer yields a potato chip. **Tape residue** on bumps after peeling, which blocks wetting later. **Handling**: below ~100 µm the wafer cannot be moved without a ring or carrier. Detection is by thickness gauges on the grinder, warpage and TTV mapping, and optical inspection; the fix is almost always upstream (tape, chuck cleanliness, wheel dressing).

### Step 3: Dicing

**Dicing** (singulation of the wafer) cuts along the **scribe streets** (also "scribe lanes"), the ~50–100 µm wide lanes between dies that the fab reserved for test structures and alignment marks. Three technologies compete.

**Blade dicing** is the incumbent. A **hub-mounted blade** (the cutting ring is bonded to an aluminum hub that bolts straight onto the spindle, so the fragile ring is never handled bare), a ~20–50 µm thick ring of nickel **electroformed** (electroplated as a solid ring) around embedded diamond grit (~2–6 µm grains) for silicon, or a thicker resin-bond blade for harder materials, spins at 30,000–60,000 rpm on an **air-bearing** spindle (the shaft floats on a cushion of compressed air so nothing rubs at that speed) and is fed through the wafer at 50–150 mm/s. **Deionized (DI) water**, water with its ions removed so it leaves no residue, cools and flushes; but pure water does not conduct, so friction charges it and the static can zap circuits, and dissolving a little CO₂ makes it just conductive enough to bleed the charge away. The blade cuts either fully through into the dicing tape or leaves a few micrometers that the tape expansion breaks. The **kerf**, the material lost to the cut, is 20–50 µm, so streets can be as narrow as ~60 µm. Typical throughput is a 300 mm wafer in 5–15 minutes, depending on die count. Blades are **dressed** (run into a sacrificial abrasive board to wear away clogged nickel and expose fresh diamonds) and replaced after some thousands of meters of cut.

The BEOL made blade dicing harder. Low-k dielectrics (Module 12) are porous, weak and poorly adhered; a blade rips through them and starts delaminations that propagate, in later thermal cycling, under the **seal ring**, the stacked wall of metal and **vias** (vertical metal plugs that connect one wiring layer to the next) built in the BEOL around every die's edge to stop cracks and moisture. The industry's fix is **laser grooving**: a UV (355 nm) laser with nanosecond or picosecond pulses (the **pulse length** is how long each flash of light lasts) **ablates** (vaporizes) a ~10–20 µm deep groove through the metal and dielectric stack in the street before the blade cuts only through the bare silicon; the shorter the pulse, the less time heat has to spread sideways into the low-k stack. Disco and others sell dual-station tools that groove and cut in one pass. A related technique is **step cutting** with two spindles: a wider blade removes the top layers and a thinner blade finishes the cut.

The second technology, **stealth dicing**, cuts from inside the wafer: a laser writes a plane of damage below the surface, and the wafer is then snapped along it.

> **Intuition:** stealth dicing is a magnifying glass focusing sunlight inside a block of ice: the surface stays cold while the interior at the focus is scored. The honest version: silicon absorbs almost nothing at 1,064 nm at ordinary intensity, but at the focus the intensity is so extreme that two photons can be absorbed at once, so energy is deposited only in the tiny volume where the beam is tightest.

Stealth dicing was invented by Hamamatsu Photonics (developed ~2002–2005, patented, and licensed to Disco and others). A pulsed near-infrared laser, typically 1,064 nm, is used because silicon is nearly transparent there: a photon must carry more energy than a semiconductor's **band gap** (1.12 eV for silicon) to be absorbed, and a 1,064 nm photon carries 1.17 eV, only just above it, so ordinary absorption is weak and the light travels on the order of a millimeter before most of it is absorbed, further than the wafer is thick. The beam is focused by a **high-numerical-aperture (NA)** objective (a lens that focuses very steeply, to a spot a few µm across) to a point *inside* the wafer, tens of micrometers below the surface. At the focus the intensity is high enough for **multi-photon absorption**, in which two photons arriving together supply the energy one cannot; the silicon there is locally melted, recrystallized and cracked into a ~10–20 µm tall stealth-dicing ("SD") layer of modified material, while the surface above and below stays untouched. The laser scans the street at hundreds of mm/s, writing a plane of these defects; for thick wafers, several passes at different depths are stacked. Then the dicing tape is stretched radially on an **expander** (a ring that pushes the tape outward) by ~10–20%, and the wafer cleaves cleanly along the defect planes. There is no kerf, no water, no debris, no chipping and no blade wear; the die edges are near-atomically clean. Stealth dicing dominates for thin wafers (memory, and image sensors, whose front-side **micro-lenses**, a tiny lens over each pixel, cannot tolerate water) and dies with narrow streets (~15–30 µm). Its limitation is that anything opaque in the street (metal test pads) blocks the beam, so fabs must keep streets clear or groove them first.

**Plasma dicing** treats the streets as an etch pattern. A photoresist or water-soluble mask is applied and opened over the streets (or, on some flows, the passivation itself is used as the mask), and a **Bosch process** deep reactive ion etch (alternate a few seconds of SF₆ plasma etching with a few seconds of Teflon-like C₄F₈ coating, so each cycle etches only downward, exactly as in Module 09) etches all streets on the wafer simultaneously, clear through 50–100 µm of silicon in tens of minutes. Since every street etches at once, throughput is independent of die count, which makes plasma dicing compelling for wafers with tens of thousands of tiny dies: **RFID** tags (radio-frequency identification chips the size of a grain of sand), **LEDs** (light-emitting diodes), **MEMS** (micro-electro-mechanical systems, chip-scale mechanical sensors such as accelerometers) and small power devices. Streets can be ~10 µm wide, die edges are smooth and rounded, and shapes need not be rectangular. Tools come from Panasonic Connect (the APX300 series), SPTS (KLA) and Plasma-Therm. Adoption has been slow because the mask step adds cost and any metal in the streets stops the etch.

> **Worked example WE-D: dicing time, and when plasma wins.** A 300 mm wafer of 10 × 10 mm dies needs ~30 cut lines in each direction, ~60 cuts of up to ~300 mm each, roughly 18 m of cut. At 100 mm/s that is ~3 min of blade time; add indexing, the 90° rotation and the edge passes and the wafer takes ~5–8 min. Halve the die to 5 × 5 mm and the cut count doubles to ~120, so blade time doubles too, and at 2 mm dies it is five times the original. Plasma etches all streets at once: a 100 µm wafer takes tens of minutes whether it carries 600 dies or 60,000. Draw both lines against die size and they cross somewhere in the few-millimeter range; below it, plasma dicing is faster as well as gentler, which is why it is chosen for wafers of tens of thousands of tiny dies and not for GPUs.

> **What can go wrong:** **Front-side chipping**: the blade's exit tears out fragments of passivation and top metal; the specification is typically < 10–25 µm, and it must never reach the seal ring. **Backside chipping**: the exit face flakes, worse on thin wafers and with a worn blade; both are found by optical inspection of the die edges. **Low-k delamination**: a blade through the BEOL starts cracks that creep under the seal ring during later thermal cycling and show up only as field failures, which is why laser grooving exists. **Blade wear and loading**: a clogged blade rubs instead of cutting and chipping rises; the fix is dressing or replacement on a metered schedule. **Metal in the streets**: it blocks the stealth beam and stops the plasma etch, so street design is negotiated between fab and packaging house before tape-out. **Die strength**: a blade-diced edge is a field of microcracks, and a plasma-diced die is roughly 2× stronger in bending, which matters for 50 µm dies that will be stacked.

After dicing, the film frame goes to a die bonder or flip-chip bonder, which picks dies from the tape one by one; a needle-less **ejector** pushes each die up from below the tape while a **vacuum collet** (a small nozzle whose soft, die-sized tip grips the die's top face by suction) lifts it from above.

### Step 4: Die Attach and Pick-and-Place

For a wire-bonded or leadframe package the die is mounted face-up, and something must fix its backside to the substrate or to the **paddle** (the central metal island of a leadframe). The layer must be thin and free of voids, must carry heat and, for power parts, current, must survive the 175 °C of molding and the 260 °C of board reflow, and must not creep onto the bond pads. **Die attach** is the choice of that layer and the machine that places the die into it. The materials:

- **Epoxy paste.** A silver-flake-filled (conductive, 2–10 W/m·K) or silica-filled (insulating) epoxy dispensed in a pattern on the paddle; the die is pressed in and the epoxy cured at 150–175 °C for ~1 h. The **bond line** (the cured layer) is typically ~20–50 µm thick, with a small **fillet**, the ramp of adhesive that climbs the die edge. Cheap and universal.
- **Die attach film (DAF).** A ~10–25 µm thermosetting film (Resonac, formerly Hitachi Chemical; Nitto Denko; Lintec; Furukawa) laminated onto the wafer backside *before* dicing, as a combined dicing-tape/DAF stack. Each die comes off the tape with its own perfectly uniform adhesive layer, with no fillet and no **bleed** (resin seeping onto pads), essential for stacked memory (up to 16 dies at 20–30 µm each in a microSD card or **eMMC**, embedded multimedia card, the soldered-down flash storage of cheap phones).
- **Eutectic AuSi.** A gold preform or gold-plated backside heated above 363 °C forms a gold-silicon **eutectic**, a mixture that melts sharply at a temperature lower than either pure component; **hermetic** (sealed against gas and moisture, since there is no polymer to absorb water) and highly conductive but expensive; now mostly for **RF** (radio-frequency) and hermetic military parts.
- **Soft solder.** Lead-based or lead-free (SnSb, SnAgCu) solder for power devices needing a low-resistance electrical and thermal path from the backside terminal (the **drain** of a MOSFET or **collector** of an IGBT, the electrode the load current leaves through).
- **Sintered silver.** A paste of micro- or nano-silver particles is pressed (10–30 MPa) at 200–250 °C; the particles **sinter**, fusing into a solid below their melting point like snow packing into ice, forming a porous silver layer (~200 W/m·K, melting point 961 °C) without ever melting. Used for SiC (silicon carbide) and GaN (gallium nitride) power modules in electric vehicles because it survives **junction temperatures** (the temperature of the transistors themselves) of 175–200 °C that would age solder to death.

The bonder (Besi, ASMPT, K&S, Palomar for high-accuracy) picks the die, images it and the target with cameras, and places it. Accuracy is quoted at **3σ**, three standard deviations, meaning 99.7% of placements land within the figure:

| Technology | Placement accuracy (3σ) | Throughput | Why that accuracy is enough |
|---|---|---|---|
| Die attach (wire bond, leadframe) | ±10–25 µm | 10,000–20,000 units/h | Wires are bonded afterwards to wherever the pads ended up |
| Flip-chip mass reflow | ±5–10 µm | Thousands of units/h | Molten solder self-aligns the die (Step 6) |
| Thermocompression bonding (TCB), microbumps at 36–40 µm pitch | ±1–2 µm | ~1,000–2,000 units/h | Solder is pressed, not free to move; the die is held flat at temperature |
| Hybrid bonding (Module 17) | ±0.1–0.2 µm | Hundreds of units/h | Copper pads a few µm wide must overlap directly |

> **Worked example WE-J: what the die-attach layer costs in heat, and what a void does to it.** The bond line is the first layer the die's heat crosses on its way out through the paddle, so use the same R = t / (k·A) as job 3. Take a 5 × 5 mm power MOSFET die (A = 25 mm² = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²) dissipating 10 W.
> Silver-filled epoxy, 25 µm bond line, k = 3 W/m·K: R = 25 × 10⁻⁶ / (3 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ **0.33 K/W**, so the die runs 3.3 °C hotter than the paddle.
> Soft solder, 50 µm (solder bond lines are thicker because a preform or paste must fill the gap), k ≈ 50 W/m·K: R ≈ **0.04 K/W**, 0.4 °C.
> Sintered silver, 30 µm, k ≈ 200 W/m·K: R ≈ **0.006 K/W**, 0.06 °C.
> Epoxy is fine for a 10 W part; at 100 W the same 0.33 K/W is 33 °C, which is why power modules pay for solder or sintered silver. Now the void. A void covering 10% of the area removes 10% of the conducting cross-section, so the average resistance rises only to 0.33 / 0.9 ≈ 0.37 K/W. That is why a 10% void limit is tolerable on average. The danger is local: heat generated above the void cannot go straight down and must travel sideways through the silicon to the nearest attached region, so the transistors directly over a void run hotter than the average suggests, and in a power device whose load current also flows down through the same layer, the current crowds into the edge of the void, heating it further. A void under the hottest cell of a MOSFET is therefore a failure site even when the part passes its average thermal spec, and C-SAM maps look for the largest single void, not just the total.

> **What can go wrong:** **Voids** in the bond line (trapped air or solvent) are thermal hot spots and, for power parts, current-crowding sites; they are found by X-ray or by **C-SAM** (scanning acoustic microscopy, which bounces ultrasound off internal interfaces and images the ones that reflect like an air gap), with limits typically under ~10% of the area (specifications vary by product). **Die tilt** from an uneven paste pattern leaves one edge high, so the wire bonder's programmed loop heights are wrong across the die. **Resin bleed** onto bond pads gives non-stick bonds. **DAF residue or delamination** becomes a steam pocket at molding or reflow. **Die crack** from excessive bond force on a thin die, or from a chipped edge finding its stress concentration.

### Step 5: Wire Bonding

**Wire bonding** draws a thin metal wire from a die pad to a substrate or leadframe finger, one loop at a time. It was the first interconnect technology (1950s), and it is still, by unit count, the majority: as of ~2025 an estimated ~70–80% of all packaged semiconductor devices (roughly a trillion units a year including discretes, LEDs and sensors) are wire-bonded. The tools come overwhelmingly from **Kulicke & Soffa (K&S)** and **ASMPT**, with Shinkawa (Yamaha Robotics) third.

The dominant process is **thermosonic ball-and-stitch bonding**, so called because it uses heat, ultrasound and pressure together:

1. The wire, 15–25 µm in diameter (0.6–1.0 **mil**, one mil being a thousandth of an inch), runs through a ceramic **capillary** with a chamfered tip.
2. An **electronic flame-off (EFO)** spark melts the wire tip into a **free air ball (FAB)** about twice the wire diameter.
3. The capillary descends onto the die pad, which is heated to ~150–250 °C. It presses the ball with ~20–100 **gf** (gram-force, the weight of one gram, ~0.01 N, so 100 gf ≈ 1 N) while a **piezoelectric transducer**, a crystal that changes shape when a voltage is applied, driven at 60–140 kHz, vibrates the capillary by less than a micrometer, scrubbing the oxide off. Heat, pressure and vibration together form a metallurgical weld (for gold on aluminum, a thin gold-aluminum **intermetallic**, a hard compound of the two metals in fixed ratio that grows where they touch and keeps growing with heat). This is the **ball bond**.
4. The capillary rises and traces a programmed loop shape, then descends on the substrate finger and presses the wire sideways under the capillary face, crushing and welding it into a **stitch bond** (or wedge), then tears the wire, leaving a tail for the next ball.
5. Repeat at ~15–25 wires per second.

Loops are 80–150 µm tall and up to ~5 mm long; a 1,000-pin wire-bond BGA is feasible but ungainly, which is why high pin counts moved to flip chip.

> **Worked example WE-E: what a wire costs electrically.** A 25 µm copper wire 3 mm long has a cross-section A = π × (12.5 µm)² ≈ 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m²; with copper's **resistivity** ρ = 1.7 µΩ·cm (its resistance per unit length per unit cross-section), R = ρL/A = 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m × 3 × 10⁻³ m / 4.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m² ≈ **0.10 Ω**. A thin wire in free air has an inductance of roughly 1 nH per mm, so ~**3 nH** (a **nanohenry** is a billionth of a henry; 1 nH at 1 A/ns produces 1 V). Consequences: 1 A through the wire drops 0.1 V, an eighth of a 0.8 V supply, and a 1 A/ns current step across 3 nH produces V = L·dI/dt = 3 × 10⁻⁹ H × 10⁹ A/s = **3 V**, several times the supply. That is why power devices use 100–500 µm aluminum wires or ribbon, and why a 1,000 A GPU cannot be wire-bonded at all. A copper pillar bump 40 µm tall and 50 µm in diameter has A ≈ 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ m², so R ≈ 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ × 4 × 10⁻⁵ / 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ ≈ **0.35 mΩ**, and ~**0.05 nH**: about 300× and 60× lower than the wire.

The **gold-to-copper transition** is the biggest change in wire bonding's history. Gold wire (99.99%) was the standard for 50 years because it does not oxidize and bonds easily. When gold crossed ~$1,000/oz around 2008–2010, the industry moved to **copper wire**: ~5× cheaper, ~25% lower resistivity (so a given wire drops less voltage and carries more current), and a slower-growing intermetallic with aluminum. But copper oxidizes, so the free air ball must be formed under **forming gas** (95% N₂ / 5% H₂), whose hydrogen scavenges any oxygen so the molten ball cannot oxidize; and copper is harder than gold, so bonding needs more force and risks **cratering** (fracturing the pad and the dielectric beneath it). Fabs responded with thicker aluminum pads and stiffer under-pad structures; wire makers with **palladium-coated copper (PCC)** wire. Today most wire bonds are Cu or PCC; gold survives for RF, high-reliability and very fine-pitch (< 40 µm) work; silver alloy wire has a niche in LEDs and memory.

For power devices, **wedge bonding** with thick aluminum wire (100–500 µm) or aluminum/copper ribbon carries tens of amps; it is ultrasonic only (no heat, no ball) and forms both bonds as wedges.

> **What can go wrong:** **Non-stick on pad (NSOP)**: the ball does not weld, from pad contamination, resin bleed or a pad oxide too thick to scrub off. **Lifted stitch**: the second bond peels from a finger with poor plating. **Cratering** under a copper ball on a fragile low-k stack. **Wire sweep** at molding, when the flowing compound pushes adjacent loops into contact (Molding section). **Purple plague**: with gold wire on aluminum pads, hours at high temperature grow the purple Au-Al intermetallic until the bond goes brittle and open; with copper on aluminum the risk is instead **Kirkendall voids**, gaps left where one metal diffuses into the other faster than the reverse. Detection: **ball-shear** and **wire-pull** tests on sample units (push a ball sideways, or hook and pull a loop, and record the force at failure; minimums are a few gf for 25 µm wire, set per product), X-ray for sweep, and high-temperature storage for the intermetallic failures (Reliability section).

### Step 6: Flip Chip: Mass Reflow and Thermocompression

In **flip chip** the bumped die is turned face-down and its bumps are joined directly to pads on the substrate. There is no wire, so the interconnect is shorter (~70 µm vs. millimeters) and far lower in inductance (~0.05 nH vs. ~1–2 nH per wire, as WE-E showed), and, critically, the entire die area is available for connections, not just the perimeter: **area-array** I/O (input/output). Flip chip is the only way to get 5,000+ connections off a die. Substrate pads are typically copper with either a **solder-on-pad (SOP)** pre-coat, a small solder bump pre-placed on the pad, or an **organic solderability preservative (OSP)** finish, a thin organic film that keeps the copper from oxidizing until reflow.

**Mass reflow (MR)** is the high-volume process:

1. The die is dipped into a thin film of tacky **flux** (an organic acid such as adipic acid in a solvent carrier) so that each bump tip carries flux.
2. A pick-and-place head sets the die on the substrate, bumps against pads. The flux's tackiness holds it.
3. Substrates travel through a convection reflow oven, typically 7–10 zones in nitrogen (< 1,000 ppm O₂), with a profile that **soaks** at 150–190 °C (holds the whole assembly at one temperature so the flux can clean the surfaces before the solder melts), peaks at 240–250 °C for SAC/SnAg, with an illustrative40–90 s above the alloy's liquidus. The **liquidus** is the temperature above which an alloy is fully molten (about220–221 °C for SAC305; melting starts at its roughly217 °C solidus), and that **time above liquidus** is chosen to be long enough for the solder to wet and grow a thin intermetallic, and short enough that the intermetallic does not thicken and go brittle.
4. As the solder melts, **self-alignment** occurs (Intuition box below): the molten solder minimizes its surface energy by wetting the full pad on both sides, and the surface tension (~0.5 N/m for molten tin, about seven times water's 0.07 N/m, so a molten bump pulls much harder for its size than a water drop does) pulls a misplaced die back into alignment by up to roughly half a pad width. This is why mass reflow tolerates ±10 µm placement.
5. Flux residues are cleaned with DI water or a **saponifier**, an alkaline cleaner that turns flux residue into soap-like, water-soluble compounds, since residual acid under the die would cause corrosion and would stop underfill from adhering.

> **Intuition:** a drop of water on a clean coin pulls itself into a dome centered on the coin, because surface tension wants the smallest surface for its volume and the water wets the metal but not the table. Molten solder on a pad does the same, and a few thousand drops all pulling toward their own pad centers drag the whole die into place. Where the picture breaks: the drops are joined to a rigid die, so they cannot each center independently; the die settles at the position that best satisfies all of them, which is also why a badly warped die or substrate defeats self-alignment.

**Thermocompression bonding (TCB)** replaces the oven. A heated bond head picks the die, aligns it to ±1–2 µm, presses it onto the substrate (already at 100–150 °C) with a controlled force of a few to a few tens of newtons, and pulses the head to 250–300 °C for 1–3 seconds so the solder melts under pressure and then cools while still clamped. The advantages: the head holds a thin, warped die flat during joining; the bump gap is set by the tool rather than by solder collapse, so fine-pitch bumps (< 50 µm) do not bridge; and it can bond dies that are too large or too thin for mass reflow. The disadvantages are cycle time (~10–30 s per die versus a continuous oven) and tool cost (~$1–3 million per Besi or ASMPT TCB bonder; Hanmi Semiconductor and Hanwha Semitech build the TC bonders that stack HBM at SK hynix, while Semes serves Samsung).

**Underfill.** After joining, the die stands 50–70 µm above the substrate on an array of bumps, with the CTE problem from WE-A waiting to happen. **Capillary underfill (CUF)** is a low-viscosity epoxy filled with ~50–70 wt% spherical silica (particles < 1–5 µm, well under the gap) to bring its CTE from ~60 down to ~25–35 ppm/K, close to solder's. It is dispensed with a needle along one or two edges of the die while the assembly sits at 70–110 °C to lower viscosity; capillary action pulls it under the die in 10–60 s. It is then cured at 150–165 °C for ~1–2 h. A good underfill has no voids (voids concentrate stress and trap moisture, and become steam bombs at reflow), a uniform fillet at the die edges, and a Tg (defined in job 5 above) above the maximum operating temperature. Fill time grows with die size squared, because capillary flow slows as the front advances: the same surface tension must pull the liquid through an ever longer channel of viscous drag. For 30+ mm dies the dispensing pattern and material are therefore tuned carefully, or the industry switches to **molded underfill (MUF)**, mold compound that underfills and encapsulates in one shot (Molding section), or to **non-conductive paste (NCP)** and **non-conductive film (NCF)**, applied *before* bonding and cured in the TCB head. Samsung's and Micron's HBM stacks use TC-NCF, while SK hynix uses MR-MUF, mass reflow with molded underfill (Module 15).

> **Worked example WE-F: where 1,000 A actually meets resistance.** Take the 0.35 mΩ copper pillar of WE-E and add its solder cap (tin alloys are several times more resistive than copper, but the cap is only about half the pillar's height), and call the whole bump **~0.7 mΩ**. A large GPU die might devote ~20,000 of its bumps to power and ground (an illustrative count; real designs vary). Twenty thousand 0.7 mΩ resistors in parallel give 0.7 mΩ / 20,000 ≈ **0.035 µΩ**, so the bump layer is essentially invisible against the sub-0.1 mΩ budget of job 2, and the resistance that matters lives in the substrate's plated through-holes, planes and balls. But look at each bump alone: 1,000 A / 20,000 ≈ **50 mA**, through a 50 µm diameter (1.96 × 10⁻⁵ cm²), is ~**2.5 kA/cm²**. Set that against the two materials' limits, quoted here only as orders of magnitude: tin-based solder begins to electromigrate over a product's life at current densities of order ~10⁴ A/cm² (~10 kA/cm²), while copper tolerates of order ~10⁶ A/cm², about a hundred times more. An all-solder bump at 2.5 kA/cm² would sit within a factor of ~4 of its limit, with no margin for the spots where current crowds into one edge of the bump as it turns the corner from the wiring above, whereas the copper pillar is hundreds of times below its own limit: it is why the pillar is copper and only the cap is solder. (Step 1's "5–10× more current density" is the practical design allowance for the pillar joint as a whole, whose weakest link is still the solder cap and the intermetallic beneath it, not the ratio of the two material limits.)

> **What can go wrong:** **Non-wet opens**: a bump whose oxide the flux never removed sits on its pad without joining; found by X-ray (the joint looks round instead of collapsed) and electrical test. **Bridging**: too much solder, or two bumps too close, merge into a short. **Head-in-pillow**: bump and pad solder both melt, but a flux-starved oxide skin keeps them from merging, so the bump rests in a dimple on the pad like a head on a pillow; it passes test and fails in the field, and only a cross-section proves it. **White bumps**, or **chip-package interaction (CPI)**: the CTE stress of WE-A is transmitted through the corner bumps into the low-k BEOL beneath them and delaminates it; the name comes from the bright spot each delamination shows under C-SAM, and it links straight back to the weak dielectrics of Module 12. **Underfill voids and delamination**, from moisture, contamination or a dispensing pattern that traps air under a big die; C-SAM finds them. **TCB die tilt and NCF voids**, when the bond head is not parallel to the substrate or the film traps air. Detection is X-ray, C-SAM and cross-section; the fix is usually flux, profile or material, rarely the machine.

> **Why it matters downstream:** TCB's 1,000–2,000 units per hour, against a mass-reflow oven that never stops, is why the makers of HBM stacking bonders (Hanmi, Hanwha, Semes) became a bottleneck in Modules 15 and 17: a 12-high HBM stack is twelve TCB placements, and every HBM4 stack the world needs must pass through a fleet of such bonders that is counted in the hundreds, not thousands (an order-of-magnitude estimate from trade press).

## The Substrate in Depth

Underneath every FCBGA is a **build-up laminate substrate**, a miniature multilayer PCB manufactured to tolerances a PCB shop could not hit. Its layer count is set by a routing problem: ten thousand signals must escape from under a 30 mm die through channels between via pads that fit only ~3 traces each at today's 10 µm lines, so escaping them all takes several signal layers, each wanting a copper plane beside it, on both sides of a core (worked example WE-G at the end of this section does the arithmetic). For a large GPU it costs on the order of $100–300 apiece and is often the most expensive single component in the package after the silicon itself.

> **Intuition:** the substrate is a multi-storey car park. Each floor is a wiring layer, the ramps between floors are vias, and cars (signals) enter from the die at the top and must spread out to exits (balls) at the bottom without any two using the same lane. The **semi-additive process** described below builds each floor by growing copper walls only where a stencil is open, rather than carving lanes out of a solid slab, which is what lets the walls be 8 µm wide.

### Anatomy

Reading from the center outward:

**Core.** A rigid sheet of glass-fiber cloth impregnated with epoxy resin, typically **BT (bismaleimide triazine)** resin from Mitsubishi Gas Chemical or a **high-Tg** epoxy (one that stays rigid to ~170–200 °C), 0.4–1.2 mm thick for large packages (thinner, ~0.1–0.2 mm, for mobile). The glass cloth pins the in-plane CTE to ~10–15 ppm/K and gives stiffness. Copper foil is laminated on both faces. **Plated through-holes (PTHs)** are mechanically drilled (carbide bits, ~100–250 µm diameter, thousands of holes per minute on multi-spindle machines) or laser-drilled for finer sizes; the hole walls are made conductive by **electroless copper** (copper deposited from a chemical bath with no external current, triggered by palladium atoms seeded onto the surface as an **activator**, which is what lets copper coat an insulator), then electroplated, then the holes are plugged with resin and capped with copper so that build-up vias can be stacked over them.

**Build-up layers.** On each side of the core, N layers of dielectric and copper are added one at a time; an "8-2-8" substrate has 8 build-up layers on top, a 2-layer core, and 8 below, 18 metal layers in all. Each build-up cycle:

1. **Laminate ABF.** **Ajinomoto Build-up Film (ABF)** is a ~25–40 µm thick, uncured sheet of epoxy resin loaded with ~60–75 wt% sub-micron spherical silica filler, supplied on a PET (polyester) film carrier. It is vacuum-laminated at ~100–120 °C so it flows into the copper pattern below without voids, then thermally cured at ~170–190 °C.
2. **Laser-drill microvias.** A CO₂ laser (Via Mechanics, Mitsubishi Electric) or UV laser drills blind vias ~50–70 µm in diameter (down to ~30 µm with UV) through the ABF to the copper pad below, at rates of thousands of holes per second.
3. **Desmear and roughen.** The laser leaves a film of charred, re-deposited resin on the via bottom, the **smear**, which would insulate the via from the pad beneath it. Permanganate (KMnO₄), a strong oxidizer, etches that smear away (**desmear**) and at the same time dissolves the resin skin of the ABF surface, leaving the silica filler particles protruding as a micro-rough texture. This roughness is what makes electroless copper adhere; it is the reason ABF is filled the way it is.
4. **Electroless copper seed** (~0.5–1 µm) over the entire surface, catalyzed by the palladium activator.
5. **Semi-additive process (SAP) patterning.** A dry-film photoresist (~15–25 µm) is laminated and exposed with **laser direct imaging (LDI)** (Orbotech/KLA, SCREEN, Via Mechanics), which writes the pattern directly from the **CAD** (computer-aided design) layout file and can compensate for the panel's shrinkage, essential because a panel of substrates moves by tens of micrometers between layers. The resist is developed, opening the traces and vias.
6. **Pattern electroplating.** Copper is electroplated ~10–15 µm thick only where the resist is open, filling the microvias and forming the traces.
7. **Strip and flash etch.** The resist is stripped and the thin seed between traces is quickly etched away, leaving isolated conductors. Ordinary PCBs use **subtractive etching**: start from a full copper sheet and etch away the unwanted parts; the acid **undercuts** sideways as it goes down, so lines cannot be narrower than about the copper thickness. Because the SAP seed is under a micrometer thick, its undercut is negligible, which is why SAP achieves far finer lines.

Repeat N times. The result is copper lines and spaces (**L/S**, the trace width and the gap to its neighbor) of ~8–10 µm / 8–10 µm in high-volume ABF substrates as of ~2025, with leading lines at ~5/5 µm. Roadmaps target 2/2 µm, which requires a sputtered Ti/Cu seed (thinner and more uniform than electroless), thinner and smoother dielectric films, higher-resolution LDI, and cleanroom-class handling. Some vendors market this as modified or advanced SAP. Confusingly, on the PCB side **mSAP** means a related process that starts from a thin laminated copper foil and is used for the ~30/30 µm substrate-like PCBs in smartphones. A related design trick is **via-in-pad** and **stacked vias**: vias placed directly on the pads, and directly on top of one another layer after layer, so a signal can go straight down without wasting routing area. Stacked vias are demanding because each via must be plated completely solid so the next can sit on it, and any void becomes a crack under thermal cycling.

**Solder resist.** A photo-imageable epoxy (Taiyo Ink dominates) ~15–25 µm thick covers the outer copper, opened only at the bump pads on top and the ball pads on the bottom. The opening is either **solder-mask-defined (SMD)**, where the mask overlaps the pad and defines the wettable area, or **non-solder-mask-defined (NSMD)**, where the pad is smaller than the opening and the solder wets its sides; NSMD gives a stronger joint, SMD better tolerances.

**Surface finish.** Bare copper oxidizes, so pads are finished with **ENIG** (electroless nickel ~3–6 µm, a barrier against copper dissolution into solder, plus **immersion gold** ~0.05–0.1 µm, gold deposited by displacement of nickel atoms and therefore self-limiting at ~0.1 µm, to keep it solderable), **ENEPIG** (adds ~0.05–0.3 µm of electroless palladium between the nickel and gold, which prevents **black pad**, nickel under the gold that has been over-corroded during gold plating and makes brittle joints, and makes the pad both solderable and gold-wire-bondable), or OSP. For copper-pillar flip chip, substrate pads are often pre-coated with a small solder bump (SOP) to give the pillar's thin SnAg cap enough solder volume. High-speed signal traces are built to **controlled impedance**. A trace running over a copper plane is a **transmission line**: a fast voltage edge travelling along it drags a current with it, and the ratio of that voltage to that current, the line's **characteristic impedance**, is set by the geometry, mainly the trace's width and its height above the plane (wider, or closer to the plane, means lower impedance). Wherever that ratio changes, at a via, a narrowing, or a gap in the plane beneath, part of the edge bounces back toward the source and the rest carries on distorted, so designers hold the ratio constant along the whole path at a convention such as 50 Ω. In the substrate that means each high-speed trace runs at a fixed width and a fixed distance above a solid copper **reference plane** for its whole length.

### Why ABF, and Why a Food Company

Ajinomoto is a Tokyo food and amino-acid company (monosodium glutamate, seasonings). In the 1990s its fine-chemicals unit, drawing on epoxy-resin and hardener chemistry it had researched since the 1970s as an offshoot of amino-acid work, developed a film-form build-up dielectric with Intel as the launch customer; the film was first adopted in 1999, in Intel's Pentium III-era flip-chip packages. Before ABF, build-up layers were liquid resins that had to be coated, or photo-imageable dielectrics with poor thermal properties. ABF's combination of film form (uniform thickness, dry handling), laser drillability, low **dielectric loss** (the energy a signal loses heating the insulator, which matters at GHz), tight CTE control from its silica filler, and above all the reproducible permanganate roughening that gives copper adhesion, made it the standard. Thirty years of tuning (the GX, GY and low-loss GZ series) and joint qualification with every substrate maker created a moat: switching dielectric means re-qualifying every substrate design for every chip that uses it. Ajinomoto is estimated to hold well over 90% of the build-up film market (as of 2025), and it sells to substrate makers, not chipmakers. Competitors (Sekisui, Taiyo, Resonac) and photo-imageable dielectrics for **RDL**-based packages (redistribution layers, the thin-film wiring of the fan-out packages described later in this module) have taken slivers.

### The Substrate Industry and the 2021 Shortage

Substrate makers sit outside both the chip firms and the OSATs (as of 2025):

| Company | Country | Notable |
|---|---|---|
| Ibiden | Japan | Technology leader; supplier to Intel for decades and to NVIDIA for its largest GPUs; plants in Ogaki (Gifu) and a new one in Ono |
| Shinko Electric | Japan | Intel's other main supplier; taken private in 2024–2025 by a Japan Investment Corp-led consortium |
| Unimicron | Taiwan | Largest by ABF revenue; key supplier to NVIDIA and AMD; a fire at its Guanyin plant in October 2020 helped trigger the shortage |
| Kinsus (Pegatron group), Nan Ya PCB | Taiwan | High-volume FCBGA and mobile substrates |
| AT&S | Austria | Plants in Leoben and Chongqing, plus a new Kulim, Malaysia plant built largely for AMD |
| Samsung Electro-Mechanics, LG Innotek | Korea | FCBGA for Samsung, AMD and others |
| Shennan Circuits, Zhuhai Access, others | China | Mostly lower-layer-count parts |

Between 2020 and 2022 the industry learned that substrates are the easiest link in the chain to under-invest in. ABF substrate capacity grows in one-to-two-year plant projects with low margins in ordinary times, so makers had added little through the late 2010s. When pandemic-era PC, server and 5G demand arrived, and chip firms began using larger, higher-layer-count substrates (each large substrate consuming panel area that used to hold several small ones), lead times stretched beyond 30–50 weeks. Intel and AMD both cited substrate supply as their binding constraint in 2021 earnings calls, and Ajinomoto ran ABF film lines flat-out. Ibiden, Unimicron and AT&S each announced capacity expansions worth ¥100+ billion, and by 2023 a glut had arrived for mainstream parts, while the very largest AI-accelerator substrates remained tight.

Those AI substrates are extreme. A **reticle** is the largest area one lithography exposure can print, ~26 × 33 mm (Module 17), and big packages are now sized in multiples of it. As of early 2026: an H100-class package is roughly 70–75 mm on a side; Blackwell-class assemblies use **CoWoS-L** (the variant of TSMC's CoWoS that embeds small silicon bridge chips in an organic interposer, Module 17) at its current 80 × 80 mm substrate limit; and TSMC's 9.5× reticle CoWoS for 2027 calls for a 120 × 150 mm substrate (Module 17). These substrates have 20+ metal layers (10-2-10 or more), thick cores of ~1 mm or more for stiffness, and hundreds of embedded decoupling capacitors. Yield of such a part is a compounding product of per-layer yields, and the count of good units per 510 mm × 515 mm panel is only a few dozen.

**Glass core substrates** are the next step. Intel announced in September 2023 that it would replace the glass-fiber/epoxy core with a sheet of glass, with deployment targeted for the second half of the decade; at the NEPCON Japan trade show in January 2026 it showed a 10-2-10 package on a 0.8 mm-thick glass core, 78 mm × 77 mm, integrating two **EMIB** bridges (Intel's small silicon bridge embedded in the substrate, Module 17), with warpage held below ~20 µm. Glass offers ultra-flatness (fine-line lithography on it is easier than on a rough organic panel), a CTE tunable from ~3 to ~9 ppm/K to better match silicon, much lower warpage on very large packages, and **through-glass vias (TGVs)**, holes through the glass core at tighter pitch than PTHs. The problems are fragility during handling, TGV metallization and the lack of a supply chain; Absolics (SKC) in Georgia, Samsung Electro-Mechanics and the Taiwanese substrate makers all had pilot lines running as of ~2025.

> **Worked example WE-G: how many substrate layers to fan out 10,000 I/Os?**
> Take a 30 mm × 30 mm die with Cu-pillar bumps on a 150 µm pitch: 200 × 200 = 40,000 bump sites. Suppose 10,000 of them are signals (the rest power and ground, which need no routing, just vias into planes). One assumption before counting: real floorplans put the signal bumps in the outer rings of the array, nearest the package edge they must reach, and fill the center with power and ground, so treat the outer rings as all-signal. The signals must escape from under the die to the outer rings of the package.
> Substrate via pads under the bumps are ~65 µm in diameter, so the gap between adjacent pads is 150 − 65 = 85 µm. With 10/10 µm line/space, each trace consumes 20 µm and the channel must keep 10 µm from each pad. The formula is traces per channel = (gap − space) / (line + space) = (85 − 10) / (10 + 10) ≈ 3. The outermost ring of the array holds ~800 bumps (4 sides × 200, less the shared corners) and escapes directly, and the ~800 gaps between its pads are the channels for everything deeper. The next rings route through those ~800 channels at 3 traces each, ~2,400 more. So one signal layer escapes 800 + 2,400 ≈ **~3,200 signals**, the outer ~4 rings (each ring inward holds a few bumps fewer, ~796, ~792, and so on, which the rounding absorbs). Each escaped ring frees its vias, and deeper rings route on the next layer down. To escape 10,000 signals, the outer ~13 rings of the array, therefore needs 10,000 / 3,200 ≈ **~3–4 signal layers**, and for controlled impedance each signal layer wants a ground or power reference plane adjacent, giving ~6–8 top-side layers, plus a core, plus a comparable bottom-side stack to spread to the 1 mm ball pitch: a 6-2-6 to 8-2-8 substrate, 14–18 layers. Real GPU substrates with 20+ layers are in this range once power integrity requirements are added.
> Now redo it at 2/2 µm L/S: traces per channel = (85 − 2) / (2 + 2) ≈ 20, so one layer escapes ~800 + 800 × 20 = ~16,800 signals, and the entire die could theoretically escape on **one** signal layer. This is why finer L/S on substrates is worth so much: every layer removed saves a lamination cycle (~1–2 days of process time), improves yield (each layer costs a few percent, WE-H next), and reduces thickness and warpage.

> **Worked example WE-H: why "a few percent per layer" is expensive.** Suppose each build-up cycle (laminate, drill, desmear, seed, image, plate, etch) yields 98%, meaning 2% of units pick up a killing defect on that layer. An 18-layer substrate then yields 0.98¹⁸ ≈ **70%** (e^(18 × ln 0.98) = e^(−0.36)); a 12-layer one 0.98¹² ≈ **78%**. Raise per-layer yield to 99% and the figures become ~83% and ~89%. A 510 × 515 mm panel holds only ~25–36 sites of an 80–100 mm substrate, so at 70% a panel delivers a couple of dozen good units after weeks of processing, and each one carries the cost of the ones that failed. That compounding is what "a few dozen good units per panel" means, and it is why every layer that finer L/S removes is worth several percent of yield as well as two days of cycle time.

> **What can go wrong:** **Via voids**: a microvia plated incompletely leaves a gap that opens under thermal cycling, worst in stacked vias. **Misregistration**: layer N's vias land off layer N−1's pads because the panel shrank or stretched between laminations; this is why LDI measures **fiducials** (alignment marks printed on the panel) and rescales each exposure. **Warpage**: a large, thin substrate bows by tens of µm and the die's corner bumps do not touch at reflow (Module 17). **Solder-resist cracking** at ball pads, which lets moisture reach copper. **Black pad** on ENIG finishes, giving joints that pass test and shear off in the field. **ABF cracking** under a lid's adhesive at the package corners. Detection: **automated optical inspection (AOI)** after every layer, X-ray for via fill, electrical open/short testing of every net on a **flying-probe** tester (a pair of moving probes that touch each pad in turn) or a **bed-of-nails** fixture (an array of spring pins that touches them all at once), and **time-domain reflectometry (TDR)**, which fires a fast pulse down a trace and reads the reflection to locate an impedance flaw.

> **Why it matters downstream:** substrate L/S and layer count decide whether a package needs a silicon interposer at all. At 2/2 µm on a flat glass or organic core, an organic RDL could carry die-to-die traffic that today needs CoWoS-S's silicon (that is the premise of CoWoS-R and CoWoS-L, Module 17), and the substrate industry, not the foundry, would then set the ceiling.

## Leadframes and the Mass Market

The trillion-unit world of microcontrollers (MCUs), power management, discretes, analog and sensors does not use build-up substrates. Where pin count is under ~300 the cheapest carrier wins, and the cheapest carrier is a **leadframe**: a stamped or etched sheet of copper alloy that costs cents per unit, needs no lamination cycles at all, and is patterned into a central **die paddle** surrounded by lead fingers, in strips of dozens to hundreds of units. The alloys (industry codes such as C194, C7025 or KFC) are copper with ~2% iron and phosphorus, or with nickel and silicon, added for strength and springiness while keeping roughly ~40–80% of pure copper's conductivity (the range spans the alloy families). Strips are 125–250 µm thick and are **stamped** (punched between hardened tool-steel dies, economical at lead pitches down to roughly ~0.5 mm) or **chemically etched** through a photoresist pattern for finer pitches. The fingers are selectively plated with silver in spots where wires will land, or, increasingly, the whole frame is **pre-plated (PPF)** with nickel, palladium and gold (nickel ~1 µm, palladium and gold each a few tens of nanometers or less) before assembly, so no post-mold plating is needed. Makers include Mitsui High-tec, Shinko, Chang Wah, Haesung DS and SDI.

> **Intuition:** the leadframe is the rebar and the plastic body is the concrete cast around it. The metal carries every electrical and thermal load and gives the part its shape; the epoxy only protects, holds the pieces in place, and is cut and bent afterwards.

The leadframe assembly flow, in order:

1. **Stamp or etch** the strip, including a **dam bar**, a strip of metal between the leads that stops mold compound escaping between them and holds the fingers rigid until molding is done.
2. **Plate** (silver spots, or full PPF).
3. **Die attach** (Step 4) onto the paddle, then cure.
4. **Wire bond** (Step 5) from pads to fingers.
5. **Transfer mold** the whole strip (next section), then post-mold cure.
6. **Deflash** (blast or chemically strip the thin skin of compound that leaked onto the leads) and, if the frame is not PPF, **tin plate** the exposed leads for solderability.
7. **Trim and form**: a punch cuts the dam bars and lead tips free of the strip, then bends the leads into **gull wings** (down and out, so they rest flat on the board) or J-leads (curled under the body); a QFN strip is instead sawn into units.
8. **Mark**, then test.

The common leadframe package types:

| Package | Shape | Pins | Exposed thermal pad? | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **QFP** (quad flat package) | Square body, gull-wing leads on four sides at 0.4–0.8 mm pitch | 32–300 | Sometimes | Microcontrollers, legacy logic |
| **QFN / DFN** (quad / dual flat no-lead) | Leads are pads flush with the bottom edge; nothing protrudes | 4–100 | Yes, the paddle | PMICs (power-management ICs), RF, Wi-Fi/Bluetooth, MCUs; the volume king at a few cents per unit |
| **SOIC / TSSOP** (small-outline, thin-shrink small-outline IC) | Rectangular, gull-wing leads on two sides at 0.5–1.27 mm | 8–56 | Rarely | Analog, op-amps, small logic |
| **SOT-23** (small-outline transistor) | Tiny rectangle with 3–6 leads | 3–6 | No | Discrete transistors, voltage references |
| **TO-220 / TO-247** (transistor outline) | Through-hole body with a metal tab, screw hole and 3 leads | 3 | Yes, the tab | Power MOSFETs, IGBTs, regulators |
| **D2PAK** (surface-mount power) | Surface-mount version of TO-220 with a soldered tab | 3–7 | Yes | Power switches on boards |

> **What can go wrong:** **Paddle delamination**: the smooth copper paddle is the weakest adhesion interface in the package, and moisture that collects there is the classic route to popcorning at reflow (Reliability section); frames are roughened or dimpled to fight it. **Lead coplanarity**: after forming, all lead tips must lie within ~0.1 mm of one plane (the usual specification; some parts are tighter), or some will not touch the board's paste. **Tin whiskers**: pure matte tin plating spontaneously grows hair-like crystals tens of µm long that can short adjacent leads, one reason PPF frames and nickel underplating exist. **Mold flash** on leads, which blocks solder wetting if the deflash step misses it.

## Molding, Marking, Ball Attach, Lids, Singulation

### Molding

A die and its hair-fine wires need a rigid, moisture-resistant body whose CTE is close to the leadframe's and the die's, that will not soften at board reflow, and that can be formed around hundreds of units in a minute. **Transfer molding** in **epoxy mold compound (EMC)** does that. EMC is a formulated solid: ~70–90 wt% fused spherical silica filler, which sets the CTE to ~7–12 ppm/K below Tg (~30–50 above it) and the thermal conductivity to ~1 W/m·K; families of epoxy resins chosen for how they flow, shrink and tolerate heat; a hardener and catalyst; carbon black for opacity; release agents; **ion-trapping** additives that capture chloride ions before they can corrode aluminum pads; and non-halogen flame retardants. The suppliers are **Sumitomo Bakelite** (the leader, with roughly a third of the market as of 2025 by trade-press estimates), Resonac (ex-Hitachi Chemical), **Nagase ChemteX**, Kyocera, Panasonic and Chang Chun.

The process: EMC tablets are preheated to ~90 °C, often by **RF preheat** (radio-frequency heating of the tablet from the inside so it is soft before the plunger moves), dropped into the pot of a mold heated to ~175 °C, and a plunger drives the now-liquid compound at ~7–10 MPa (~70–100 atmospheres) through runners and gates into the cavities holding the leadframe or substrate strips (the **runners** are channels machined into the mold face that carry compound from the pot to each cavity; each **gate** is the deliberately narrow opening where a runner enters a cavity, sized so the cured compound snaps off cleanly there when the strip is ejected). At that pressure a honey-thick fluid is being pushed through ~100 µm gaps between wires, which is why wires sweep. It gels in ~60–120 s; the strip is ejected and then **post-mold cured (PMC)** at 175 °C for ~4–6 hours to complete cross-linking. Tools come from Towa, ASMPT, Besi and Yamada. Multiple dies on a strip are molded as one block (**molded array process, MAP**) and later sawn apart.

> **What can go wrong:** **Wire sweep**: the flow front pushes neighboring loops together; it is measured by X-ray as the sideways displacement of each wire and limited to a few percent of the wire's length (the exact limit is set per product). **Voids**, from trapped air or gas evolved by the resin, found by C-SAM. **Incomplete fill** of a far cavity when the compound gels before arriving. **Flash**: compound leaking past the dam bars onto lead surfaces. **Cure-shrinkage warpage**: the compound shrinks as it cures and the strip bows, worst for thin, one-sided molds over a stiff substrate. **Delamination** of mold from die or paddle, the seed of popcorning. Fixes are gate design, tablet preheat, pressure profile and compound choice, not usually the tool.

**Compression molding** is used where transfer molding's runners would be wasteful or impossible: whole 300 mm reconstituted wafers for fan-out packaging (Wafer-Level and Fan-Out section), 500–600 mm panels, very thin or very large packages whose dies could not survive 7–10 MPa of compound rushing past them, and **molded underfill (MUF)**, where the mold compound simultaneously underfills the flip chip and encapsulates it. It matters far beyond its share of units: every fan-out wafer and every one of SK hynix's MR-MUF HBM stacks (Module 15) is compression-molded, so it is the molding process behind the iPhone processor and behind most of the HBM in an AI accelerator.

The mechanism is a press, not a pump. The lower mold half is a shallow cavity lined with a fresh **release film** (a fluoropolymer sheet drawn onto the cavity by vacuum, so the cured compound peels off it and the steel never touches epoxy); a measured dose of EMC, as granules or as a liquid, is spread onto the film; the upper half, carrying the wafer, panel or strip face-down, descends; a vacuum is pulled to remove the air that would otherwise be trapped as voids; and the two halves close so the compound is squeezed outward until it fills the cavity to a set thickness. Because the compound travels only a few centimeters sideways under gentle pressure (of order ~1–5 MPa, an estimate; a fraction of transfer molding's), instead of being forced through a gate at 7–10 MPa, wire sweep and die displacement from flow are far smaller, there are no runners or cull to throw away (for small parts transfer molding can waste more compound than it uses), and one shot can cover a 300 mm disc or a panel that no runner system could feed evenly. Mold temperatures are similar, ~120–175 °C depending on compound (liquid MUF compounds at the low end); the wafer stays clamped for several minutes (an estimate; it depends on the compound) while it cures through, longer than transfer molding's 60–120 s gel because a whole wafer of compound must be cured in one piece, and a shorter post-mold cure follows. For MR-MUF, the stack of HBM dies is first joined by mass reflow, then a liquid compound is compression-molded so that it flows into the ~10–20 µm gaps between the stacked dies and over the whole stack in the same shot, replacing both the per-layer NCF film and a separate encapsulation. **Towa** of Japan dominates compression molding equipment; ASMPT and Besi also sell it.

> **What can go wrong (compression molding):** **Die shift**: as the compound flows and then shrinks on cure and cool-down, every embedded die moves a little toward the wafer center, by tens of micrometers at the wafer edge if nothing compensates (an order-of-magnitude figure; WE-L in the fan-out section does the arithmetic), so the RDL lithography must measure and correct. **Warpage**: a disc that is silicon on one face and epoxy on the other is a bimetallic strip (job 5); a reconstituted wafer can bow by hundreds of micrometers, sometimes more, unless the compound's filler loading, its cure shrinkage and the carrier are chosen to balance it, and it must be flat enough for a stepper's chuck to hold and its depth of focus to absorb. **Voids** from an incomplete vacuum or from moisture in the granules, worst in the narrow MUF gaps under a die or between stacked dies. **Incomplete fill** at the wafer edge when the dose or the flow is short. **Filler separation**: the silica and resin flow at different rates, leaving resin-rich zones with a different CTE. **Thickness variation** across the wafer, which matters when the mold is to be ground back to expose die pads or pillars (as in InFO). Detection is C-SAM for voids, optical measurement of every die's position, and a warpage map of the wafer; the fixes are dose, vacuum, temperature profile and compound chemistry.

### Marking

A side note only: a fiber or YAG (a solid-state) laser engraves the part number, lot code, date code and country of origin into the mold cap or lid at a few hundred units per minute. Ink marking survives only in a few legacy lines.

### Ball Attach and LGA

The balls of a **ball grid array (BGA)** are the package's built-in solder supply for board assembly, and the reason they must be built in is volume. A board assembler joins components by printing **solder paste** (solder powder suspended in flux, about half metal by volume) through a stencil ~100–150 µm thick and reflowing it; that deposit is enough to form a joint a few tens of micrometers tall to a flat lead, but a package with thousands of terminals needs joints tall enough to bridge the gap under a package that is never perfectly flat, and compliant enough to absorb the package-to-board CTE mismatch (WE-K below does the arithmetic). No practical stencil prints that much solder, so the package brings it: the ball provides ~90% of the joint's metal, sets the joint's height, and, when it melts, supplies the same self-alignment that centered the die in Step 6. The process, one strip of substrates at a time:

1. **Flux print.** A tacky flux (rosin-based **no-clean** types that leave a benign residue, or water-soluble types that are washed off) is stencil-printed onto the strip's bottom pads, a few tens of micrometers thick, so that each pad is wet with flux and nothing else.
2. **Ball placement.** A **ball placement head** carries a stencil drilled with one hole per pad; solder spheres are swept across it so that exactly one sphere sits in each hole, held by vacuum, and the head sets them onto the fluxed pads, where the flux holds them. Vision checks for empty holes and doubles before placement.
3. **Reflow.** The strip passes through the same kind of nitrogen convection oven as Step 6, with a SAC profile (soak ~150–190 °C, peak ~240–250 °C, 40–90 s above the selected alloy's liquidus); each sphere wets its pad and collapses slightly into a truncated ball.
4. **Clean and inspect.** Water-soluble flux is washed off; no-clean is left. Automated optical inspection then checks every ball for presence, position, bridging and diameter, and a 3D system checks **ball coplanarity**, that all ball tops lie within ~0.1 mm of one plane (the usual specification for a large BGA; tighter for fine pitch), since a package that rocks on its tallest balls leaves opens on its shortest.

Ball mounters come from Shibuya Corporation and Athlete FA of Japan, among others; the spheres themselves, sold in the billions to a diameter tolerance of a few percent, come from Senju Metal (Japan), Duksan Hi-Metal and MK Electron (Korea), Indium Corporation (USA) and others. Balls range from ~0.25 mm diameter at 0.4 mm pitch (mobile) to 0.6 mm at 1.0 mm pitch (large FCBGA); a large GPU package carries 4,000–7,000 balls. The alloy is SAC305, or **SAC105 / low-silver** alloys in phones: less silver means fewer of the hard Ag₃Sn intermetallic plates that form inside the joint, so the joint is softer and absorbs a drop shock instead of cracking. **Land grid array (LGA)** omits the balls; the pads are bare ENIG/ENEPIG lands that either sit in a socket with spring contacts (all desktop and server Intel and AMD CPUs, e.g., LGA1851, SP5), so a CPU can be inserted and replaced without soldering, or are soldered by paste printed on the board. **Pin grid arrays (PGA)**, with actual pins, are now rare.

> **Worked example WE-K: why the ball, and not the board's paste, makes the joint.** Take a 1.0 mm pitch FCBGA with 0.6 mm balls. One sphere holds (4/3)π(0.3 mm)³ ≈ **0.113 mm³** of solder. The board assembler's stencil is typically ~125 µm thick with a ~0.5 mm aperture over each pad, so it prints π(0.25 mm)² × 0.125 mm ≈ 0.025 mm³ of paste, of which only about half is metal: ~**0.012 mm³**. The ball therefore supplies 0.113 / (0.113 + 0.012) ≈ **90%** of the joint. Without the ball, 0.012 mm³ of solder on a 0.5 mm pad would make a joint only ~60 µm tall; with it, the collapsed joint stands ~0.4–0.5 mm tall (its height is set by the sphere's volume and the pad sizes on both sides, not by the paste). Now use WE-A's strain logic on the joint the board sees. A 50 mm package has a corner-ball DNP of ~35 mm. Its in-plane CTE is pulled below the board's ~17 ppm/K by the silicon die stiffening it, so take a mismatch of ~6 ppm/K (an estimate; it depends on die size and substrate stiffness) over a 100 K field cycle: δ ≈ 35 mm × 6 × 10⁻⁶ /K × 100 K ≈ **21 µm**. Across a ~450 µm ball that is a shear strain of ~5%; across a 60 µm paste-only joint it would be ~35%, and by Coffin-Manson (n ≈ 2) the tall joint lasts (35/5)² ≈ **50× more cycles**. The ball is the board-level equivalent of the stand-off that the copper pillar gives the die-level joint: height is what makes a solder joint survive.

> **What can go wrong:** **Missing or bridged balls** (a sphere that fell off the stencil, or two that merged), caught by AOI. **Off-center balls**, from a flux print that slid or a placement head out of register, which reflow usually corrects but which flag a drifting process. **Weak balls**: a **ball-shear** test pushes a ball sideways and must see a ductile failure through the solder, not a brittle one at the intermetallic; a brittle failure points to black pad on the ENIG finish or to an over-thick intermetallic from too many reflows. **Coplanarity out of spec** on a warped substrate strip, so the package rocks on the board. **Head-in-pillow at board level**: the same flux-starved non-merge described in Step 6, now between the package ball and the board paste, the commonest BGA assembly defect on warped packages.

### Lids, IHS, and TIM1

A high-power FCBGA is finished with an **integrated heat spreader (IHS)** or lid: a nickel-plated copper cap, ~1–2 mm thick, glued to the substrate perimeter with a silicone sealant and coupled to the die backside by **TIM1**, the thermal interface material between die and lid (TIM2 is the one between lid and heatsink, applied later by whoever builds the system). The TIM1 options are:

- **Polymer TIM (PTIM):** a silicone or hydrocarbon gel filled with alumina, zinc oxide or aluminum particles, 2–8 W/m·K, applied as a 50–150 µm bond line. Cheap, compliant and re-workable, but its thermal resistance limits it to ~100–200 W parts, and it can **pump out** of the gap: each thermal cycle the die and lid bow differently and squeeze the paste out of the gap like toothpaste from a tube, a little more each cycle.
- **Solder TIM (STIM):** a foil of **indium** (melting point 156.6 °C, thermal conductivity ~80 W/m·K), soldered between a Ti/Ni/Au metallized die backside (sputtered after backgrinding) and the gold-plated lid. It gives 5–10× lower resistance than polymer; Intel returned to solder TIM with its 9th-generation desktop CPUs in late 2018. The risks are voids and cracking after many cycles, because indium fatigues.

> **Worked example WE-B: the thermal stack under a lid.** Take a 30 × 27 mm die (8.1 cm² = 8.1 × 10⁻⁴ m²) dissipating 700 W, and use R = t / (k·A) for each layer, then ΔT = 700 W × R.
> 500 µm of silicon (k = 150 W/m·K): R = 5 × 10⁻⁴ / (150 × 8.1 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 4.1 mK/W (millikelvin per watt: thousandths of a degree of rise per watt of heat), so ΔT ≈ **2.9 °C**.
> 100 µm of polymer TIM (k = 5 W/m·K): R = 1 × 10⁻⁴ / (5 × 8.1 × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 25 mK/W, so ΔT ≈ **17 °C**.
> 100 µm of indium solder TIM (k = 80 W/m·K): R ≈ 1.5 mK/W, so ΔT ≈ **1.1 °C**.
> The polymer TIM, one-fifth the thickness of the silicon, costs six times the temperature rise; it is the hottest 100 µm in the package. Switching to indium, or removing the lid so a cold plate presses on the die through one TIM instead of two, recovers ~15 °C at the transistors, which at a fixed junction-temperature limit translates directly into clock speed or into fewer fans. (Real stacks add contact resistances at each interface and the lid's own **spreading resistance**, the extra resistance heat meets when it must fan out sideways from the small die footprint into the much wider lid and heatsink, since R = t/(k·A) assumes the heat flows straight through at a constant area; that is why measured numbers are somewhat higher than these ideal ones.)

Data-center GPUs are increasingly shipped without a lid: a stiffener ring around the die keeps the package flat, and the cold plate presses directly on the die (or on the exposed HBM and GPU top surfaces, ground to the same height) through TIM. Removing one interface saves several °C at 1,000 W, as WE-B shows. Consumer and server CPUs keep the IHS for robustness against clumsy heatsink installation; between the IHS and the heatsink is **TIM2**, the paste the end user applies.

> **Why it matters downstream:** WE-B is why Module 19's GPU modules are lidless, why their HBM stacks are ground to the same height as the GPU dies so one flat cold plate can touch all of them, and why liquid cooling arrived in AI racks before air cooling ran out of fan speed: at 1,000 W and above, every interface in the stack is worth more than any fan.

### Singulation and Handoff

Everything up to this point has been done on strips or panels carrying dozens to hundreds of packages, because handling one unit at a time would be far too slow; singulation is where they become individual parts, and it is a core step because a package whose edge is damaged here fails no matter how good everything before it was. Two routes exist. Substrate strips (BGAs) and MAP-molded leadframe strips (QFNs) are **sawn** apart; QFPs and other leaded parts go through **trim and form** instead: a die punch cuts the dam bars and lead tips free of the strip and bends the leads into gull wings, after which the parts are checked optically for lead coplanarity, the ~0.1 mm specification from the Leadframes section.

Package sawing is wafer dicing made coarse. The strip is mounted on UV dicing tape on a frame, or clamped in a vacuum **jig** (a machined plate with a pocket for each unit, which holds every package after the cuts free it), and a **package saw** (Disco again, with its DFD-series machines; also Tokyo Seimitsu and others) cuts along the saw streets between units. The blade is a resin-bond diamond blade, roughly five to ten times thicker than a wafer hub blade (of order ~0.2–0.3 mm, an estimate, against 20–50 µm), with coarser diamonds, because it must cut through ~0.2–0.5 mm of copper leadframe or copper-and-glass-fiber substrate plus the epoxy body, materials that are gummy (copper), abrasive (glass fiber) and hard (silica-filled EMC) at once; the blade wears far faster than on silicon and is dressed and replaced on a shorter schedule. The kerf is correspondingly ~0.2–0.3 mm and the saw streets on a QFN strip ~0.3 mm, a design cost of a few percent of the strip's area. Spindles run slower than for wafers, tens of thousands of rpm, and feeds are of order ~50–100 mm/s (estimates; recipes are per product), so a strip carrying a few hundred QFNs is cut in a minute or two, and dual-spindle saws cut two streets at once. QFN strips are usually cut from the leadframe side so that the blade enters the copper first and the burr it raises is pushed into the mold body rather than dragged across the solderable pads. After sawing, the units are washed to remove the copper- and epoxy-laden cutting slurry, dried, and picked from the tape or jig by a **sorter** with vision inspection of both faces.

> **What can go wrong (singulation):** **Copper burrs**: the blade smears copper along the cut edge of a QFN's pads and leads; a burr can bridge two adjacent pads or stand proud and block solder wetting, and it is the singulation defect the industry fights hardest, by cut direction, blade choice and feed rate. **Mold-to-leadframe delamination** starting at the cut edge, where the blade's side load pries the epoxy off the smooth copper, which later becomes a moisture path and a popcorning site. **Mold chipping** at the package edge and top corners, cosmetic in itself but a crack starter. **Blade loading and wear**: copper clogs a resin-bond blade, the cut heats, and chipping and burrs rise together; the fix is dressing and a metered blade life. **Cut-position error**: a saw street that drifts by a fraction of its width cuts into a BGA's outer ball pads or a QFN's leads. **Pop-off**: a unit lifts from the tape or jig mid-cut and is destroyed. **Die crack** in very thin packages clamped too hard. Detection is optical inspection of every unit's edges and pads after sorting, and the fix is almost always blade, feed or mounting rather than the saw.

Finished units are loaded into **JEDEC trays** (standardized plastic trays with a pocket per unit, for larger packages) or onto **tape-and-reel** (a pocketed plastic carrier tape with a peel-off cover, wound on a reel, the way pick-and-place machines on a board assembly line are fed) and move to package-level test, **burn-in** (running the parts hot and at elevated voltage for hours to force early-life failures to show themselves before shipment) and system-level test, the subject of Module 18. Assembly yield at an OSAT for a mature FCBGA is typically 99%+; each 1% of loss at this stage destroys dies worth more than the entire assembly cost, which is why known-good-die testing (Module 14) matters.

## Wafer-Level and Fan-Out Packaging

Two definitions first. **Fan-in** means the package's terminals stay inside the die's own footprint; **fan-out** means the wiring extends past the die edge onto surrounding material, so a small die can carry more terminals than fit on its face. Both replace the substrate with a **redistribution layer (RDL)**: thin copper wiring built directly on the die (or on the die plus its surroundings) by fab-style lithography, insulated by **polyimide** or **PBO** (polybenzoxazole), heat-resistant polymers spun on as a liquid and patterned like resist.

**Wafer-level chip-scale packaging (WLCSP)** is the fan-in case and skips the substrate entirely. On the finished wafer, a polymer dielectric is spun and patterned, a sputtered-and-plated copper RDL re-routes the peripheral pads into an area array, a second polymer layer defines UBM openings, and solder balls of ~0.2–0.3 mm at 0.35–0.5 mm pitch are dropped and reflowed directly on the die. After backgrinding, a backside laminate protects the silicon, and the wafer is diced. The package is exactly the die size; a die can only carry as many balls as fit on its own area. WLCSP dominates for PMICs (the regulators that make the dozen voltages a phone needs), RF front-end parts and small sensors in phones (hundreds per phone); the lines that run it are the bumping lines described in Step 1 with an extra polymer-and-RDL layer, at the big OSATs (ASE, Amkor, JCET, PTI) and at the bumping houses of the foundries. Its Achilles' heel is the CTE problem: a 5 mm bare silicon die soldered straight to an **FR-4** board (the standard glass-epoxy board material, CTE ~17 ppm/K) survives temperature cycling only because it is small (short DNP); WLCSPs above ~6–7 mm are rare for exactly this reason.

> **Intuition:** fan-out is re-potting a plant. The die is the root ball; it is set into a bigger pot of mold compound, and the wiring, like roots, can then spread beyond the original ball into the new soil. Where the picture breaks: the new soil (epoxy) and the root ball (silicon) expand differently, and the roots (RDL copper) must cross the boundary between them at every die edge.

**Fan-out wafer-level packaging (FOWLP)** removes the die-size limit. Infineon's **eWLB** (embedded wafer-level BGA, 2006, later licensed to STATS ChipPAC/JCET, Nanium/Amkor and ASE) established the flow:

1. Dice the wafer; place good dies face-down on a carrier coated with a **thermal-release tape** (an adhesive that holds at room temperature and lets go when heated past a set point, so the carrier can be removed later without solvents), spaced further apart than they were on the original wafer.
2. Compression-mold EMC over the dies (Molding section) to form a **reconstituted wafer**: a 300 mm disc of epoxy with the dies embedded in it (or a rectangular panel in panel-level variants).
3. Debond the carrier, exposing the die faces flush with the mold surface.
4. Build RDL layers across both die and mold, fanning connections out beyond the die edge. Each layer is ~3–5 µm of copper in ~5–10 µm of polymer, at 2–10 µm L/S; the copper is sputter-seeded and pattern-plated exactly as in bumping.
5. Drop balls, dice the reconstituted wafer.

The tool classes are the bumping line's plus a molder: compression molders from Towa; RDL steppers from Onto Innovation (the former Rudolph JetStep line), Veeco (the former Ultratech) and Canon; sputter systems from Evatec, among others.

> **Worked example WE-L: how much die shift an RDL can tolerate.** The first RDL via must land on the die's pad. Take a ~30 µm pad and a ~15 µm via (representative sizes): the via may miss the pad center by at most (30 − 15) / 2 ≈ **7 µm** before it slides off the pad's edge. Now the shift. Molding drags every die a little toward the wafer center as the compound flows and then shrinks on cure and cool-down, by a fraction ε of its distance r from the center, so δ ≈ ε × r. ε depends on the compound, the carrier and how the compound flowed, and is of order 10⁻⁴ (an estimate): a die at r = 140 mm, near the wafer edge, then moves ~15 µm, twice the budget, while a die near the center barely moves. A single fixed mask therefore cannot serve both, and it gets worse on a 600 mm panel, where r is twice as large. That is why fan-out RDL lithography measures the actual position of every die after molding and either steps the exposure die by die, correcting each field (as a stepper can, since it exposes one field at a time), or generates a custom RDL pattern per package on a maskless writer (Deca Technologies' **adaptive patterning**), and why die shift, not lithography resolution, sets the finest pad pitch a fan-out process can serve.

TSMC's **InFO (Integrated Fan-Out)** made fan-out famous. Introduced in 2016 for the iPhone 7's A10 processor, InFO-PoP replaced the substrate-based flip-chip package-on-package used through the A9 generation (which TSMC and Samsung had dual-sourced). Its innovations: dies are placed face-*up* with tall electroplated copper pillars ("through-InFO vias", TIVs, ~150–200 µm tall) alongside them; after molding, the mold is ground back to expose both the die's copper pads and the TIV tops; RDLs (down to ~2/2 µm L/S, finer than any substrate) are built on top; and the **LPDDR** memory package (low-power DRAM, the phone's main memory) is stacked on the TIVs in a **package-on-package (PoP)** arrangement. The result is thinner than a substrate-based PoP by ~30%, has lower resistance and inductance to the memory, and better thermal performance. Every iPhone **application processor (AP)** since has used an InFO variant (as of 2025), and InFO-on-Substrate (InFO-oS) serves networking and lower-cost multi-die parts. Samsung, ASE, PTI and Innolux pursue panel-level fan-out (**FOPLP**) on 500–600 mm panels to cut cost per area (a 600 mm square panel has ~5× the area of a 300 mm wafer, with less edge waste for rectangular packages), with mixed results as of ~2025. What has gone wrong is everything that scales with size: die shift grows with distance from the panel center (WE-L), a panel that is half silicon and half epoxy warps by more than a wafer and is harder to hold flat for lithography, there is no standard panel size, so every tool is custom and the equipment base is thin, and a single excursion scraps a panel's worth of dies rather than a wafer's. Samsung's panel line packaged the processor of its Galaxy Watch (2018) and remains the best-known production example; as of 2025 FOPLP is spreading for lower-value parts and for large multi-die substrates, while the high-value phone processors stay on InFO wafers.

> **What can go wrong:** **Die shift**: dies move by micrometers to tens of micrometers as the EMC flows and shrinks during molding, so the RDL lithography must measure every die's actual position and adjust each exposure (the same trick LDI plays on substrate panels; WE-L). **Warpage** of a wafer that is half silicon and half epoxy, which fights the lithography's **depth of focus** (the range of heights, only some micrometers for fine lines, over which the projected pattern stays sharp; a wafer that bows more than that cannot be printed in one focus setting) and the bonder's flatness. **RDL cracking** at the die-to-mold step, where the copper crosses from silicon (2.6 ppm/K) onto epoxy (~7–12 ppm/K) and every thermal cycle flexes it. **Mold-to-die delamination**, which lets moisture reach the RDL. **Panel-level yield**: on a 600 mm panel one process excursion scraps many more dies than on a wafer, and the tooling to hold a panel flat to a few µm is still maturing (as of 2025).

## The Package Taxonomy

An engineer chooses a package along four axes. **Pin count**: under ~100 a leadframe suffices; up to ~300 a QFP can still fit leads around the perimeter; beyond that only an area array (BGA) has room. **Power**: a few watts dissipate through a QFN's exposed paddle; hundreds of watts need a flip-chip die with a lid or a bare backside for a cold plate. **Height and footprint**: a phone wants a WLCSP or fan-out part under 1 mm tall and no larger than the die; a server does not care. **Cost per unit**: cents for a leadframe part, dollars to tens of dollars for an FCBGA, thousands for a CoWoS assembly. High-speed signaling pushes toward flip chip regardless of the other axes, because a bond wire's ~1–3 nH cannot carry a multi-GHz signal cleanly.

A package name encodes most of this. "FCBGA-2397, 52.5 × 45 mm, 1.0 mm pitch" says flip chip, ball grid array, 2,397 balls, the body size, and the ball spacing; "QFN-48, 7 × 7 mm, 0.5 mm pitch" says a leadframe part with 48 no-lead pads. The table places the families in history:

| Package | Era | Interconnect | Pin/ball count | Pitch | Typical use today |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DIP (dual in-line package) | 1970s | Wire bond, leadframe | 8–64 | 2.54 mm | Hobbyist, legacy logic |
| SOIC / TSSOP / SOT | 1980s+ | Wire bond, leadframe | 3–56 | 0.5–1.27 mm | Analog, discretes |
| QFP | 1980s+ | Wire bond, leadframe | 32–300 | 0.4–0.8 mm | MCUs, legacy ASICs (application-specific ICs) |
| QFN / DFN | 2000s+ | Wire bond or flip chip, leadframe | 4–100 | 0.4–0.65 mm | PMIC, RF, MCUs, the volume king |
| PGA (pin grid array) | 1980s+ | Wire bond or flip chip, pins into a socket | ~100–1,300+ | 1.27–2.54 mm | Socketed CPUs; now rare (AMD's AM4 was the last mainstream one) |
| PBGA (plastic BGA) | 1990s+ | Wire bond, laminate | 100–1,000 | 0.8–1.27 mm | FPGAs (field-programmable gate arrays), networking, mid-range SoCs |
| FCBGA / FCLGA (flip-chip ball / land grid array) | Late 1990s+ | Flip chip, build-up substrate | 500–8,000+ | 0.65–1.0 mm | CPUs, GPUs, high-end SoCs |
| WLCSP | 2000s+ | Direct balls on die (fan-in) | 4–200 | 0.35–0.5 mm | Mobile PMIC, RF, sensors |
| FOWLP (eWLB, InFO) | 2010s+ | RDL over die and mold | 100–1,500 | 0.35–0.5 mm | Smartphone APs, RF, automotive radar |

| 3D (HBM, SoIC, Foveros): dies stacked vertically | 2015+ | TSVs (through-silicon vias), microbumps or hybrid bonding | Die-to-die 10⁴–10⁶ | 1–40 µm | HBM stacks; stacked SoCs via TSMC SoIC and Intel Foveros, their 3D die-stacking technologies (Modules 15, 17) |

The pattern: as pin counts pass ~300, perimeter leads cannot fit, so the industry moved to area arrays (BGA). As signal speeds and counts passed what a bond wire could serve, it moved to flip chip. As dies passed the reticle limit and as memory bandwidth demanded thousands of wires a few millimeters long, it moved to 2.5D and 3D.

## Package Reliability and Qualification

A package must last 10–20 years in the field but must be qualified in weeks, so every qualification test is an accelerated version of one field stress: hotter, wetter, faster or harder than anything the part will ever see, run for a few hundred hours, and interpreted through an acceleration model that maps test hours to field years. Standards come from **JEDEC**, the industry standards body for semiconductor components, and from the **AEC** (Automotive Electronics Council), whose AEC-Q100 is the car standard. This section covers the package-level stresses and their physics; Module 18 covers the qualification *flows* (JEDEC's JESD47 for commercial parts, AEC-Q100 for automotive) in which these tests are combined with electrical and burn-in tests. The core package tests:

- **Temperature cycling (TC), JESD22-A104.** −40 to +125 °C (condition G) or −55 to +125 °C (condition B), typically 500–1,000 cycles at 1–3 cycles/hour, with electrical test at intervals. Fails: solder fatigue, underfill delamination, die or substrate cracks.
- **HAST (Highly Accelerated Stress Test), JESD22-A110.** 130 °C, 85% **RH** (relative humidity, the fraction of the water vapor the air could hold at that temperature), ~2 atm, 96–264 h with **bias**, a voltage applied during the test so that corrosion and electrochemical migration are driven electrically as in service. It accelerates moisture-driven corrosion, ionic migration and delamination. Unbiased HAST (uHAST, A118) omits the bias; the older THB (temperature-humidity-bias) test (85 °C/85% RH, 1,000 h) tests the same mechanisms more slowly.
- **High-temperature storage (HTS), JESD22-A103.** 150 °C for 1,000 h, driving **intermetallic growth** at wire bonds and bumps: the brittle Au-Al **purple plague** of gold wire on aluminum pads, or Kirkendall voiding at Cu/Sn interfaces (the one-way-diffusion voids defined under Wire Bonding; here copper diffuses into tin faster than tin into copper, and the vacancies it leaves behind coalesce along the interface).
- **Drop test, JESD22-B111.** A board with the packages is dropped to produce a 1,500 g (1,500 times gravity), 0.5 ms half-sine shock, 30 times; it is the pass/fail test for handset packages and the reason low-silver SAC alloys exist.

> **Worked example WE-I: from 1,000 test cycles to years in the field.** A simplified Coffin-Manson acceleration factor for solder fatigue is AF = (ΔT_test / ΔT_field)^n, where ΔT_test and ΔT_field are the temperature swings in the chamber and in service, and n ≈ 1.9 is the fitted exponent for SAC solder. For a −40/+125 °C test (ΔT = 165 K) against a field cycle of 25–85 °C (ΔT = 60 K, a laptop warming up and cooling down once a day): AF = (165/60)^1.9 = 2.75^1.9 ≈ **6.8**. So 1,000 test cycles represent ~6,800 field cycles, about **18 years** at one cycle per day. The honest caveat: the full **Norris-Landzberg** model multiplies this by terms for cycle frequency and for peak temperature. Slow field cycles let the solder **creep** (slow, permanent deformation under a sustained stress, which solder does even at room temperature because 25 °C is already more than half its melting point on the absolute scale, 298 K against 490 K) and so do more damage per cycle than a fast chamber cycle of the same swing, so real qualification uses AF values fitted to the product's mission profile rather than this single exponent.

Before any of these, samples undergo **preconditioning** per J-STD-020 (revision F, 2022): moisture soak to a **Moisture Sensitivity Level (MSL)** followed by three passes through a package-dependent lead-free classification reflow profile, up to 260 °C; the required peak depends on package thickness and volume and is distinct from the actual assembly profile. The MSL system rates how long a package can sit in factory air (30 °C, 60% RH) between opening its dry-pack and being soldered: **MSL 1** = unlimited, **MSL 2** = 1 year, **MSL 2a** = 4 weeks, **MSL 3** = 168 hours (the most common rating for BGAs), MSL 4 = 72 h, MSL 5 = 48 h, MSL 5a = 24 h, MSL 6 = must be baked before use.

> **Intuition:** a popcorn kernel is a sealed shell around a little moisture; heat it and the water flashes to steam at a pressure the shell cannot hold, and it bursts. A molded package that has absorbed moisture is the same kernel: the shell is the epoxy, the weak spot is an internal interface, and the reflow oven is the pan.

The failure it guards against is **popcorning**: epoxy mold compound and substrates absorb ~0.1–0.3 wt% water; at 260 °C, water trapped at an interface (die-to-paddle, mold-to-die, underfill) vaporizes at a pressure of several MPa (the saturated vapor pressure of water at 260 °C is ~4.7 MPa), delaminating the interface and cracking the package with an audible pop. The remedy is baking (125 °C for 24 h) and shipping in dry-packs with desiccant and a humidity indicator card.

> **What can go wrong, and how it is found:** the failure-analysis toolkit is small and universal. **C-SAM** (scanning acoustic microscopy) images internal delaminations and voids non-destructively, because an air gap reflects ultrasound almost totally. **X-ray** shows voids in solder and die attach, wire sweep and bridged balls. **Dye-and-pry** floods a mounted BGA with red dye, pries the package off the board, and any ball that was already cracked shows a red fracture face. **Cross-section**: pot the part in resin, grind and polish to the plane of interest, and look at the joint, the intermetallic thickness and the crack path under a microscope. Environmental chambers for the tests themselves come from ESPEC of Japan and others.

## The Industry: OSATs, Foundry Back Ends, and Equipment

### Why OSATs exist

Packaging is labor- and floor-space-intensive, its tools cost a fraction of a fab's, and its cleanroom class is looser, so chipmakers moved assembly to low-cost regions early (Intel's first offshore assembly plant opened in Penang in 1972) and specialist assemblers grew up alongside them (Anam, the ancestor of Amkor, in Korea in 1968; ASE in Taiwan in 1984). The fabless model completed the split: a company with no fab has no reason to own a packaging plant either, and TSMC's rise created a whole customer base that needed someone to package what the foundry produced. **Outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT)** companies package and test chips for fabless firms, foundries and IDMs; they run on volume, thin margins and rapid tool turnover, and cluster in Taiwan, China, Korea and Southeast Asia. **Electronics manufacturing services (EMS)** firms are the next step down the chain, assembling boards; ASE owns one (USI).

### OSATs

The top-10 OSATs had combined 2024 revenue of ~$41.6 billion (TrendForce's May 2025 ranking; this text keeps the 2024 figures because the 2025 ranking was not incorporated at the time of writing). By share of the top-10:

- **ASE Technology Holding** (Taiwan; includes SPIL, acquired 2018, and the USI EMS business): ~$18.5 billion, ~45% of the top-10 and roughly 30% of the entire OSAT market including smaller players. Kaohsiung is the center of gravity.
- **Amkor** (USA, with major plants in Korea, the Philippines, Japan, Taiwan, Vietnam and Portugal): ~$6.3 billion, ~15%.
- **JCET** (China; owns the former STATS ChipPAC of Singapore): ~$5.0 billion, ~12%, growing at double digits.
- **Tongfu Microelectronics** (China; AMD's partner, with the former AMD Suzhou and Penang plants): ~8%; then **Powertech Technology (PTI)** (Taiwan; memory packaging leader), ~5.5%; **Huatian (HT-Tech)** (China), ~5%; and WiseRoad, Hana Micron (Korea), KYEC (Taiwan; test only) and ChipMOS (Taiwan) making up the rest.

### TSMC's Own Back End

TSMC is unusual among foundries in operating a large in-house back end, because it decided in the 2010s that advanced packaging (InFO, CoWoS and **SoIC**, its 3D die-stacking technology, Module 17) was a competitive weapon rather than a commodity. Its **Advanced Packaging (AP)** fabs in Taiwan, as of early 2026:

- **AP1**, Hsinchu: the original site, being repurposed for R&D.
- **AP2**, Tainan: bumping and microbumps, some CoWoS.
- **AP3**, Longtan: InFO, the iPhone AP line.
- **AP5**, Taichung: CoWoS.
- **AP6**, Zhunan: opened June 2023; the main CoWoS and SoIC site.

### Cost

For a mainstream chip, assembly and packaging is on the order of 10–20% of the total manufactured chip cost, with test adding a few percent more. A QFN costs cents; a WLCSP a few cents per die; bumping a 300 mm wafer on the order of a few hundred dollars; a mid-range FCBGA several dollars to tens of dollars, most of it the substrate. For an AI accelerator the picture inverts: a CoWoS assembly costs thousands of dollars in packaging and interposer before counting the HBM, so packaging and memory together can exceed 30% of module cost, and the packaging step, not the wafer, sets the volume ceiling. That is the subject of the next module.

## Summary

- A package converts 40–150 µm die terminals into 0.4–1.0 mm board terminals, delivers ~1,000 A at 0.8 V through well under 0.1 mΩ, removes 700+ W, and protects a brittle die for 10–20 years.
- The root problem is CTE mismatch: silicon 2.6 ppm/K versus organic substrate ~15 ppm/K. A 30 mm die's corner bump would shear ~50 µm on cool-down (70% strain); underfill converts that shear into shared bending and buys more than 10× lower strain, roughly 200× more cycles by Coffin-Manson, and 1,000+ survivable thermal cycles.
- FCBGA flow: bump → backgrind → dice → flip-chip join → underfill → lid or mold → mark → ball attach → singulate → test. Leadframe parts branch after dicing into die attach, wire bond, mold, and trim and form.
- Bumping is fab-style lithography plus plating: Ti/Cu UBM, thick resist, a 30–50 µm Cu pillar with a 15–25 µm SnAg cap, at ~0.22 µm/min per A/dm² (about an hour per pillar layer). Cu pillars replaced solder C4 because height no longer depends on pitch and copper resists electromigration.
- Backgrinding takes 775 µm to 300–700 µm (FCBGA), 100–200 µm (wire bond), 30–50 µm (HBM); fine grinding plus stress relief removes the damage layer that would halve die strength; TAIKO rings and temporary carriers handle floppy wafers; Disco has ~70–80% share.
- Dicing: blade (20–50 µm kerf, chipping risk, laser grooving for low-k), stealth (an internal defect plane written at 1,064 nm plus tape expansion, no kerf), plasma (all streets at once, best for tens of thousands of tiny dies).
- Wire bonding still serves ~three-quarters of units: thermosonic copper ball-and-stitch at 15–25 wires/s. A 3 mm wire is ~0.1 Ω and ~3 nH, which is why high-current, high-speed parts moved to flip chip (area-array I/O at ~0.35 mΩ and ~0.05 nH per bump).
- Mass reflow relies on solder self-alignment (±10 µm placement tolerated); TCB holds thin dies flat at ±1–2 µm for fine pitch at 10–30 s per die; underfill choices are CUF, MUF and NCP/NCF.
- The build-up substrate is a miniature PCB: BT core with PTHs, then ABF layers built by SAP (laser microvia, permanganate roughening, electroless seed, LDI, pattern plating); 8–10 µm L/S in volume, 2 µm on the roadmap. Escape routing sets the layer count, and every layer removed is a lamination cycle and a few percent of yield (0.98¹⁸ ≈ 70%).
- Ajinomoto's ABF holds > 90% of the build-up film market; the 2020–2022 shortage was of substrate fabrication capacity (Ibiden, Unimicron, AT&S), not of film; glass cores are the next step for 100 mm-class packages.
- Leadframe + wire bond + transfer mold is the trillion-unit, cents-per-unit world of QFN and QFP; EMC is 70–90% silica in epoxy, molded at 175 °C and 7–10 MPa. Compression molding, a gentle press with no runners, is the variant behind every fan-out wafer and MR-MUF HBM stack, and die shift and warpage are its failure modes.
- A BGA ball supplies ~90% of the board-level joint and sets its height (WE-K); singulation saws strips apart with a thick resin-bond blade, and copper burrs and edge delamination are its defects; finished units ship in trays or tape-and-reel to test and burn-in.
- Moisture absorbed by EMC and substrate flashes to steam at 260 °C reflow (~4.7 MPa), so packages carry MSL ratings (MSL 3 = 168 h floor life) and are baked and dry-packed; 1,000 cycles of −40/+125 °C stand in for ~18 years of daily 60 K cycles (AF ≈ 6.8).
- The TIM between die and lid is the largest thermal resistance in the stack (~17 °C at 700 W for a polymer TIM versus ~3 °C for the silicon); indium solder TIM or a lidless cold plate recovers most of it.
- Fan-out (eWLB, InFO) re-embeds dies in mold so RDL wiring can extend beyond the die edge; InFO-PoP has carried every iPhone processor since the A10. OSATs (ASE ~30%), TSMC's in-house AP fabs, and a concentrated tool base (Disco, Besi, ASMPT, K&S, Towa) make up the industry; packaging is ~10–20% of cost for ordinary chips and > 30% for CoWoS/HBM-class parts.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"Packaging is a low-tech afterthought."** → A leading FCBGA substrate is a 20-layer laminate with 8 µm copper lines, laser vias, and micrometer-level registration across a half-meter panel; bumping uses fab lithography and plating; TCB places dies to ±1 µm. Packaging now sets the volume and cost ceiling for AI accelerators.
- **"Wire bonding is obsolete."** → It is obsolete for high-pin-count, high-speed parts, but by unit count roughly three-quarters of all packaged devices are still wire-bonded, on copper wire, at 15–25 wires per second.
- **"Underfill is just glue to hold the chip on."** → Underfill is the structural element that converts an unsurvivable ~50 µm shear displacement at the die corners into a shared bending of die and substrate. Without it, flip chip on organic substrates would fail in a few thermal cycles.
- **"Thinner dies are always better."** → Thin dies conduct heat better and stack more, but they warp, are hard to handle below ~100 µm without carriers or TAIKO rings, and lose fracture strength unless the grinding damage layer is polished off. Large FCBGA dies are deliberately kept at 300–700 µm.
- **"A shortage of ABF means Ajinomoto is out of stock."** → The 2020–2022 "ABF shortage" was mainly a shortage of ABF *substrate* fabrication capacity at Ibiden, Unimicron, and peers (two-year plant build cycles), with the film itself a secondary constraint. Ajinomoto sells to substrate makers, not chip companies.
- **"Stealth dicing is just a laser saw."** → A laser saw ablates material from the surface; stealth dicing writes a plane of defects *inside* the silicon with a beam the surface barely absorbs, and the wafer is then cleaved by stretching the tape. There is no kerf and no debris.
- **"The die is always the most expensive part of a package."** → For many mid-range FCBGAs the substrate costs more than the whole assembly process and can rival the die; for a large GPU the substrate alone is $100–300, and a CoWoS assembly's packaging and HBM exceed 30% of module cost.
- **"Lead-free solder was a drop-in replacement for tin-lead."** → SAC melts at ~217 °C against ~183 °C for eutectic tin-lead, ~35 °C higher. That pushed reflow peaks to 240–250 °C in production and 260 °C in qualification, which is what made moisture sensitivity and popcorning a first-order problem, created the MSL system, and forced the reformulation of mold compounds, laminates and underfills to survive the hotter reflow.
