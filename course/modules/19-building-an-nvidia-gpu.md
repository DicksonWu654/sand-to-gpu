# Module 19: Building an NVIDIA GPU: From RTL to Rack

How does a **fabless** company (one with no fab, no packaging house, and no factories of its own) turn a few million lines of **SystemVerilog** (the programming language chips are written in) into a 1.4-tonne liquid-cooled rack that sells for $3–4 million, and do it on a twelve-month product cadence?

An H100 **die** (one rectangular chip cut from a wafer) is 814 mm² of TSMC **4N** silicon (NVIDIA's custom variant of TSMC's 5 nm-class process; the name is explained below) carrying 80 billion transistors. A B200 package holds two dies of that size plus eight **HBM3E** stacks (towers of memory chips standing beside the GPU, Module 15) on a **CoWoS-L interposer** (TSMC's method of wiring dies together on a base of silicon and polymer, Module 17), and a GB200 NVL72 rack holds 72 such packages, 36 **Arm** CPUs (processors built on Arm's licensed designs, the same family as in phones), 18 **NVLink switch** chips (chips whose only job is to pass traffic between GPUs), roughly 5,000 copper cables, and pulls 120–140 kW through **cold plates** (water-cooled copper blocks bolted to each chip).

It is hard for three reasons that compound. First, the design is at the edge of what can be verified: 80–200 billion transistors cannot be simulated at speed, so NVIDIA is by most accounts the largest buyer of **hardware emulators**, room-sized machines that run a chip design at a fraction of its final speed before any silicon exists. Second, the die is at the edge of what a lithography scanner can print: a scanner exposes one 26 × 33 mm rectangle (the **reticle field**, Module 07) at a time, so a conventional single-field die must fit within 858 mm²; specialized wafer-scale designs use multiple stitched fields, and every NVIDIA data-center GPU since A100 has been within a few percent of that limit. Blackwell had to go to two reticle-limited dies stitched together at 10 TB/s, which is more than the 8 TB/s the B200's eight HBM stacks deliver, so the die-to-die fabric offers high aggregate bandwidth, although latency, directionality and sustainable application bandwidth still differ from HBM. Third, the supply chain is at the edge of capacity: during the 2023–26 ramp, trade reporting identified CoWoS packaging and HBM as major constraints; qualified wafer allocation, test and system capacity can also bind, and the whole industry's allocation politics ran through TSMC's advanced-packaging fabs.

The module takes four things in order: the design, from architecture to **tape-out** (the handoff of the finished layout to the foundry); the manufacturing timeline of one GPU; the module, board and rack it becomes; and the economics of who captures the value. By the end you should be able to explain how a GPU is designed and verified before any silicon exists, why its die costs a tenth of its memory, why it takes six months to build one and a year to get one, and why the rack it lives in has to be water-cooled.

## Before you start

- **Transistors and gates** (Module 11). A transistor is a switch controlled by a voltage; a handful of them make a **logic gate** (a circuit that computes AND, OR, NOT and the like); ~80 billion of them make the H100 die.
- **Layers and masks** (Modules 07, 08, 12). A chip is built from ~80 **mask layers**, each a patterning step with its own mask: the transistors at the bottom, then ~15–18 levels of copper wiring stacked above them (a level of wiring often needs more than one mask).
- **The reticle field** (Module 07). The scanner prints one 26 × 33 mm rectangle per exposure, so no single die can exceed ~858 mm².
- **Defects and yield** (Module 13). Random defects land at a density D0 per cm²; a die of area A survives with probability ≈ e<sup>−A·D0</sup>, so big dies fail more often than small ones.
- **Wafer sort and e-fuses** (Module 14). Wafer sort probes every die on the wafer, and **e-fuses** (tiny on-die wires blown open by a current pulse) permanently record which blocks are disabled.
- **HBM** (Module 15). High-bandwidth memory is DRAM dies stacked and wired vertically with **through-silicon vias** (copper wires drilled through the silicon), standing beside the GPU on the package.
- **CoWoS, interposers and bumps** (Modules 16, 17). CoWoS puts dies on a **silicon interposer** (a slab of silicon whose wires are ~100× finer than a circuit board's), then on an **ABF substrate** (a laminated package board); **C4 bumps** and **micro-bumps** are the solder connections between the levels.
- **Final test** (Module 18). After packaging every part goes through final test, burn-in and system-level test before it is sold.
- **School electricity you will use.** V = I·R and P = V·I = I²·R; a **capacitor** stores charge and can release it in nanoseconds; an **inductor** resists changes in current; a **decibel (dB)** is ten times the logarithm of a power ratio, so 30 dB of loss means one-thousandth of the power arrives.
- **Clocked logic** (programming background). A clock coordinates when storage elements accept new values; 1.8 GHz is 1.8 billion ticks per second, 0.55 ns each. The work between consecutive storage stages must settle within its allotted time. A larger computation can cross several such stages and take several ticks.


**How to read the numerical examples.** Public product specifications and dated company statements are attributed where checked. Unattributed geometry, recipe, yield, throughput and price ranges are representative teaching assumptions or reported estimates, not disclosed production data. They illustrate a mechanism or a calculation; applying them to a named chip requires its process, configuration, date and measurement definition. ## What the die has to be

The first group of sections follows the design from architecture to tape-out, the part of the chain no fab supplies. It starts with what the chip is for. A GPU is thousands of small arithmetic units that all execute the same instruction on different data at the same time, which is the shape of almost every graphics and AI workload. A **streaming multiprocessor (SM)** is the basic group of ~128 such units with its own instruction scheduler and its own small local memory; think of an SM as one "core". A **Tensor Core** is a unit inside the SM that multiplies two small matrices in a single step, because neural-network training and inference are, at the level of arithmetic, almost entirely matrix multiplication. **FP32, FP16, FP8 and FP4** are floating-point number formats with that many bits per number; fewer bits means more operations per second per watt at lower precision, and AI tolerates low precision in a way that scientific computing does not, which is why each generation adds a smaller format.

> **Intuition: the die as a city.** The SMs are the factories, all built from one blueprint and repeated across the map. The L2 cache is the central warehouse that keeps the factories supplied without a trip to the port for every part. HBM is the port: enormous throughput, but a long way from any given factory. NVLink ports are the highways to the neighbouring cities (the other GPUs), and PCIe is the ordinary road to the CPU. The design question is how many factories, how big a warehouse, how many highway lanes, all inside a fixed city limit of 858 mm².

On the full GH100 die (the silicon behind H100) the hierarchy is 8 **GPCs** (Graphics Processing Clusters), each holding 9 **TPCs** (Texture Processing Clusters) of 2 SMs, for 144 SMs on the die.

Inside each SM, the main blocks are:

- 128 FP32 **CUDA cores** (CUDA is NVIDIA's programming platform, and a CUDA core is its name for one arithmetic unit).
- 4 fourth-generation Tensor Cores.
- A 256 KB **register file** (the fastest memory on the chip, holding the values the arithmetic units are working on right now), and 256 KB of combined L1 cache / shared memory.

The Tensor Core numbers need one calculation. Each executes 8 × 4 × 16 FP16 **multiply-accumulate** operations per clock. A multiply-accumulate multiplies two values and adds the product to a running total. The three dimensions describe matrix tiles: an 8 × 4 input multiplied by a 4 × 16 input gives an 8 × 16 output. That requires 8 × 4 × 16 = 512 multiply-accumulates per clock per Tensor Core.

To check the published total, count the units, their work per tick, and the ticks per second: 132 enabled SMs × 4 = 528 Tensor Cores × 512 × 2 FLOP per multiply-accumulate × 1.83 GHz ≈ 989 TFLOPS. **FLOP** means floating-point operation, and **TFLOPS** means trillions of those operations per second. This is NVIDIA's **dense** FP16 H100 figure: every weight is multiplied. The doubled **sparse** figure assumes half the weights have been pruned to zero and the hardware skips them. Dense and sparse figures therefore describe different amounts of work, not two speeds for the same calculation.

The **Transformer Engine** adds FP8 with per-tensor scaling. This is NVIDIA's combination of Tensor Core hardware and library software that chooses FP8 or FP16 for each neural-network layer and rescales each block of numbers so that 8 bits are enough. It is a separate idea from skipping zero weights: scaling changes number representation; sparsity changes which multiplications are performed.

A **cache** exists because HBM is hundreds of nanoseconds away: it is a copy of recently used data kept on the die so the SMs do not wait, and L1 is the small one inside each SM.

Around the GPCs sits a 50 MB **L2 cache** split into two partitions with a **crossbar** (a switch that lets any SM reach any L2 slice) between them, then 12 512-bit HBM3 memory controllers (512-bit meaning 512 wires carrying data in parallel; six stacks, each with two 512-bit **pseudo-channel** groups, a pseudo-channel being an independently addressed half of a memory channel), 18 fourth-generation **NVLink** ports at 50 GB/s each (900 GB/s bidirectional total; NVLink is NVIDIA's proprietary GPU-to-GPU connection), a **PCIe** Gen5 x16 host interface (the standard connection to the host CPU, 16 lanes wide), and the copy engines (units that move data between memories without occupying an SM), video decoders, and the logic that lets one GPU be partitioned into several smaller virtual ones.

Every memory and link interface has two halves: the **PHY** (physical layer), the analog circuit at the die edge that drives and receives signals over the package wires, and the **controller**, the digital logic behind it that schedules requests. Roughly speaking: the SMs are ~60% of the die area, L2 and memory PHYs ~25%, and the rest is I/O and glue.

The architectural decisions are made two to four years before silicon. The team decides the FP8/FP4 formats, the size of the L2, the number of NVLink ports, and the harvesting plan. The L2 size trades die area against HBM traffic, and the trade exists because every byte found in L2 is a byte not fetched from HBM, and HBM bandwidth (3.35 TB/s on H100) and its energy per bit (several times that of an on-die access) are the two scarcest resources on the chip. The NVLink port count sets the rack-scale topology three years later. **Harvesting** is the plan for selling imperfect dies: the die is built with 144 SMs and 6 HBM sites, but the shipping H100 SXM5 (SXM is the module format the GPU is sold on, described later) enables 132 SMs and 5 HBM stacks. The reason is defect statistics. At ~0.1 defects per cm² (Module 13) an 8.14 cm² die carries on average ~0.8 defects, so roughly half of all dies have at least one; without spare SMs those dies would be scrap. That redundancy is what makes an 814 mm² die economically yieldable, and it is designed in from day one, with each SM and each memory partition individually **fusable**, meaning an e-fuse (the current-blown on-die wire from Before you start) can permanently record a setting such as "SM 17 is disabled".

Blackwell's architectural leap was structural rather than per-SM: two dies of ~104 billion transistors each, on TSMC 4NP (a performance-tuned derivative of 4N, see the PDK section), joined by **NV-HBI** (NVIDIA High-Bandwidth Interface), a die-to-die link running at 10 TB/s across the CoWoS-L local silicon bridges so that the pair behaves as one 208-billion-transistor GPU with a single **coherent** L2 (every SM sees one consistent copy of the data, whichever die holds it) and one CUDA device. That link is the reason Blackwell needed CoWoS-L instead of CoWoS-S. Recall the interposer from Before you start: a transistor-free slab of silicon under the dies whose wires are ~100× finer than a circuit board's. In CoWoS-S that slab is already larger than one reticle field, so it is **reticle-stitched**: exposed as several overlapping reticle fields whose wiring is joined at the seams (H100's interposer is ~2.5 reticles of silicon). Two reticle dies plus eight HBM stacks need ~3.3 reticles, and at that size a whole-silicon slab becomes too costly and too low-yielding: the interposer wafer is itself a lithography-and-copper product whose defect count scales with area (Module 13), and a single flaw in it scraps the silicon under thousands of dollars of known-good dies and HBM. The way out is to notice that only the strips under the die-to-die edge and under each HBM stack need silicon-fine wiring; the rest of the interposer carries power and slower signals that coarser wiring handles. So CoWoS-L puts silicon only there: small silicon bridges (**LSI**, Local Silicon Interconnect) embedded at exactly those edges inside an **organic RDL interposer** (**redistribution layer**: wiring built in polymer rather than in silicon; "organic" here means a carbon-based resin, as in a circuit board, rather than silicon or ceramic), and the 10 TB/s of NV-HBI traffic crosses those bridges on thousands of fine-**pitch** (centre-to-centre spacing) wires. Number sense, all illustrative: 10 TB/s is 80 Tb/s; at ~10–20 Gb/s per wire that is ~4,000–8,000 signal wires crossing a die edge ~26–33 mm long, one wire every few micrometres, which only silicon or a silicon bridge can carry and no circuit board can.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Two numbers fixed here decide the shape of things built years later. The NVLink port count (18 on H100 and on B200) is what lets 72 GPUs form one rack-sized network in the NVL72 section, and the number of HBM sites (6 on GH100, 8 on B200) is the multiplier on the single largest line in the bill of materials. An architect choosing 18 ports and 8 stacks years before launch was choosing the rack topology and the HBM bill.

## RTL: writing the chip

The architecture is a set of decisions; something has to turn them into an exact, executable description that tools can check, simulate and build from, and that is the register-transfer level. The design is captured in **RTL** (register-transfer level) code, almost entirely SystemVerilog, describing what every flip-flop holds at every clock edge and the combinational logic between them. Those words need a picture first.

> **Intuition: the clock as a metronome.** A **flip-flop** is a one-bit memory that copies its input on each tick of the clock and holds it until the next. Between ticks, the **combinational logic** (gates with no memory of their own) computes the next values from the current ones. At 1.8 GHz a tick is 0.55 ns. For a path meant to complete in one tick, the logic and the wires together must settle within that time. Larger computations can be divided into stages, with storage between stages; this is a **pipeline**. Physical design, later in this module, must make each path meet its timing requirement. RTL is the description of what the flip-flops should hold after each tick; it says nothing yet about where anything sits on the die.

NVIDIA's codebase is not 200 billion transistors of hand-written logic; it is a few million lines of parameterized RTL describing one SM, one L2 slice, one memory controller, one NVLink port, and so on, which the **synthesis** tools (synthesis is the step, described under physical design, that turns RTL into gates) **instantiate** (stamp out a copy of, the way one class in software becomes many objects) 144 times, 96 times, 12 times, 18 times. A modern GPU generation is a mix of carried-forward blocks (the video engines, PCIe controller, and display logic change little), evolved blocks (the SM **datapath**, its arithmetic pipeline, is revised every generation), and new blocks (the Transformer Engine's FP8 scaling in Hopper, NV-HBI and the decompression engine in Blackwell).

The work looks more like software engineering than like drafting. NVIDIA does not publish team sizes; on the order of thousands of engineers work on a generation over three to four years, the RTL lives in version control, and every night's changes are run against a **regression suite** (the accumulated set of tests that every change must keep passing, so that a fix in one place cannot silently break another). The milestone that ends this phase is **RTL freeze**: the point after which the logic is not supposed to change, because everything downstream (synthesis, emulation, physical design) is built on it. In practice freezes are partial and staged, block by block, and every late change has a cost measured in re-runs.

> **What can go wrong: the cost of a late change.** What goes wrong at this level is amplified by the replication that makes the design tractable. A bug in the shared SM RTL is replicated 144 times, and so is its fix; a late architecture change (a wider datapath, a different cache policy) re-triggers synthesis of every block that touches it (hours per block), the emulation compile (hours to days, see the next section) and the floorplan, and the nightly regression that guards the code tree (tens of thousands of tests across thousands of CPU cores, an illustrative scale; NVIDIA publishes none of this) must pass again before anyone downstream can trust the RTL. A change that costs an engineer a day before freeze can cost the program a week after it, which is why architects fight over freeze dates and why the verification effort of the next section is sized the way it is. Detection is the regression itself: a test that passed last night and fails tonight points at one day's commits, which is the same discipline as in software, applied to a codebase whose "release" costs a mask set.

Alongside the RTL, the architecture team maintains a **C++ performance model** (a cycle-approximate simulator of the whole GPU) and a **functional model** that runs CUDA programs at a higher abstraction to generate reference results. The RTL is checked against both. As a side note, NVIDIA has also published on using machine learning inside this loop: PrefixRL (reinforcement learning for adder circuit design, used in Hopper), AutoDMP (GPU-accelerated macro placement), and ChipNeMo, a domain-adapted LLM for engineering assistance. These are real but incremental; the flow is still overwhelmingly human engineers plus commercial **EDA** (electronic design automation, the software industry that sells chip-design tools).

## Verification: why NVIDIA runs more emulators than anyone

Verification consumes more engineer-hours than design, because a chip cannot be patched after it is made and a mistake found after tape-out costs months. The first line is **RTL simulation** on Synopsys VCS or Cadence Xcelium: **constrained-random** test benches (the test bench generates random but legal inputs rather than hand-written ones) written in SystemVerilog and **UVM** (the Universal Verification Methodology, the standard framework for writing such test benches) drive each block, with **coverage** metrics (a tally of which states and branches the tests have actually reached) tracking what has been exercised. Simulation is slow because a simulator on a CPU must evaluate every gate that changes, one after another, and a full GPU has billions of them changing per cycle. Simulation of a block runs at perhaps 10–1,000 cycles per second on a CPU; simulation of a full GPU runs at a few cycles per second, which means booting a driver (billions of cycles) is impossible. **Formal verification** (Cadence JasperGold, Synopsys VC Formal) proves properties of small critical blocks (arbiters, the logic that decides which of several requesters goes first; cache-coherence state machines; floating-point rounding) exhaustively, by mathematics rather than by running tests.

For the full chip, the industry uses **hardware emulation**: the RTL is compiled onto a machine built from either custom processor arrays (Cadence Palladium Z2/Z3, whose processing elements execute the Boolean operations of the **netlist**, the gate-level description of the design, in parallel) or large **FPGA** arrays (a **field-programmable gate array** is a chip whose logic can be rewired after manufacture by loading a configuration; Synopsys ZeBu EP1/EP2 are built on AMD/Xilinx FPGAs). Emulation runs at ~1–10 MHz, a million times faster than simulation, fast enough to boot Linux and the CUDA driver, run real **kernels** (a kernel is a program that runs on the GPU), and run real games on a GeForce derivative, months before tape-out. Emulator capacity is quoted in **gates**, meaning two-input NAND equivalents of roughly four transistors each. A single Palladium Z3 rack holds on the order of tens of billions of gates of capacity; emulating a full Blackwell die needs many racks ganged together, and NVIDIA has for over a decade been widely reported (and cited by Cadence) as the largest Palladium customer, with data-center-scale emulation farms. The compile of a full GPU onto the emulator takes many hours to days on large CPU clusters, and every RTL change re-triggers it. The economic logic is simple: an emulator farm costs hundreds of millions of dollars; a **base-layer respin** of a reticle-limited N4 die (a **respin** is re-manufacturing the chip with corrected masks; base-layer means the fix changes the transistor layers and therefore needs a complete new mask set, as opposed to the cheaper metal-only kind described under bring-up) costs a ~$20–30M mask set plus a three-month delay in a market where NVIDIA books tens of billions of dollars per quarter.

> **Worked example: the cycle budget.** Booting Linux plus the GPU driver is on the order of 10<sup>10</sup> clock cycles (an illustrative figure). At 2 MHz on an emulator that takes 10<sup>10</sup> / (2 × 10<sup>6</sup>) = 5,000 s ≈ 1.4 h. At ~5 cycles per second in full-chip simulation it takes 2 × 10<sup>9</sup> s ≈ 60 years. Now capacity: 208 billion transistors at ~4 transistors per gate-equivalent is ~50 billion gates, against Cadence's quoted ceiling of up to 48 billion gates for a fully built-out Z3 system, so a full Blackwell model needs more than one such system before the test bench and the memory models are added on top. That is what "data-center-scale emulation farm" means in practice. The cycle count and the transistors-per-gate ratio are illustrative; the 48-billion-gate ceiling is Cadence's published figure.

> **What can go wrong: escapes.** An **escape** is a bug that reaches silicon. Three kinds matter. A logic bug that only appears at cycle 10<sup>11</sup> under one particular driver sequence, past the horizon of any emulation run. A performance bug that halves throughput on some kernel but passes every functional test, because functional tests check answers, not speed; this is what the C++ performance model is for. And the class emulation cannot see at all: electrical margin, power-grid weakness, and layout-related yield loss such as the 2024 Blackwell flaw described below, because an emulator executes the logic, not the physics of the layout. That third class is why the power, IR-drop and DRC **signoffs** of the physical-design section exist as separate gates (a signoff is a final check the design must pass before tape-out; IR drop is supply voltage lost to wiring resistance and DRC the geometric rule check, both defined there), and why first-silicon bring-up is a discipline of its own.

Beyond functional correctness, **power** is verified pre-silicon by replaying emulation activity traces through power-estimation tools (Synopsys PrimePower, Ansys PowerArtist). The reason is a transient the logic never sees. The **VRM** (voltage regulator module) is the circuit on the board that turns 12 or 48 V into the ~0.8 V the die runs on. When thousands of SMs start a kernel in the same nanoseconds, the current the die draws steps by hundreds of amps within ~100 ns; a regulator cannot ramp that fast (its **slew** capability, the rate at which it can change its output current, is far slower), so the capacitors and the on-die power grid must carry the step or the voltage sags and logic fails. A 700–1,400 W part with a peak-current transient that exceeds the VRM's slew capability will **brown out** (its supply voltage dips below the level at which the logic is reliable) in the lab, and no amount of functional verification will have caught it.

> **Why it matters downstream.** The activity traces from emulation are the start of one unbroken chain that ends at the building. They feed PrimePower and RedHawk (power and IR-drop signoff), whose current maps size the VRM's phase count on the SXM module (how many parallel converters the module's voltage regulator needs), whose power draw sizes the power shelves in the rack (the rack's power supplies), whose heat sizes the coolant distribution unit (the pump-and-heat-exchanger that feeds the cold plates); all three are described in the module and rack sections. A kernel that toggles more transistors than the trace assumed is a rack that trips a breaker.

## The PDK, the libraries, and "4N"

Before the logic can become geometry, everything downstream has to agree on what the process can build. That agreement is the **PDK** (**process design kit**), the package of files a foundry gives its customers describing a process: the DRC/LVS **rule decks** (a deck is the file of rules a checking tool executes; the DRC deck holds the geometric rules and the LVS deck the layout-versus-netlist checks, both defined in the physical-design section), the **SPICE** transistor models (SPICE is the circuit simulator every chip designer uses; its transistor model is a set of equations fitted to measured devices), the parasitic extraction technology files (how much resistance and capacitance each wire shape has), the **layer map** (which drawn layer becomes which physical layer), and the **DFM** rules (**design for manufacturability**: rules that make yield better rather than merely legal). To make a rule concrete: the finest metal layer of the N5 family has a minimum pitch of ~28–30 nm, so a DRC rule says, in effect, "no M0 wire narrower than ~14 nm, no two M0 wires closer than ~14 nm edge to edge", with hundreds of companion rules about how far a wire must extend past a via, how much metal must surround a via, and how many wires may run side by side before a gap is required (figures illustrative of the N5 family; TSMC's actual decks are confidential). A production PDK for a node of this class carries thousands of such rules across ~80 layers, and it is versioned like software: a 1.0 release at process qualification, then point releases as the fab learns which geometries yield badly.

The PDK is the contract between NVIDIA and TSMC: every downstream tool reads it, and a rule-deck update from TSMC can reopen blocks that were already closed. Here is what that looks like in practice. Suppose yield learning on early N4 wafers shows that a particular via-to-line-end geometry shorts more often than the model predicted, and a point release tightens that enclosure rule by a few nanometres. Every block that was DRC-clean under the old deck is re-run under the new one; a block that used the old minimum in ten thousand places now has ten thousand violations, each of which the router must fix by nudging a wire, and each nudge can lengthen a timing-critical path or create a new violation against a neighbour. A late deck update on a reticle-sized design costs days of tool time per block and, at worst, reopens timing closure, which is why the PDK version is frozen by agreement at a milestone and why a foundry that keeps changing its deck is a foundry designers avoid.

On top of the PDK sit the **standard-cell libraries** (the catalogue of pre-designed logic gates, described in the physical-design section) and **memory compilers** (tools that generate blocks of on-die memory to order); TSMC supplies its own, and Arm, Synopsys, and Cadence sell alternatives, but NVIDIA is known to build custom cells for its most critical datapaths (its published NVCell work uses reinforcement learning and simulated annealing to lay out standard cells automatically for new nodes). Each library is **characterized**: every cell is simulated in SPICE at each combination of manufacturing variation, supply voltage and temperature the chip must work at, to measure its delay, its power and the timing margins its inputs need, and the results are tabulated in the Liberty (.lib) files that synthesis and timing analysis read (the physical-design section uses them). Characterization is automated (Synopsys SiliconSmart, Cadence Liberate), because of the arithmetic in the next box.

> **Worked example: the size of a library characterization.** A standard-cell library has a few thousand cells (say 4,000, counting each gate at several drive strengths and threshold voltages). Each cell's Liberty entry is a table of delay against input slew (how slowly the input edge rises) and output load (how much capacitance the cell drives), typically a 7 × 7 grid, for each input-to-output arc, of which a cell has perhaps 4 on average: 4,000 × 4 × 49 ≈ 800,000 SPICE simulations for one corner. Signoff needs the library at every corner the chip will be timed at, say 3 process corners (slow, typical and fast transistors) × 3 voltages × 3 temperatures = 27: ~20 million simulations, plus the same again for power and for setup and hold, ~60 million in all. At about a second each on a CPU core, that is ~60 million core-seconds, on the order of 15,000–20,000 core-hours per library release, which is why a PDK point release that changes the transistor model is a multi-week event before any designer sees a new library. All figures illustrative.

"TSMC 4N" is not a public TSMC node; it is a customized variant of TSMC's N5 family (N4-class) tuned for NVIDIA, with its own rule deck and its own library characterization (the corners and voltages above, run to NVIDIA's targets). **4NP** (Blackwell) is a further performance-tuned derivative, and **3N** (Rubin) is the equivalent on the N3 family (NVIDIA says only "TSMC 3 nm"; trade reports put Rubin on N3P). What "custom" actually means is not disclosed; publicly, TSMC's foundry model allows large customers to negotiate design rules, metal-stack options (thicker top metals for power delivery, more layers), and library cells characterized to the customer's target voltage and temperature. It does not mean a different transistor. The name also has a commercial function: it signals that NVIDIA's wafers are on a dedicated flow that TSMC has committed capacity to.

## IP: the blocks NVIDIA does not design from scratch

No team can design every circuit on a die of this size from scratch, and the interface circuits at the die edge are a specialty of their own, so a GPU of this class contains a large amount of licensed or foundry-provided **IP** (**intellectual property** blocks: finished sub-designs bought or reused rather than written for this chip). Recall that each interface has a PHY (the analog circuit at the die edge) and a controller (the digital scheduler behind it); PHYs are the hard part and the ones most often bought. The list: the HBM3/HBM3E/HBM4 PHY and controller (Rambus, Synopsys, and Cadence sell these; NVIDIA is widely believed to use in-house PHYs on its data-center parts, but has not said so), the PCIe Gen5/Gen6 controller and PHY (Synopsys is the dominant supplier industry-wide), the NVLink **SerDes** (in-house, since it is NVIDIA's proprietary protocol), the CPU cores in **Grace** and **Vera** (NVIDIA's own Arm-based server CPUs, Grace paired with Hopper and Blackwell GPUs and Vera with Rubin; **Neoverse** is Arm's family of licensable server-class core designs, and Grace uses Neoverse cores as licensed, while Vera uses NVIDIA's own custom "Olympus" cores built under an Arm architecture license), and the video codecs, thermal sensors, **PLLs** (phase-locked loops, the circuits that multiply a slow reference clock up to gigahertz), and e-fuse macros.

A **SerDes** (**serializer/deserializer**) recurs throughout this module, so it deserves a full definition: it is the circuit that turns many parallel bits into one very fast stream on a single pair of wires and back again, so that a link can run at 100–200 Gb/s per pair instead of needing hundreds of slow wires. Its health is read from the **eye diagram**: the overlaid picture of many received bits drawn one on top of another, so that the transitions between levels form the edges of an eye shape; the open area in the middle is the margin the receiver has for telling one level from the next, and a closed eye is a link that does not work. The 200 Gb/s-per-lane SerDes in NVLink 5 uses **PAM4** signalling (**pulse-amplitude modulation with four levels**: four voltage levels per symbol instead of two, so each symbol carries two bits). This halves the symbol rate for a given bit rate, and so halves the frequency the wires and connectors must carry, at the cost that the three stacked eyes between four levels are each only one-third as tall as the single eye of a two-level signal: roughly a 9.5 dB loss of noise margin (20 × log<sub>10</sub>(1/3) ≈ −9.5 dB in voltage terms) that the SerDes's equalizers and error-correcting code have to win back. NVLink 5's SerDes is at the edge of what has been done on N4.

Each IP block ships as a **hardened** layout (finished geometry, not editable RTL) plus timing and physical **abstracts** (simplified models of the block's timing and outline for the top-level tools) and is verified on a **test chip** run on a TSMC **MPW shuttle** (**multi-project wafer**, a wafer shared by many customers' small test designs, in TSMC's CyberShuttle program) a year or more before the GPU tapes out, because a SerDes that does not meet its eye margin on real silicon is not something you discover on the production die.

> **What can go wrong: qualifying a PHY on the test chip.** The MPW test chip carries a few instances of the SerDes, a few HBM PHY channels, the PLLs and the e-fuse macro, with loopback paths and the on-die eye monitors described under design for test, on a bench board that lets engineers drive the lanes through real package traces, connectors and a length of cable. The qualification recipe is to sweep each lane across voltage, temperature and channel loss and record the eye opening at every point; a passing PHY shows an eye that stays open across the whole envelope with margin to spare. A failed one shows up in a few characteristic ways. An eye that is open at 25 °C and closes at 85 °C points to a PLL or a receiver bias circuit drifting with temperature. An eye that closes only on the longest channel means the equalizer cannot compensate the loss the package and board actually have, so either the equalizer gets more stages in a revision or the board designers get a tighter loss budget. Bit errors that appear only after minutes of running point to the clock-recovery loop losing lock on long runs of identical bits. And an HBM PHY channel that trains at one voltage and not another is a timing-margin problem in the receiver. Each of these, found on a shuttle a year early, costs a second test-chip pass; found on the production die, it costs a respin of an 800 mm² mask set, which is why no PHY goes onto the GPU without silicon proof.

## Physical design: from netlist to polygons

Once the RTL is frozen, the **physical design** flow turns it into the polygons on ~80 mask layers. Why this is hard, before how it is done. There are ~100 million **nets** (electrically connected groups of wires) to route, and a signal on a long wire is slower than a gate, so where things sit decides whether they are fast enough. Everything must fit inside the 0.55 ns clock period. About 900 A must reach the transistors through the wiring stack without the voltage sagging. And every polygon must obey thousands of manufacturing rules describing what the scanners and etchers of Modules 07–12 can print. No person could draw it; the tools do, and the engineers steer. On the layer count: ~80 masks build ~15–18 metal layers plus the transistor layers, because most levels need two to four masks each (a via level and a wire level, and on the finest layers more than one exposure per level, Modules 07 and 12).

> **Intuition: wires are slower than gates.** A wire has resistance R and capacitance C, and the time to charge it is proportional to R·C. Both R and C grow with the wire's length, so the delay grows with the square of the length: double the wire, quadruple the delay. At 4N wire widths a 1 mm wire can cost more time than ~10 gates (an illustrative ratio), which is why the router breaks long wires with **repeaters** (buffer cells that re-drive the signal), why blocks are kept small and replicated rather than drawn large, and why the placement of a cell matters as much as its speed.

The die is too big to handle flat, so it is partitioned hierarchically: a GPC is one physical block, designed once and instanced 8 times; an SM is a sub-block inside it; the L2 slices, memory controllers, and I/O rings are separate blocks; and a top level stitches them together. Each block goes through the following sequence, and the top level goes through it again with abstracted timing models of the blocks.

1. **Logic synthesis**: the RTL becomes a **netlist** (a list of gates and the nets connecting them) built from library cells chosen to meet the clock; without it nothing has a physical form.
2. **Floorplanning**: the big objects (memories, PHYs, power grid) are placed first, because everything else must fit around them and the current has to get in.
3. **Placement**: every cell is assigned a site, chosen so that critical wires are short and no region is over-crowded.
4. **Clock tree synthesis**: the clock is distributed to every flip-flop so that all of them hear the tick at nearly the same moment.
5. **Routing**: the ~100 million nets are drawn as actual wires on the metal layers, obeying the rules of each layer.
6. **Static timing analysis and closure**: every path is checked against the 0.55 ns budget, and the failures are fixed until none remain.
7. **Power integrity**: the current of the real layout is simulated to prove the voltage holds everywhere and no wire will wear out.
8. **Signal integrity**: neighbouring wires that disturb each other are found and separated.
9. **Physical verification**: every polygon is checked against the foundry's rules and the layout is proved to match the netlist.
10. **Design for test**: the circuitry that will let a tester see inside the finished die is inserted, in parallel with the steps above.

**Logic synthesis** (Synopsys Fusion Compiler or Design Compiler; Cadence Genus) maps the RTL to a gate-level netlist using the foundry's **standard-cell library**: a few thousand pre-designed, pre-characterized cells (NAND2, NOR3, flip-flops, multiplexers, adders), each offered in multiple **drive strengths** (how much current the cell can push, that is how fast it can charge the wire it drives, bought with area and power) and **threshold voltages** (the gate voltage at which a transistor turns on; lower is faster but leaks more current when idle). The tool chooses cells to meet timing at the target clock (~1.7–2.0 GHz boost for H100 SMs) while minimizing area and power, using the library's **Liberty (.lib)** timing tables characterized at multiple process-voltage-temperature **corners** (a corner is an extreme combination of manufacturing variation, supply voltage and temperature at which the chip must still work). Synthesis also inserts the **clock-gating** cells that let idle SMs stop toggling, which is where most dynamic-power savings come from. The reason is the equation for the power a switching circuit burns:

P<sub>dyn</sub> = α · C · V² · f

where α is the **activity factor** (the fraction of the capacitance that switches each cycle), C is the switched capacitance, V the supply voltage and f the clock frequency. Power scales with the square of voltage and linearly with everything else. Dropping V from 0.9 to 0.8 V cuts dynamic power by 1 − (0.8/0.9)² ≈ 21%, which is why every millivolt of supply is argued over; gating the clock to an idle SM sets its α to ~0, which is why clock gating is the biggest single saving.

**Floorplanning** places the big objects first: the **SRAM macros** (pre-built blocks of on-die memory; the 50 MB L2 is hundreds of individual SRAM instances from TSMC's memory compiler, and the register files and L1s inside each SM are more), the HBM PHYs along the two long edges of the die facing the HBM stacks, NVLink and PCIe PHYs along the other edges, and the power grid. The power delivery network on a 700 W die is itself a major design object: the chip draws ~700–1,000 A at ~0.7–0.9 V, so the top two or three thick metal layers are dense meshes of **Vdd** and **Vss** (supply and ground) stripes tied to thousands of **C4 bumps** (the solder balls on the die face that connect it to the package, Module 16), and the placement of every block is constrained by how much current can be brought down through the stack without exceeding the **IR-drop** budget (the voltage lost to resistance on the way in, V = I·R; typically ~5–10% of Vdd from bump to transistor).

> **Worked example: the IR-drop budget.** Take Vdd = 0.8 V and a 5% budget: 0.8 × 0.05 = 40 mV may be lost between the bump and the transistor. At 900 A, the total resistance of that path must be under 40 mV / 900 A ≈ 44 µΩ (microohms). No single wire could do it; the grid works because several thousand power bumps (the count is not disclosed) each carry a fraction of an amp in parallel, and the mesh's job is to spread that current so that no local region under a busy Tensor Core sees more than its share. All values illustrative.

**Placement** (Cadence Innovus, Synopsys IC Compiler II / Fusion Compiler) assigns each of the tens of millions of cells in a block to a legal **site** (a slot in one of the standard-cell rows the floorplan laid out), driven by timing (critical paths short), **congestion** (routing demand kept below **track** supply, a track being a lane on a metal layer where one wire may run), and power. **Clock tree synthesis** then builds the network that distributes the clock to every flip-flop with controlled **skew** (the difference in the moment two flip-flops hear the same tick; two flip-flops hearing it 20 ps apart is like two runners hearing the starting gun at different moments, and the design must tolerate the gap). GPUs typically use a clock **mesh** (a grid of wires driven from many points, so every flip-flop is close to a driver) or a hybrid **H-tree** (a branching tree whose every leaf is the same wire length from the root) plus mesh for the high-**fanout** core clock (fanout: the number of inputs one output drives, here millions), accepting several watts of clock-distribution power to keep skew under ~10–20 ps across a block. **Routing** connects the ~100 million nets in the die across ~15–18 metal layers: global routing assigns nets to regions, track assignment to metal tracks, detailed routing to exact geometries obeying the design rules, and on the lower layers (M0–M3 at 4N, metal layers being numbered from the transistors upward, with ~28–30 nm minimum **pitch** on M0, pitch being the centre-to-centre wire spacing) the router must also respect the **multi-patterning** or **EUV** (extreme ultraviolet, the 13.5 nm lithography of Module 08) single-exposure constraints of each layer (some layers are printed in two or more exposures, so neighbouring wires must alternate between them, Modules 07–08).

**Static timing analysis (STA)** (Synopsys PrimeTime is the signoff standard; Cadence Tempus) then checks every path against **setup** and **hold** constraints (setup: data must arrive at the flip-flop a margin before the tick; hold: it must not change for a margin after it) across all corners and **modes** (the chip's operating configurations, such as normal running versus test), with **on-chip variation** margins (statistical margins for the fact that two nominally identical gates a millimeter apart differ by a few percent in delay). A full-chip GPU signoff involves hundreds of corner-mode combinations and billions of paths. **Parasitic extraction** (Synopsys StarRC, Cadence Quantus) supplies the wire **RC** values (each wire's resistance and capacitance, whose product is its charging time) from the routed geometry, so that the timing analysis sees the real wires and not an idealized netlist.

> **Worked example: the timing budget at 1.8 GHz.** The clock period is 1 / 1.8 GHz = 555 ps. Subtract the flip-flop's clock-to-output delay (~40 ps, the time from the tick until its output is valid), the setup margin of the next flip-flop (~20 ps), and the skew and **jitter** (cycle-to-cycle wobble of the clock itself, ~30 ps together): 555 − 40 − 20 − 30 ≈ 465 ps is left for logic. A 4N gate driving a short wire takes ~15–25 ps, so a path can hold ~20–30 levels of logic per cycle. A signal that has to cross a 1–2 mm SM on a mid-level wire costs ~100 ps or more, a quarter of the budget, before it has done any computing; this is why the SM is the size it is and why signals that cross the die take several cycles with flip-flops along the way. All values are illustrative; NVIDIA publishes none of them.

**Timing closure** is the loop of finding violating paths, fixing them with cell upsizing, buffering, re-placement, or, if necessary, RTL changes, and re-running; it consumes weeks per block and is the single largest schedule risk in physical design.

**Power integrity signoff** (Ansys RedHawk-SC is the standard; Cadence Voltus) simulates the current draw of the placed-and-routed design under realistic **activity vectors** (the emulation activity traces of the verification section, converted into a per-net record of which signals switch in each cycle so the power tool can replay them) and computes IR drop and **electromigration** across the full grid (electromigration: the electron flow physically pushes copper atoms along the wire until it thins and opens; Module 12 gives the physics). Hot spots force grid re-work or the insertion of **decoupling capacitors** (on-die capacitors that store charge next to the logic and supply it in the nanoseconds before the board can respond). **Signal integrity** (**crosstalk**-induced delay and glitches, crosstalk being a switching wire disturbing its neighbour through the capacitance between them) is analyzed inside STA. **Thermal** analysis is done at the package level with the die's power map.

**Physical verification** is the last gate. **DRC** (design rule checking) verifies every polygon against the thousands of geometric rules in the PDK: minimum widths, spacings, enclosures, densities, **antenna** ratios (during plasma etch a long metal line collects charge that can punch through the thin gate oxide it connects to, so the rule limits metal area per gate), and the pattern-matching **forbidden pitch** rules (a spacing the lens cannot print cleanly even though wider and narrower spacings are fine) that came with **193i** (193 nm immersion **deep-ultraviolet (DUV)** lithography, Module 07) multi-patterning and EUV. **LVS** (layout versus schematic) extracts the transistors and wires from the polygons and proves the layout matches the netlist. The near-universal signoff tool is Siemens Calibre (nmDRC, nmLVS, and the DFM **fill** insertion, which adds dummy metal shapes to empty regions so that every part of the wafer polishes flat when the copper is planarized, and the **litho-hotspot** checks, a pattern search for layouts that obey every rule but that the lithography model predicts will print badly); Synopsys IC Validator and Cadence Pegasus compete but TSMC's reference flows are Calibre-first. A full-chip DRC run on an 800 mm² N4 die takes many hours on hundreds of CPU cores and typically returns tens of thousands of violations in the first pass, most of them at block boundaries.

**DFT** (**design for test**) insertion happens in parallel with synthesis and placement. Every flip-flop is made part of a **scan chain** so that test patterns can be shifted in and captured out through a few hundred pins; the patterns come from **ATPG** (**automatic test pattern generation**; Synopsys TestMAX, Siemens Tessent) targeting **stuck-at** faults (the test assumes a wire is permanently stuck at 0 or 1) and **transition** faults (the test assumes a wire does switch, but too slowly for the new value to arrive within one clock cycle) with **compression** ratios of ~100:1 (patterns are stored and shifted in compressed form) so that test time at sort stays in seconds. Every SRAM instance gets **MBIST** (memory built-in self-test) logic that exercises it with **March** algorithms (a family of memory tests that walk through every address writing and reading patterns) at speed. The HBM PHYs, NVLink and PCIe SerDes get **loopback** (the transmitter's output is fed back into its own receiver) and eye-monitoring test modes. A **JTAG** (IEEE 1149.1, the standard four-wire debug port) and IJTAG (IEEE 1687) network ties all of this together, and it is the same network that bring-up engineers will use to debug first silicon. The e-fuse block that records which SMs and HBM controllers are disabled at sort is also part of this infrastructure (see Module 14).

> **What can go wrong: the timing-closure spiral and its relatives.** Fixing one failing path by upsizing its cells makes them bigger and hungrier, which crowds the neighbours (congestion), lengthens their wires (new timing failures) and raises the local current (new IR-drop hot spots); a block can circle for weeks, and the escape is sometimes an RTL change that restarts the emulation compile. IR hot spots appear under the Tensor Cores, where the densest arithmetic sits, as red regions on the RedHawk map. Boundary DRC violations cluster where two separately designed blocks meet and their wire spacings disagree; Calibre's results database lists them by rule and location. Antenna violations are fixed with a small diode that drains the charge or by hopping the wire to a higher layer. Each is found by a report, not by silicon, which is the whole point of the flow.

## Tape-out: from polygons to masks

The layout is finished, but a mask is not a copy of the layout, and the reason is the physics of Module 07: the scanner prints features smaller than the light's wavelength, so what is drawn is not what prints. **Tape-out** is the moment the final layout database is sent to the foundry. The native format is **GDSII** (a 1970s binary stream format for polygons) or, for anything this size, **OASIS**, a newer format roughly 5–10× more compact. A hierarchical N4 full-chip layout for an 800 mm² die runs from tens to a few hundred gigabytes in OASIS, and it grows enormously through the next step.

> **Intuition: OPC as pre-distortion for a blurry projector.** Diffraction rounds every corner and shortens every line end, like a slide projected slightly out of focus. If you know exactly how the projector blurs, you can draw the slide with exaggerated corners and extra dots that the blur turns into the intended shape. That is optical proximity correction: the mask carries a deliberately wrong pattern so that the wafer receives the right one (Modules 07–08).

**Mask data preparation** (MDP) at TSMC turns the design layers into mask layers. First the Boolean layer operations and sizing (combining drawn layers with AND, OR and NOT and growing or shrinking shapes) that convert the drawn layers into the physical shapes to be printed. Then **OPC** (optical proximity correction), which pre-distorts every edge so that after the diffraction, the resist blur (the smearing of the image in the light-sensitive resist coating) and the etch bias (the small widening or narrowing the etch adds) of the real process, the printed pattern matches the intent; for the most critical layers this is **ILT** (inverse lithography technology), which solves for the mask pattern as a free-form optimization rather than by moving edge segments, producing curvilinear masks with **sub-resolution assist features** (mask features too small to print themselves that shape the light for their neighbours). ILT on a full chip is one of the largest compute jobs in the industry: TSMC's computational lithography ran on tens of thousands of CPU cores, and this is why NVIDIA built **cuLitho**, a GPU-accelerated library for OPC/ILT that TSMC and Synopsys brought into production in 2024, with NVIDIA claiming that 350 H100 systems replace 40,000 CPU servers and deliver ~40× speedups, plus a further ~2× from a generative-AI initialization step. After OPC the data is **fractured** (every shape is cut into the rectangles or pixels the mask writer understands; trapezoids or, for multi-beam writers, pixel maps), checked by **mask rule check** (the mask shop's own set of what its writer can make), and sent to the mask shop. Post-OPC data for a full mask set runs into terabytes.

**Mask making** (TSMC has its own mask shops; Photronics, Toppan, and DNP serve the merchant market) writes each layer onto a 6-inch quartz blank. For DUV layers the blank is **chrome-on-quartz** (an opaque chrome film on a transparent quartz plate, patterned by removing chrome) or **MoSi attenuated phase-shift** (a molybdenum-silicide film that lets ~6% of the light through with its phase inverted, which sharpens the printed edges), written on a **multi-beam e-beam writer** (a machine that exposes the mask's resist pixel by pixel with a beam of electrons, like a very slow and very fine laser printer; multi-beam writers run on the order of hundreds of thousands of beams in parallel; IMS Nanofabrication MBMW, and IMS is majority-owned by Intel, with Bain Capital and TSMC holding minority stakes since 2023) or a laser writer. For **EUV** layers the mask cannot be transparent, because everything absorbs 13.5 nm light; the blank is a low-thermal-expansion glass substrate carrying a 40-bilayer Mo/Si multilayer mirror (alternating molybdenum and silicon layers each a few nanometres thick that reflect ~65–70% of the light by constructive interference, Module 08) with a tantalum-based absorber (blanks from Hoya and AGC), written on a multi-beam writer over ~10–20 hours, inspected with **actinic** tools (inspected with the same 13.5 nm light the scanner uses, so the inspection sees the defects the scanner would see; Lasertec ACTIS) and e-beam, repaired at the atom scale by Zeiss MeRiT electron-beam tools, and fitted with a **pellicle** (a thin membrane stretched above the mask that holds dust out of the focal plane, Module 07). At N4 a full set is roughly 80 or more masks, of which ~12–15 are EUV. Per-mask prices are not published; industry estimates put an EUV mask on the order of $300–500k and a critical DUV mask at $50–100k, with the non-critical DUV masks cheaper still. Summed naively, ~12–15 EUV masks plus ~65–70 DUV masks come to roughly $7–14 million. The ~$20–30 million usually quoted for a full N4-class mask set is a separate, all-in industry estimate that also counts the re-writes, repairs and revisions a set goes through before it is production-worthy; read the two figures as the floor and the all-in cost of one design revision, and either way the set is usable only for that revision. This is the sense in which a "respin" costs tens of millions before a single wafer moves.

> **Worked example: mask-set amortization.** Spread a $25M mask set over 10,000 wafers (~620,000 gross dies at 62 per wafer): $25,000,000 / 10,000 = $2,500 per wafer, about 15% on top of a ~$16,500 wafer. Over 100,000 wafers it is $250 per wafer, 1.5%. Masks are a fixed cost: decisive for a test chip or a small run, and nearly invisible for a product built on this scale. The cost of a respin, for a product like this, is the calendar, not the dollars.

A test chip goes on a shuttle; a GPU gets a **full run**: its own dedicated lot (typically 25 wafers), with the full mask set, on the production flow. First silicon lots are run as **hot lots** or "super-hot lots" (lots given priority at every tool, so they jump every queue), cutting the ~100–120 day cycle time to ~60–75 days. Add a few weeks for CoWoS (first silicon for a CoWoS part must be packaged with real HBM before it can even be probed properly) and it is roughly three months from tape-out to the first packaged part on a lab bench.

> **What can go wrong.** A printable defect found at actinic inspection (a particle or a pit in the multilayer that would print on every die) sends the blank back for repair or the mask for a re-write, which is days to weeks. And a first lot that fails the wafer acceptance test (the electrical check of test structures on every wafer, defined in the timeline section) because of a process excursion (a tool that drifted during those 60–75 days) is not a design problem; telling a process excursion apart from a design flaw is the first job of the bring-up team, and Module 13's fab-side data is what settles it.

## First silicon, bring-up, and respins

Bring-up is the first time the design meets physics, and it happens in NVIDIA's own labs: the packaged part is mounted on a custom bring-up board with bench instruments (oscilloscopes, precision supplies) and a thermal chamber around it, while the first sort maps come from the ATE (automatic test equipment) at KYEC or TSMC (Module 18). It begins with the JTAG network: can the chip be seen, do the **PLLs lock** (the PLL, from the IP section, multiplies a slow reference clock up to gigahertz; **lock** means it has settled onto the target frequency), can the on-die sensors be read. Then power: the die is stepped through voltage and frequency on each **rail** (a separate supply voltage) to make **shmoo** plots (two-dimensional maps of pass or fail across voltage and frequency; holes in the map point to specific marginal paths) and find the operating envelope. Then memory: the HBM PHYs are **trained** (the PHY sweeps its sampling time on each of its thousand-plus wires to find the centre of the data eye), and a failed HBM channel here is one of the more common first-silicon problems. Then the firmware boots, the driver loads, and CUDA kernels run; then months of characterization across thousands of parts to set the production voltage/frequency tables, **binning** rules (how parts are sorted by measured speed and power), and the harvesting configuration.

Bugs found at this stage go into an **errata** list (the formal record of known defects in a silicon revision) and are triaged: fixable in firmware or the driver (most), fixable with a **metal-only respin** (change only the interconnect layers, re-using front-end wafers held at the contact layer, costing a few million dollars in masks and ~6–8 weeks), or requiring a **base-layer respin** (full new mask set, full cycle time, ~3–4 months). The metal-only option exists because of **wafer banking**: the fab deliberately stops some wafers of the first lots just before the first metal layer and holds them; if only the wiring changes, those wafers are finished with the new metal masks, which is why a metal respin takes weeks rather than months. **Steppings** (silicon revisions) are named by letter and number: A0 is first silicon, A1 a metal respin, B0 a base-layer respin.

> **What can go wrong: the common first-silicon failures.** An HBM channel that will not train, which may be a PHY timing problem in the design or a bad CoWoS micro-bump under that channel; the two are told apart by whether the failure follows the die or the package. Shmoo holes at the cold, low-voltage corner, typically hold violations (data changing too soon after the tick) on a handful of paths, found by correlating the failing frequency and voltage with the timing reports. PLL jitter that closes the eye on the fastest SerDes lanes, seen on the eye monitors built in at DFT. And a yield-limiting layout flaw that only shows as a spatial pattern on the sort maps (a ring, a stripe, one corner of every die), localized by failure analysis of the failing dies (Module 13). Each has a different owner: the first two are design, the third can be either, the fourth is settled by cutting a die open.

NVIDIA does not publish its stepping history, but the Blackwell program made one public in 2024: a yield-limiting design flaw found in mid-2024 forced a mask change (reported to involve the top metal layers and bumps, i.e. a metal-level respin) that pushed B200 volume by roughly a quarter, and in October 2024 Jensen Huang confirmed it was a "design flaw", "100% NVIDIA's fault", fixed with TSMC's help. That single event cost on the order of one production quarter of the most valuable product line in the industry, which is the cleanest possible illustration of why the verification budget is what it is.

Rubin CPX was announced in September 2025 as a GDDR7-based companion part for the prefill phase of inference, with availability stated as end of 2026. The reticle limit that bounds every "reticle die" in this table is 26 × 33 mm = 858 mm² (see Module 07); GH100 at 814 mm² is 95% of it.

## The manufacturing timeline of one GPU

This section is the module's central process: one B200 package followed from wafer start to a GPU on a board, through every factory it visits. The problem it answers is why a product whose fab processing takes four months takes six months to build and, in 2023, a year to receive. The dates are representative; NVIDIA and TSMC do not publish cycle times, and the ranges below are assembled from foundry norms (Modules 05, 13, 17, 18) and public reporting. The nine steps, with what physically happens and why each takes as long as it does:

1. **Wafer processing at TSMC Fab 18 (~90–120 days).** A lot of 25 wafers starts on the N4P flow and goes through ~80 mask layers and ~1,000-plus unit steps, first the front end (the transistors) and then the **back end of line (BEOL)**, the copper wiring above them. Each mask layer is ~10–15 unit steps (deposit, coat, expose, develop, etch, strip, clean, inspect), each with queue time in front of a shared tool (Module 05), which is why a layer costs ~1.3–1.5 days even though the actual processing is hours.
2. **Wafer acceptance test, wafer sort and harvest map (~1 week).** **Wafer acceptance test (WAT)** measures the test transistors in the **scribe lines** (the gaps between dies where the saw will later cut) to confirm the process landed where it should; then **wafer sort** probes every die for seconds to minutes on an Advantest V93000, and the **harvest map** (which SMs and which memory partition each die will run with) is burned into its e-fuses (Module 14).
3. **Micro-bumping, backgrind and dicing at a TSMC advanced-packaging fab (~1–2 weeks).** The wafer receives its **micro-bumps** (copper pillars with a solder cap at ~40 µm pitch, versus ~130–150 µm for the larger C4 bumps that will later join the package to the substrate), goes through **backgrinding** (thinning from the back to a fraction of its starting thickness) and **dicing** (sawing into individual dies).
4. **HBM stacks arrive in parallel.** The eight HBM3E stacks come as **known-good stacked die**: each stack is eight DRAM dies on a base die, wired through TSVs, tested as a unit before it ships. Their own DRAM fab cycle of ~2–3 months plus ~1–2 weeks of stacking and test started ~3 months earlier, at SK hynix, Micron or Samsung.
5. **Chip-on-Wafer (~2–3 weeks).** **CoWoS** is **Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate**, and this is the first half: the two GPU dies and eight HBM stacks are attached, micro-bump to micro-bump, onto the still-whole CoWoS-L interposer wafer, whose LSI bridges are embedded in its RDL layers. **Underfill** (epoxy flowed under the dies so the bumps do not crack under thermal stress) and **mold** (an epoxy compound cast over the assembly for rigidity) follow, then the interposer wafer is thinned to **reveal the TSVs** (the through-silicon vias are exposed on the back so they can be connected), and C4 bumps are placed on that back face.
6. **Wafer-on-Substrate (~1–2 weeks).** The second half: the interposer wafer is cut into individual modules, and each module is reflowed (heated so the solder melts) onto its **ABF substrate**, a package board built of ~10–14 **build-up layers** per side, each a sheet of **Ajinomoto build-up film** (ABF, an epoxy film) laminated and patterned with copper. Underfill again, then a **stiffener** (a metal frame that keeps the substrate flat) and either a **lid** (the metal cap the cold plate or heatsink will press on) or no lid at all (**bare die**: the cold plate presses directly on the silicon through a thin thermal interface material, as on some SXM and GB200 parts, which removes one layer from the heat's path at the cost of a die that must not be chipped during handling), plus the capacitors on the substrate. This step runs at TSMC or at an **OSAT** (an outsourced semiconductor assembly and test house, such as ASE/SPIL or Amkor).
7. **Final test, burn-in and system-level test (~1–2 weeks).** The packaged part is tested on ATE, run hot at elevated voltage in **burn-in** to weed out early-life failures, run under real software in **system-level test (SLT)**, and sorted into speed and power bins (Module 18).
8. **Board assembly at an ODM (~1–2 weeks).** An **ODM** (original design manufacturer, a company that builds hardware to another company's design) reflows the package onto an SXM module or, for GB200, onto the **Bianca** board (the superchip board that carries one Grace CPU and two GPUs, described in the GB200 section), and tests the board.
9. **Tray and rack integration and rack-level burn-in (~2–4 weeks).** Boards go into compute trays, trays into racks, the rack runs under load for days, and the rack ships.

So the **cycle time** (time a given unit of material spends moving through the chain) from wafer start to a GPU shipping on a board is roughly six months, and to a burned-in rack, seven or more. The **lead time** a customer experiences is longer, because the chain has to be primed: HBM DRAM wafers start two to three months before the logic wafer arrives at CoWoS, substrates are ordered 8–12 weeks out, and NVIDIA must commit to TSMC wafer and CoWoS capacity a year or more ahead. In 2023 the widely quoted H100 lead time reached ~52 weeks; NVIDIA's executives on the mid-2023 earnings calls described "significantly long" supply cycle times and said they had already placed purchase commitments for the following year, which is what a ~12-month planning horizon looks like from the inside. The distinction matters for reading the industry: a 52-week lead time was mostly queueing for CoWoS and HBM allocation, not 52 weeks of processing.

> **Worked example: dies per wafer and cost per good die for H100.**
> GH100 is 814 mm²; treat it as ~26 × 31.3 mm to fit the 26 × 33 mm reticle. The standard gross-die estimate for a 300 mm wafer (usable diameter ~297 mm after edge exclusion, d = 297) is
> N = π(d/2)² / A − π d / √(2A) = (69,300 / 814) − (933 / 40.3) = 85.1 − 23.1 ≈ 62 gross die per wafer,
> where A is the die area in mm² and d the usable wafer diameter in mm; the first term is how many dies would fit if the wafer were all usable area, and the second term subtracts the partial dies lost around the curved edge. Real placements with scribe lanes give ~60–65, matching what has been reported. Now yield. At a mature N4 defect density of D0 ≈ 0.1 per cm², the Poisson model gives Y = exp(−A·D0) = exp(−8.14 × 0.1) = 0.44 (in words: if defects land at random at D0 per cm², the chance that a die of area A escapes all of them is e<sup>−A·D0</sup>), and Murphy's model ((1 − e<sup>−AD</sup>)/AD)² gives 0.47 (Murphy assumes defects cluster somewhat, which is kinder to big dies). That would be ~28 perfect die per wafer, which is why nobody ships a perfect GH100. With 12 of 144 SMs and 1 of 6 HBM controllers held in reserve, a die with a defect in an SM or in one memory partition is still sellable, so only a defect in the non-redundant part of the die kills it. Model that with the same formula on a smaller area: the die section put the SMs at ~60% of the area, so L2, crossbar, PHYs, controllers and glue are ~40%, less the spare memory partition; call the unprotected fraction ~35%, and Y<sub>sellable</sub> ≈ exp(−0.35 × 8.14 × 0.1) ≈ exp(−0.28) ≈ 0.75. Redundancy never covers exactly the area assumed (a defect that kills a thirteenth SM, or two SMs in the same TPC, still scraps the die), so the effective yield of *sellable* die is roughly 65–80% depending on how much area the redundancy really protects, or ~40–50 die per wafer. At a 4N wafer price on the order of $16,000–17,000, that is $16,500 / 45 ≈ $370 per sellable die, consistent with the ~$300–400 range that appears in most public BOM estimates. The lesson is that harvesting can make large dies more economical, while defect density and the location and correlation of defects remain essential. These sellable-die counts are model outputs, not disclosed NVIDIA wafer yields.

> **What can go wrong.** A CoWoS assembly defect (a missed micro-bump, a crack in an LSI bridge, a void in the underfill) scraps two good dies plus eight tested HBM stacks, on the order of $4,000 or more of known-good parts at this module's BOM numbers, because a bonded assembly cannot be taken apart; this is why every part is tested before it goes on the interposer (Module 14's known-good-die argument). An HBM stack that passed its own test can still fail after packaging, when its thousand-plus micro-bumps are stressed by the first thermal cycles. A substrate carrying a 3.3-reticle interposer warps as it heats, because silicon, copper and epoxy expand at different rates, and a warped substrate lifts bumps at the corners; the stiffener exists to fight this. And a hot lot that loses its queue priority for a day at one tool, because a production lot was more urgent, loses that day for the whole program.

> **Why it matters downstream.** Six months of cycle time plus the priming of HBM, substrates and CoWoS slots means NVIDIA must commit to wafer, CoWoS and HBM volumes roughly twelve months ahead of shipment. When demand jumped in 2023 the commitments could not, which produced the 52-week queue; and when customers saw a 52-week queue they ordered from every channel at once, which produced the double-ordering that later distorted everyone's demand signals. A manufacturing constant became a financial-market event.

## The SXM module

The third group of sections follows the packaged GPU into the module, the board, the server and the rack. The packaged GPU does not plug into anything directly. It is reflowed (heated in an oven so the solder melts and joins) onto a small, extremely dense **PCB** (**printed circuit board**: layers of copper foil and glass-epoxy laminated together), the **SXM** module (NVIDIA's name for its mezzanine GPU module format; the letters are not officially expanded; SXM4 for A100, SXM5 for H100/H200, and a Blackwell-generation module for the HGX B200/B300), which is what actually sits in a server. The module carries three things besides the package, and the problem it solves is that a package wants ~1,250 A at less than a volt and gives back ~1 kW of heat from a few square centimetres.

The **VRM** (voltage regulator module) converts the 12 V (or 48 V on newer boards) that the baseboard supplies into the ~0.7–0.9 V core rail plus separate rails for HBM, PHYs, and I/O, delivering 700 W on H100 and ~1,000–1,400 W on Blackwell parts. At 0.8 V, 1,000 W is 1,250 A, so the VRM is a multiphase **buck converter** (a switching regulator that chops the input with a transistor and smooths the result through an inductor and capacitor to a lower voltage; a **phase** is one such converter, and 16–30 of them run in parallel, staggered in time, because no single one can carry 1,250 A). Each phase is a **power stage** (an integrated pair of **MOSFETs**, large discrete versions of the transistor inside the chip, with their driver, from Monolithic Power Systems, which was NVIDIA's primary supplier through the Hopper generation, plus Infineon, Renesas, and onsemi on later boards), an inductor per phase (Vishay, Cyntec, Delta), and a digital controller. The power stages sit as close to the package as possible because every millimeter of copper between the VRM and the C4 bumps is resistance and inductance the core-voltage transient response has to fight. Around the package are hundreds of **MLCCs** (multilayer ceramic capacitors, from Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, TDK, and Taiyo Yuden; the highest-value ones sit in the **substrate cavity**, a recess in the underside of the package substrate directly beneath the die, where they are millimetres from the transistors they serve) that supply the current for the nanoseconds between a load step and the VRM's response. Getting this right is a significant part of why an SXM board is a 1,000-plus-component object.

> **Worked example: current and I²R loss on the board.** 1,000 W at 0.8 V is 1,250 A. Suppose the copper between the power stages and the package has a total resistance of 0.1 mΩ: the loss is I²·R = 1,250² × 0.0001 = 156 W, a sixth of the GPU's own power turned into board heat before it reaches the die. At 0.02 mΩ the loss is 31 W. That factor of five is why the power stages sit millimetres from the package, why the board carries thick copper planes rather than traces, and why 48 V input (a quarter of the current for the same power, upstream of the VRM) has spread through newer designs. All resistances illustrative.

The **mezzanine connector** (a board-to-board connector that stacks one board parallel to another) connects the module to the baseboard: on SXM5 this is a pair of high-density connectors carrying the 18 NVLink ports, PCIe x16, power, and management signals (Amphenol and TE Connectivity supply this class of connector). NVLink at 50–100 Gb/s per lane through a connector is a **signal-integrity** problem in its own right (signal integrity: whether a fast pulse still has a recognisable shape after the wires, **vias** (the drilled, plated holes that connect one board layer to another) and connectors have attenuated and smeared it), and the **channel budget** (the total loss, in decibels, the receiver can tolerate before it can no longer tell the bits apart; 30 dB means the signal arrives at one-thousandth of its power) is why the switch chips are on the same baseboard rather than on a separate card.

The **thermal solution** for an air-cooled HGX H100 is a large **vapor-chamber** heatsink (a sealed copper chamber with a wick and a working fluid that boils at the hot spot and condenses at the fins, spreading the die's ~700 W over a fin stack that the server's fans drive air through at high **static pressure** (the fans must push hard against the resistance of a dense fin stack); Auras, AVC, Cooler Master, and Delta supply these), mated to the lid with a **thermal interface material** (**TIM**: a paste, pad or soft metal that fills the microscopic air gaps between the lid and the heatsink, since air is an excellent insulator) and a spring-loaded retention frame that applies a controlled load. For liquid-cooled Blackwell and the NVL72, the heatsink is replaced by a **cold plate**: a copper block with microchannels that water at ~25–45 °C inlet flows through, removing ~1,000–1,400 W from a surface a few square centimeters across; CoolIT, Boyd, Asetek, Auras, and Delta are the main suppliers, and the plate's flatness and the TIM under it determine whether the GPU throttles.

> **Worked example: the thermal-resistance budget.** Take a maximum allowed transistor temperature of ~85–90 °C and 45 °C inlet water, so ~40 °C of temperature difference is available. If 1,200 W must cross that difference, the total **thermal resistance** (temperature rise per watt) from transistor to water may be at most 40 / 1,200 ≈ 0.033 K/W, and that budget is shared by the die itself, the first TIM, the lid, the second TIM, the copper plate and the water film in the channels. A TIM that dries out and adds 0.01 K/W adds 0.01 × 1,200 = 12 °C, and the GPU throttles. All values illustrative.

> **What can go wrong.** Voltage droop and over-current trips when a kernel launches and the current steps faster than the VRM can follow, caught in system-level test as a crash under a specific workload. TIM **pump-out**, in which repeated heating and cooling cycles slowly squeeze the paste out from between lid and plate, raising thermal resistance over months. Solder-joint fatigue on a 1,000-component board that flexes with every thermal cycle. And throttling from a plate that is not flat enough to load the whole lid evenly. The first is found in SLT, the rest in rack-level burn-in or, worst, in the field (Module 18).

## The HGX baseboard

Eight GPUs are only a system if every one can talk to every other at full speed, and the board that makes that true has to carry 144 NVLink ports across half a metre of copper without losing the signals. Eight SXM modules mount on one **HGX** baseboard, along with the **NVSwitch** chips that connect them: an NVSwitch is a chip whose only job is to forward NVLink traffic between GPUs, like an Ethernet switch, so that every GPU reaches every other at full speed without going through a CPU. On HGX H100, four third-generation NVSwitch chips, each with 64 NVLink ports at 50 GB/s, give every GPU its full 900 GB/s to every other GPU without any host involvement. On HGX B200/B300, two fifth-generation NVLink switch chips (72 ports of 100 GB/s each, 7.2 TB/s per chip) supply each of the eight GPUs with 1.8 TB/s.

The baseboard is a large (~60 × 40 cm), heavy PCB with on the order of 20–30+ layers because it carries 8 × 18 NVLink ports, 8 × PCIe Gen5 x16, and a power plane that delivers 6–10 kW. The problem the board solves is loss. Every high-speed trace runs on low-loss **laminate** (the insulating sheet the copper foil is bonded to): Panasonic Megtron 6 or Megtron 7 (a polyphenylene-ether resin with a **dielectric loss tangent** of ~0.002–0.005 at 10 GHz, versus ~0.02 for **FR-4**, the ordinary glass-epoxy board material), with EMC and Isola equivalents. The loss tangent is the fraction of the signal's energy the insulator absorbs per cycle; ten times lower means a trace can be several times longer for the same loss, which is what buys the reach from an SXM connector to a switch chip across a 60 cm board. The copper foil is chosen for low surface roughness to limit **skin-effect** loss: at gigahertz the current flows only in the outer fraction of a micrometre of the copper, so a rough surface lengthens the path the current follows and raises the resistance. As an illustration of the budget: a receiver at these rates tolerates on the order of 30 dB of channel loss, and a board trace, its vias and two mezzanine connectors have to share it, so on ordinary FR-4 the traces would run out of budget in a fraction of the distance the board needs.

The fabrication is done by Unimicron, Ibiden, WUS Printed Circuit, Victory Giant Technology, Gold Circuit Electronics, and TTM, and the assembly (placing ~10,000-plus components, including the GPUs themselves via mezzanine connectors after board-level test) is done by the ODMs. What goes wrong: a mis-registered via (a drilled connection between layers that misses its pad by a few tens of micrometres on one of thirty layers) scraps the whole board, because a board cannot be repaired inside; and NVLink lane errors, a single lane of the 144 that will not run clean, are found at board test with the SerDes eye monitors before the GPUs are mounted. HGX baseboards are sold by NVIDIA as a unit, to be integrated into a chassis by the **OEM** (**original equipment manufacturer**: a server brand such as Dell or HPE that sells under its own name, as distinct from the ODM that does the building; the two are contrasted under Who builds it); the reported list price of an HGX H100 baseboard was ~$200–300k in 2023.

## The server: DGX and its OEM cousins

A baseboard cannot run by itself: it needs a CPU to launch work, storage to feed it and a network to reach other servers. Around the baseboard, a complete 8-GPU server adds: the **CPU tray** (two **x86** CPUs, the Intel/AMD processor family; Intel Xeon Platinum 8480C on DGX H100, Xeon 8570 on DGX B200, or AMD EPYC on many OEM designs) with 2 TB of **DDR5** (standard server DRAM, soldered as separate chips on modules rather than stacked) and **NVMe** storage (flash drives connected over PCIe); **PCIe switches** (Broadcom PEX-series Gen5 switches, chips that fan the CPUs' limited PCIe lanes out to the GPUs and network cards); eight **ConnectX-7** (400 Gb/s) or **ConnectX-8** (800 Gb/s) **NICs** (**network interface cards**, the parts that connect a server to the data-center network), one per GPU, so that each GPU has a dedicated path to the scale-out network; two **BlueField-3** **DPUs** (**data processing units**: NICs with their own CPU cores that take storage and management traffic off the main CPUs); and a power supply set of 6 × 3.3 kW or so. DGX H100 is rated at 10.2 kW maximum; DGX B200 at 14.3 kW. NVIDIA sells DGX as its own branded server at roughly $300–400k for H100 (list) and higher for B200; Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro sell functionally similar HGX-based servers at their own margins; and **hyperscalers** (the largest cloud operators: Amazon, Microsoft, Google, Meta and their peers) buy HGX baseboards for their own chassis designs.

Now the why of each part. The CPUs do surprisingly little arithmetic during training: they run the operating system and the CUDA driver, launch kernels (each launch is a few microseconds of CPU work that starts milliseconds of GPU work), read training data from storage and decode it into tensors, and orchestrate the **collective operations** (the synchronized exchanges, such as summing gradients across every GPU, that end each training step); the heavy lifting is on the GPUs, and a CPU that falls behind starves them. The PCIe switches exist because of a lane budget. A server CPU has a fixed number of PCIe lanes (80 per Xeon 8480C, so 160 for the pair), and each GPU wants a x16 link, each NIC a x16 link and each NVMe drive x4; demand exceeds supply, so switch chips fan a few CPU x16 links out to many devices and, more importantly, let a GPU and its NIC talk to each other through the switch without the traffic ever entering the CPU. That is the point of one NIC per GPU: with **GPUDirect RDMA** (remote direct memory access straight from the NIC into GPU memory), a gradient leaving GPU 3 for another server goes GPU → PCIe switch → NIC → network, a path that never touches CPU memory and never waits for a CPU core, and the eight NICs give the server 8 × 400 or 8 × 800 Gb/s of scale-out bandwidth, matched one-for-one to the GPUs so that no GPU queues behind another's traffic. A PCIe Gen5 x16 link carries ~64 GB/s each way, against 900 GB/s of NVLink per GPU, which is why GPU-to-GPU traffic inside the server goes over NVLink and PCIe is reserved for the host and the network. The DPUs take the remaining chores (storage traffic, the management network, encryption) off the main CPUs.

> **Worked example: the PCIe lane budget of an 8-GPU server.** Count the lanes the devices want: 8 GPUs × 16 + 8 NICs × 16 + 2 DPUs × 16 + 8 NVMe drives × 4 = 128 + 128 + 32 + 32 = 320 lanes, against 160 lanes on two CPUs (device counts follow the DGX H100 configuration; the lane arithmetic is illustrative). The switches provide the fan-out, and it works because the CPU-facing links only carry the traffic that actually goes to the CPU (data loading, kernel launches, checkpoints), a small fraction of the GPU-to-NIC traffic that flows switch-locally. In bandwidth terms: at ~64 GB/s per x16 link, the eight NIC links can move up to ~512 GB/s of gradients out of the server; if that traffic had to be staged through CPU memory instead, every byte would be written into DRAM and read back out by a CPU core, adding a copy at the CPU's memory bandwidth and microseconds of latency to every exchange, which is what the GPUDirect path through the switch avoids.

> **What can go wrong.** A PCIe link that trains at Gen4 speed or x8 width instead of Gen5 x16 (a marginal connector, a bent pin, a firmware setting) halves the bandwidth to one GPU's NIC, and shows up as one slow worker holding up every synchronization step of a training job; fleet tools check link width and speed on every boot for this reason. A CPU-side data pipeline that cannot decode and feed samples fast enough leaves the GPUs idle, visible as low GPU utilization with high CPU load. A NIC whose GPUDirect path is misconfigured falls back to bouncing data through CPU memory, which works but at a fraction of the rate. And at 10–14 kW in an air-cooled chassis a single failed fan raises the inlet temperature of the GPUs behind it until they throttle; the baseboard management controller logs and alarms these, and the DGX design pushes air front to back through the vapor-chamber fin stacks of the SXM section at high static pressure for exactly this reason.

## GB200: the superchip and the compute tray

Blackwell changed the server design. Instead of x86 CPUs on a separate tray, **GB200** puts one Grace CPU (the Arm-based CPU introduced in the IP section: 72 Arm Neoverse V2 cores, 480 GB of **LPDDR5X**, the low-power DRAM used in phones, soldered beside it at ~500 GB/s) and two B200 GPUs on one board, the "Bianca" superchip board, connected by **NVLink-C2C** (chip-to-chip, an NVLink variant for a CPU and GPU on the same board) at 900 GB/s so the GPUs can use the CPU's memory as a slow extra tier. The why is memory: over NVLink-C2C the CPU's 480 GB becomes an addressable tier behind each GPU's 192 GB of HBM, with no PCIe hop between them, so a model that does not fit in HBM does not fall off a cliff. A superchip draws on the order of 2,700 W. Two superchips (2 Grace, 4 B200) share one **1U** (one rack unit, 44.45 mm of rack height) liquid-cooled **compute tray**, together with four ConnectX-7/-8 NICs, a BlueField-3, and the cold plates; the tray is built to NVIDIA's **MGX** reference design (NVIDIA's modular server design that ODMs build to) and is physically a slab of copper cold plates and PCB with **quick-disconnect** fittings (couplings that seal both halves when pulled apart, so a tray can be removed without draining the loop) on the back.

Two more reasons than memory capacity drove the design. First, NVLink-C2C makes the CPU and GPU memories **cache-coherent** (either processor can read or write the other's memory and always sees the latest value, with the hardware keeping the copies consistent), which lets a program allocate one address space spanning HBM and LPDDR5X and let the hardware move pages between them, instead of the programmer copying buffers over PCIe by hand; that is what makes the CPU memory a usable tier rather than a separate pool. Second, the ratio changed: one Grace serves two GPUs where an HGX server has two x86 CPUs for eight, and Grace's 72 cores with ~500 GB/s of memory bandwidth are sized to feed the two GPUs' data pipelines and drive the collective operations described under the DGX server, not to compute. The Bianca board is the densest object in the rack: ~2,700 W on one board, with the 900 GB/s C2C link running as a few centimetres of board trace between the CPU package and each GPU package, and each GPU's 18 NVLink ports heading to the tray's rear connectors and on to the spine, all on the same low-loss laminate as the HGX baseboard.

> **Worked example: the compute tray's power and memory tiers.** One tray holds two superchips: 4 × ~1,200 W of GPU plus two Grace CPUs and their memory make ~5,400 W for the two superchips (2 × ~2,700 W); add four NICs, a DPU and the VRM losses of the SXM section's arithmetic and the tray is on the order of 6–7 kW all in (illustrative; NVIDIA does not publish tray power). That is between a third and two-thirds of what a conventional data-center rack draws in total (10–20 kW), in a slot 44.45 mm tall, which is why the tray is a slab of cold plates and why 18 of them make a 120–132 kW rack. Memory: each GPU sees its 192 GB of HBM at 8 TB/s directly, plus a share of Grace's 480 GB over the 900 GB/s C2C link, so a model layer that spills from HBM to LPDDR5X runs at roughly 900 / 8,000 ≈ 11% of HBM bandwidth, slow but not a cliff; over a PCIe Gen5 x16 link (~64 GB/s) the same spill would run at under 1%.

> **What can go wrong.** Signal integrity on the Bianca board: the C2C link and the NVLink ports leaving each GPU run at tens of gigabits per second per lane through board vias and the tray's rear connectors, and a via stub (the unused length of a plated hole beyond the layer it serves, which acts as a tiny resonant antenna) or a laminate batch with a slightly higher loss tangent shows up at board test as a lane that will not train or trains with a marginal eye; the fix is a board respin, and because the packages are soldered directly to Bianca (there is no mezzanine connector to unplug), a board that fails after assembly puts four GPU packages and two CPUs through a costly rework, which is why the bare boards are impedance-tested and the laminate lots controlled before any package is placed. Tray-level leaks: each tray carries its own quick-disconnect pair, and a fitting not fully seated when the tray slides home, or an O-ring nicked on insertion, drips into a tray of electronics; leak-detection rope along the tray floor and pressure monitoring on the rack manifold are standard, and the rack section returns to this. Thermal-interface failure under a bare-die GPU: with the cold plate pressing on silicon, an uneven plate load leaves one corner of the die running hot, seen in system-level test as one GPU throttling while its neighbours do not.

## The NVL72 rack

The **GB200 NVL72** is the unit NVIDIA now sells as "one GPU", and it is the module's central system: the point where a link technology (NVLink), a cable technology (copper) and a cooling technology (water) fix one another's shape. In one rack (an **OCP** **ORv3**-derived design ~2.2 m tall, ~1.36 tonnes fully loaded; OCP is the Open Compute Project, the open hardware-standards body started by Facebook, and Open Rack v3 is its 21-inch rack standard with a vertical DC busbar): 18 compute trays (72 B200 GPUs, 36 Grace CPUs, ~13.4 TB of HBM3E and ~17 TB of LPDDR5X); 9 **NVLink switch trays**, each with two NVLink 5 switch chips (18 chips, 129.6 TB/s of switching); 6 to 8 power shelves at ~33 kW each feeding a DC **busbar** (a bare copper bar down the back of the rack that every tray clips onto for power); and the **NVLink spine**, a **backplane** (fixed wiring at the back of the rack that every tray plugs into) of roughly 5,000 **passive** copper cables (plain wire, with no electronics along it) totaling about 2 miles that connects every GPU's 18 NVLink ports to the switch trays. All 72 GPUs form one **NVLink domain** (the set of GPUs that can read each other's memory directly) with 130 TB/s of aggregate bandwidth, which is the point of the design: a 72-GPU all-to-all **fabric** (network) with no optics.

Copper is why the rack is the shape it is. At 200 Gb/s per lane, passive copper reaches about 1–1.5 m; that is enough to reach from any compute tray to the switch trays in the middle of a single rack, but not to a second rack.

> **Intuition: why copper stops at a metre or so.** Cable loss rises steeply with frequency, for two reasons already met on the baseboard: the skin effect (current crowding into the surface of the conductor) and dielectric loss in the insulation. A 200 Gb/s PAM4 lane needs tens of gigahertz of bandwidth, and at those frequencies a metre of thin cable already eats most of a ~30 dB budget. Thicker cable loses less but 5,000 of them would not fit; a **retimer** chip in the cable (one that cleans up and re-transmits the signal partway along) would extend the reach but costs power, which is what the design is avoiding. So the domain is exactly as big as a metre of copper allows, which is one rack.

NVIDIA has said the copper spine saves ~20 kW per rack versus an optical equivalent (each optical transceiver burns ~15–20 W and the 72 GPUs would need thousands of them) and a large fraction of the cost. The consequence is that NVL72 is power-dense: ~120–132 kW per rack for GB200, ~135–142 kW nominal for **GB300 NVL72** (Blackwell Ultra, 288 GB per GPU, ~20 TB HBM per rack, ~1,400 W per GPU), against a conventional data-center rack of 10–20 kW. There is no way to move that with air.

**Direct liquid cooling** therefore is not optional. Each GPU, CPU, and switch chip sits under a cold plate. Inside the tray, hoses run to a rear **manifold** (a pipe with many branch connections); the tray connects to the rack manifold through **quick disconnects** (Stäubli, CPC, Parker) that seal drip-free when a tray is pulled. The rack manifold runs to a **CDU** (coolant distribution unit, from Vertiv, Motivair (now Schneider Electric), CoolIT, Boyd, and Delta), which is a heat exchanger and pump set that isolates the clean **secondary loop** (the treated water that touches the cold plates, with a corrosion inhibitor, ~25–45 °C supply) from the **facility loop** (the building's own chilled or tower water, which never enters a server). In-row CDUs handle ~1–2 MW; a "sidecar" CDU per rack is also used. About 80–90% of the rack's heat leaves through the liquid; the rest (NICs, **DIMMs**, the CPU's memory modules, and power shelves) still goes to air. A 100 MW building of NVL72s moves ~80–90 MW through water, which is a plumbing project as much as an electrical one. One term for the later worked example: **PUE** (power usage effectiveness) is total facility power divided by the power delivered to the IT equipment, so a PUE of 1.2 means 20% overhead for cooling and distribution.

> **Worked example: coolant flow for one rack.** A GB200 NVL72 at 130 kW with ~85% of its heat going to liquid puts ~110 kW into the water. Water carries 4.18 kJ per kg per °C. Allow the water to warm by ~10 °C between inlet and outlet: flow = 110 / (4.18 × 10) ≈ 2.6 kg/s, which is about 160 L/min per rack, roughly a full bathtub of water through every rack every minute, continuously. For the 770 racks of a 100 MW site that is ~2 m³/s, about two tonnes of water in motion every second. The pumps, manifolds and pipe diameters of an AI data center follow from this one line of arithmetic.

Beyond GB300, NVIDIA's roadmap goes to the **Vera Rubin NVL72** (announced at GTC 2025 as "NVL144", counting the 144 Rubin dies, and renamed at CES 2026 to count the 72 packages, each with two Rubin dies, plus 36 Vera CPUs; second half of 2026; NVLink 6 at 3.6 TB/s per GPU, ~20.7 TB of HBM4 per rack; power reported at ~190–230 kW per rack) and then the **Kyber** rack for Rubin Ultra ("NVL576" at GTC 2025 counting dies, "Kyber NVL144" in 2026 materials counting packages) in the second half of 2027: 144 packages of four reticle dies each, carried on **compute blades** (thin server modules, each the successor of a compute tray, standing on edge like books on a shelf rather than lying flat) rotated vertical in four 18-blade **pods** (groups of blades that mount together as one unit and plug into the rack's midplane, the central board defined under the dies table), ~600 kW per rack, on an 800 V DC power architecture that NVIDIA announced in 2025 to make 1 MW-class racks feasible. The arithmetic behind 800 V: 1 MW at 48 V is ~21,000 A, which no busbar in a rack can carry; at 800 V it is 1,250 A, ~17× less copper for the same power. All of this is roadmap as of the 2025–2026 announcements, and in July 2026 SemiAnalysis reported the Kyber rack slipping to 2028 because the midplane PCB proved hard to manufacture, with a stopgap back-to-back NVL72x2 configuration dropped after customer pushback.

> **What can go wrong.** Leaks: a quick disconnect that drips when a tray is pulled, or a fitting that weeps over months, in a cabinet holding millions of dollars of electronics; leak sensors under the trays are common. Corrosion and biological growth in the secondary loop, which is why the coolant is treated water with inhibitors and biocides rather than tap water, and why the loop is sampled. NVLink lane errors across 5,000 cables: one marginal cable of 5,000 degrades a 72-GPU job, and the burn-in of step 9 in the timeline exists to find it. And rack burn-in fallout generally: a tray that fails under days of full load is swapped, which is what the quick disconnects and the **blind-mate** busbar (one that connects by itself when the tray slides home) are for (Module 18).

## Networking beyond the rack

Inside the rack, NVLink over copper. Between racks, optics, for the reason in the intuition box above: once a link is longer than a metre or so at these speeds, copper cannot carry it. This is the **scale-out network** (the network that connects racks, as opposed to the scale-up NVLink domain inside one): either **InfiniBand** (a low-latency network standard used in supercomputers; NVIDIA Quantum-2 at 400 Gb/s or Quantum-X800 at 800 Gb/s, inherited from Mellanox, the InfiniBand company NVIDIA acquired in 2020) or **Ethernet** (the universal data-center network standard) with NVIDIA's **Spectrum-X** (Spectrum-4 51.2 Tb/s switches with congestion control and adaptive routing tuned for AI traffic). Each GPU's ConnectX NIC connects to a **leaf** switch (the bottom level of the switch tree); a 100,000-GPU cluster uses a three-tier **fat-tree** (a tree of switches in which each level has as much bandwidth upward as downward, so any GPU can talk to any other at full rate) with tens of thousands of switch ports, and every link over a few meters is optical.

The **optical transceivers** (the pluggable modules that turn electrical bits into light and back) are their own supply chain. An **800G** (800 Gb/s) **OSFP** module (a pluggable form factor standard) holds 8 lanes of 100 Gb/s (or 4 × 200 Gb/s for 1.6T), each driven by an **EML** (electro-absorption modulated laser, an **indium-phosphide** chip; InP is a compound semiconductor that, unlike silicon, emits light, Module 04; from Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Mitsubishi Electric, or Sumitomo) or, increasingly, by a **silicon-photonics** modulator (silicon cannot emit light but can switch it, so an external **continuous-wave** (steadily on, not pulsed) InP laser supplies the light and a silicon chip modulates it), plus a **DSP** (a digital signal-processing chip that recovers the bits from the distorted received waveform; Marvell, Broadcom) that recovers the PAM4 signal. The modules are assembled by Innolight, Eoptolink, and Coherent (the three leaders), plus Accelink, Hisense, and others, largely in China and Thailand. A single NVL72-scale cluster of 100,000 GPUs consumes on the order of hundreds of thousands of 800G transceivers, and the 1.6T generation ramped alongside ConnectX-8 and Quantum-X800 in 2025–26. NVIDIA's answer to the transceiver power and cost is **co-packaged optics** (**CPO**, putting the optical engine on the switch chip's own package instead of in a pluggable module): the Quantum-X and Spectrum-X Photonics switches announced at GTC 2025 put silicon-photonics engines (built with TSMC's **COUPE** process, its process for stacking a photonic die on an electronic die) on the switch package, replacing pluggable modules on the switch side, with Quantum-X Photonics slated for late 2025 and Spectrum-X Photonics for 2026 (as announced at GTC 2025). GPUs themselves still use copper to the NIC.

The numbers that size this network. Reach: the 800G transceivers used inside a building are **DR** or **FR** parts (the standard names for parallel single-mode-fiber links to 500 m and for links to 2 km respectively), and 500 m covers any two racks in one hall; the copper alternatives are a **DAC** (direct-attach copper cable) which at 800G reaches ~2–3 m, and an **AEC** (active electrical cable, a DAC with retimer chips in each end) at ~5–7 m, which is why leaf switches sit at the top of the rack or in the row and everything beyond is fiber. Power: an 800G pluggable draws ~15–20 W and a 1.6T part reportedly ~25–30 W, most of it in the DSP. Cost: 800G modules sold for on the order of $1,000 each in 2024–25 depending on reach and volume, with 1.6T at a premium (market figures, and they fell through the period).

> **Worked example: transceivers for a 100,000-GPU cluster.** Take a three-tier fat-tree with 800G links and full bandwidth at every level. Each GPU's NIC has one 800G link to a leaf switch: 100,000 links, each with a transceiver at both ends, 200,000 modules. A non-blocking fat-tree needs as much bandwidth from the leaf tier up to the spine tier as down to the GPUs, and again from the spine tier up to the core, so each tier above adds another ~100,000 links, or ~200,000 more modules per tier: two upper tiers give ~600,000 transceivers in total, before the storage and management networks. At ~15 W each that is ~9 MW of transceiver power, ~7–8% of the ~120 MW the GPUs themselves draw at ~1.2 kW each; at ~$1,000 each it is on the order of $600 million of optics, a few percent of the GPUs' price. Co-packaged optics on the switches removes the switch-side module of every link, so in this model the switch-to-switch links lose both pluggables and only the ~100,000 NIC-side modules remain; NVIDIA states ~3.5× better link power efficiency for its CPO switches (an announcement figure). This is the arithmetic behind "hundreds of thousands of 800G transceivers" and behind CPO. All values illustrative.

> **What can go wrong.** Transceiver failure: pluggable optics fail in the field at a rate on the order of a fraction of a percent per module per year (a laser that dims, a DSP that overheats, dust on a fiber face), and with ~600,000 modules that is thousands of failures a year, each of which takes a link down; large operators keep spares at the rack and design the training software to survive a lost link. **Link flaps** (a link that repeatedly drops and re-trains, from a marginal module, a dirty connector or a fiber bent past its minimum radius) are worse than a dead link, because every flap forces a route recalculation and stalls every collective operation in flight; NIC and switch firmware count corrected and uncorrected errors per lane so that a flapping link can be found and shut down. DSP power and heat: at 15–30 W a module in a 64-port switch face plate is a cooling problem in its own right, and a transceiver that runs hot shortens its laser's life, which is part of the case for CPO. And fiber-plant errors: ~300,000 links and ~600,000 module faces, each terminating several fibers, mean millions of individual fiber ends that must be clean, correctly keyed and within loss budget, so every link is checked with an optical power meter at installation and a mis-patched link shows as a topology error before the first job runs.

## Who builds it

NVIDIA designs the GPU, the switch chips, the NICs, the SXM module, the HGX baseboard, the Bianca board, the compute and switch trays, and the rack, and publishes all of them as reference designs (it contributed the NVL72 designs to the Open Compute Project in 2024). It manufactures none of them. Two words already met carry the industry's structure, and their relationship is the point: ODMs build servers and racks to NVIDIA's or the customer's design, often unbranded, and sell them to hyperscalers or to OEMs; OEMs sell under their own brand and warranty to enterprises, sourcing the boards from NVIDIA and often the manufacturing from an ODM. The board-level and system-level builders are the Taiwanese ODMs: **Foxconn** (Hon Hai, including its Ingrasys server unit, the largest builder of NVL72 racks), **Quanta** (QCT), **Wistron** and its spinoff **Wiwynn**, **Inventec**, and **Pegatron**. **Supermicro** is a hybrid, designing and building its own chassis around HGX and MGX. **Dell**, **HPE**, and **Lenovo** are OEMs that sell to enterprises under their own brands, sourcing boards from NVIDIA and manufacturing from the ODMs. ZT Systems, a major hyperscaler ODM, was bought by AMD in 2024, with its manufacturing arm sold to Sanmina in 2025.

Geography shifted fast. Through 2023, most of this was assembled in Taiwan. By 2024–25, Foxconn's Guadalajara plant in Mexico was reported to be the largest GB200 assembly site, and in April 2025 NVIDIA announced that Foxconn (Houston) and Wistron (Dallas) would build Blackwell and Rubin systems in Texas, with TSMC Arizona wafers, Amkor and SPIL packaging in Arizona, as part of a stated plan for up to $500 billion of US-built AI infrastructure over four years. As of 2026, the split is roughly Taiwan for boards and early production, Mexico and the US for high-volume rack integration for US hyperscalers, with tariff policy as the variable.

## The bill of materials of an H100: where the cost goes

The last group of sections is the economics, and it begins with a question that sounds like accounting and is actually about supply: who captures the value of a scarce product, and which of its inputs therefore governs how many can be built. A small-cost input can stop the whole assembly line, so cost shares alone cannot identify the limiting capacity. Two terms first. **COGS** (cost of goods sold) is the accounting cost recognized for products sold; it can include manufacturing overhead, inventory charges and other costs beyond the **BOM** (bill of materials), an itemized list of components. The estimates below model parts and manufacturing services rather than NVIDIA's reported product-level COGS. Public estimates of these costs come from teardowns, published wafer and HBM prices, and yield assumptions; nobody outside NVIDIA and its suppliers has the real invoices, so treat every figure as a range.

Public estimates of the H100 SXM's manufacturing cost (SemiAnalysis and others, 2023–24) cluster around $3,000–3,500. The approximate decomposition:

| Item | Cost (2023–24 estimate) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| GPU die | ~$300–400 | ~$16–17k N4 wafer, ~45 sellable die |
| HBM3, 5 stacks, 80 GB | ~$1,300–2,000 | ~$15–25/GB in 2023–24; HBM was the scarcest input |
| CoWoS-S interposer, assembly, ABF substrate | ~$500–1,000 | interposer ~2.5 reticles; substrate ~$100–200 |
| Wafer sort, final test, burn-in, SLT | ~$100–200 | test time on V93000 class ATE |
| SXM board, VRM, passives, heatsink | ~$200–400 | |
| Estimated manufacturing bill | ~$3,000–3,500 | vs. ~$25,000–30,000 selling price |

Note what this says: the 814 mm² die that consumes the entire front half of this course is ~10% of the cost of the product; memory is ~40–50%; packaging and test ~20–25%. For B200 the same structure at roughly double: two dies (~$800–1,000), eight HBM3E stacks (192 GB at ~$15–20/GB is ~$3,000–3,500), CoWoS-L (~$1,500–2,000, since a three-reticle-plus interposer with embedded bridges yields worse and TSMC charges more), and a larger substrate, for a COGS on the order of $6,000–8,000 against a ~$30,000–40,000 price. A full GB200 superchip has been estimated at ~$14,000 COGS. The rack: an NVL72 at ~$3–4 million contains ~$500–600k of GPU COGS at these figures, and the rest of the price is NVIDIA's margin on the GPUs plus the CPUs, switches, NICs, boards, cold plates, and ODM assembly at their own margins.

## NVIDIA's margin, TSMC's revenue, and the allocation game

If the product is scarce and the design company sets the price, the question becomes how the profit is split between the company that designed the chip and the companies that made it, and the split explains why the whole chain behaves as it does. **Gross margin** is revenue minus COGS, as a share of revenue. NVIDIA's company-level gross margin ran ~75% in fiscal 2025 and ~70–75% through the Blackwell ramp (CoWoS-L and HBM3E are more expensive per package than the Hopper generation, and early Blackwell yields were lower). Comparing an estimated H100 manufacturing bill with an observed resale price produces a large price-minus-estimate spread, but it does not establish NVIDIA's H100 gross margin: the realized selling price, channel margins and full recognized COGS are not disclosed at this level. TSMC's revenue from NVIDIA rose from a few percent in 2022 to 12% in 2024 and 19% in 2025 (per TSMC's annual-report customer disclosures as reported in January 2026), overtaking Apple (17%) as TSMC's largest customer. Even so, TSMC captures on the order of $2,000–3,000 of an $8,000 B200 COGS (wafers plus CoWoS), while the remaining observed price includes the designer's costs and margin as well as any channel margin. This asymmetry is why, in the foundry model, the profit above a normal return flows to the design company when the product is scarce and to the foundry when it is not.

The **allocation politics** of 2023–26 were fought over two inputs. **CoWoS**: TSMC's capacity was ~10–15k wafers per month in 2023, ~35k at end-2024, ~75–80k at end-2025, with a target of ~120–130k for end-2026, and NVIDIA held roughly half or more of it throughout, with AMD, Broadcom, and the hyperscalers' custom **ASICs** (application-specific integrated circuits, chips designed for one customer's own workload: Google TPU, Amazon Trainium) competing for the rest. A B200-class package needs ~3.3 reticles of interposer (3.3 × 858 mm² ≈ 2,800 mm² per package; the dies-per-wafer formula from the timeline section gives ~12–16 such packages on a 300 mm wafer, depending on the package's aspect ratio and how the rectangles tile the circle), so a CoWoS wafer yields only ~16 packages; 1 million B200s is ~60,000 CoWoS wafers, roughly two months of end-2024 capacity or ~15–20% of a full 2024 year. NVIDIA prepaid, took capacity commitments, and pushed TSMC to qualify ASE/SPIL and Amkor for the wafer-on-substrate step to free CoW capacity at TSMC. **HBM**: SK hynix supplied essentially all H100 HBM3 in 2023 and the majority of HBM3E for Blackwell; Micron qualified HBM3E in 2024 and reached ~20%+ share by 2025–26; Samsung's HBM3E qualification at NVIDIA lagged until September 2025, when its 12-high HBM3E finally passed. HBM is ~5% of DRAM bits but ~30–40% of DRAM revenue, and every HBM wafer displaces ~3 wafers of conventional DRAM bits, which is why DRAM prices for everyone else rose in 2025–26.

> **Worked example: wafers and HBM for one million B200 GPUs.**
> Logic: 1 million packages × 2 dies = 2 million dies at ~800 mm² each. From the die-per-wafer formula, ~63 gross die per 300 mm wafer; with harvesting, ~70% sellable, so ~44 good die per wafer, and 2,000,000 / 44 ≈ 45,000 N4P wafers. TSMC's N5-family capacity is on the order of 150,000 wafers per month, so a year's worth of B200 dies is about ten days of fab output. The wafer fab is not the constraint.
> HBM: 8 stacks per GPU = 8 million HBM3E stacks. Each 24 GB 8-high stack is eight 24 Gb (3 GB) DRAM dies plus a base die, so 64 million DRAM dies. A 24 Gb HBM3E die on a 1β-class node (DRAM generations are named 1α, 1β, 1γ rather than in nanometres; 1β is the 2023–25 generation, roughly 12–14 nm-class, Module 15) is reportedly ~110–120 mm²; at ~500 gross die per wafer and ~70% net yield (HBM DRAM dies are also tested and thinned, with TSV yield loss on top), call it ~350 good die per wafer, so ~185,000 DRAM wafers, plus base-die wafers and stacking loss. That is roughly 15,000 wafers per month for this one SKU, about 1% of the ~1.5 million-plus DRAM wafers per month the three vendors start (~2 million industry-wide). Add H200, B300, AMD and the custom-ASIC customers, and at ~3× the wafer area per bit of commodity DRAM, HBM as a whole reportedly took ~30% of SK hynix's DRAM wafer starts by 2025, which is why HBM crowded out commodity DRAM in 2025–26.
> CoWoS-L: at ~16 packages per interposer wafer and ~90% assembly yield, 1,000,000 / 14.4 ≈ 70,000 CoWoS wafers, or about 6,000 per month. Against ~35,000 wafers per month of total CoWoS capacity at end-2024, one million B200s would have been ~17% of a year's global advanced-packaging capacity for a single SKU. This comparison illustrates why CoWoS could constrain the ramp; it does not prove the relevant front-end node, HBM supply or allocated test capacity had spare capacity.

> **What can go wrong: allocation.** In 2023 the 52-week queue produced double-ordering, with customers placing the same demand through several OEMs and clouds at once, and a grey market in which H100 servers changed hands at multiples of list price; when the queue shortened, the phantom demand vanished and the order books had to be read again. In 2024 the Blackwell design flaw shifted roughly a quarter of volume, which for the HBM makers and CoWoS meant a quarter of committed capacity with nothing to fill it, then a rush. And HBM's crowd-out of commodity DRAM raised memory prices for every PC and phone maker in 2025–26, which is how a decision at one design company reaches products that contain no NVIDIA part at all.

## The supplier tree

The point of listing the suppliers is to see how few there are at each layer. Reading upward from the rack, the dependencies are:

- **Logic wafers:** TSMC only (Fab 18 Tainan Phases 1–4 for 4N/4NP; Fab 18 Phases 5–8 for N3; Fab 20 Hsinchu and Fab 22 Kaohsiung are N2, not N3; TSMC Arizona Fab 21 for N4 from 2025). No second source exists for the GPU die; Samsung built earlier NVIDIA consumer parts (Ampere GeForce on Samsung's 8 nm-class 8LPP) but not the data-center line.
- **HBM:** SK hynix (leader), Micron, Samsung.
- **Advanced packaging:** TSMC CoWoS (nearly all); ASE/SPIL and Amkor for wafer-on-substrate and overflow; Amkor Arizona (Peoria) from 2028.
- **Substrates:** Ibiden, Unimicron, with Shinko, AT&S, Kinsus; ABF film from Ajinomoto; glass cloth from Nittobo; copper foil from Mitsui.
- **Test:** Advantest (V93000 EXA) testers, FormFactor probe cards, KYEC and ASE test services.
- **EDA and IP:** Synopsys, Cadence, Siemens (Calibre, Tessent); Ansys; Arm.
- **Masks and lithography compute:** TSMC in-house; IMS writers; Hoya/AGC blanks; NVIDIA's own cuLitho.
- **Module and board:** MPS, Infineon, Renesas, onsemi (power stages); Murata, Samsung Electro-Mechanics, TDK (MLCCs); Amphenol, TE (connectors); Panasonic, EMC, Isola (laminates); Unimicron, Ibiden, WUS, Victory Giant, GCE, TTM (PCBs); Auras, AVC, Delta, CoolIT, Boyd (thermal).
- **Rack:** Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron/Wiwynn, Inventec, Supermicro (build); Dell, HPE, Lenovo (OEM); Vertiv, Schneider (Motivair), CoolIT, Delta (CDUs and power); Stäubli, CPC, Parker (quick disconnects); Amphenol, Luxshare, Molex (NVLink spine cables).
- **Network:** NVIDIA (switches, NICs); Innolight, Eoptolink, Coherent (transceivers); Lumentum, Coherent, Broadcom, Sumitomo (InP lasers); Marvell, Broadcom (DSPs); Broadcom (PCIe switches); Corning (fiber).
- **CPU:** Arm (IP), Intel and AMD (x86 for HGX); Grace and Vera built at TSMC.

Every one of these is one to three companies deep. The ones with genuinely no substitute on a two-year horizon are TSMC (wafers and CoWoS), the three HBM makers as a group, Ajinomoto (ABF), and ASML upstream of all of them.

> **Worked example: NVL72 racks per 100 MW.**
> A GB200 NVL72 rack draws ~120–132 kW at full load; take 130 kW. A data center with 100 MW of IT power (excluding cooling and distribution losses, i.e., before PUE) holds 100,000 / 130 ≈ 770 racks, or ~55,000 GPUs and ~28,000 Grace CPUs. If the 100 MW is the utility feed rather than IT power, at a PUE of ~1.2 for a liquid-cooled facility, the IT budget is ~83 MW and the count falls to ~640 racks, ~46,000 GPUs. At ~$3.5 million per rack that is ~$2.2–2.7 billion of racks alone before the network, storage, building, and power infrastructure, which at ~$30–50 million per MW all-in adds another $3–5 billion. Move to GB300 NVL72 at ~140 kW and the GPU count per 100 MW falls ~7% while the FP4 throughput per rack rises ~50%; move to the roadmap Kyber rack at 600 kW and the same 100 MW holds ~140–165 racks of 576 Rubin Ultra dies each. The power per rack is rising far faster than the power per site, which is why 2026-era data-center design is a story of 800 V DC distribution and liquid cooling rather than of more floor space.

## Summary

- NVIDIA designs the GPU, the switches, the boards and the rack and manufactures none of them; its only factory is an emulation and compute farm, and its product is a design plus reference systems that TSMC, the HBM makers, OSATs and ODMs turn into hardware.
- A data-center GPU is ~150 copies of one streaming multiprocessor plus a memory system; the RTL is a few million lines of SystemVerilog instantiated hundreds of times, not 80 billion hand-placed transistors.
- Verification dominates the schedule: simulation runs at cycles per second, emulation at megahertz, and a base-layer respin costs a ~$20–30M mask set plus three months, so NVIDIA runs data-center-scale Palladium and ZeBu farms.
- Physical design turns the netlist into ~80 mask layers through synthesis, floorplanning, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing, static timing, power signoff, DRC/LVS and DFT; every step is a fight with a 0.55 ns clock period, ~900 A of supply current and thousands of manufacturing rules.
- "4N" is a custom N5-family flow; the PDK, the cell libraries and pre-silicon IP test chips on MPW shuttles are the contract between NVIDIA and TSMC.
- Tape-out sends terabytes of OPC-corrected data to the mask shop; a hot lot returns first packaged silicon in ~3 months; steppings go A0, A1, B0, and the 2024 Blackwell design flaw shows one metal-level respin costs about a production quarter.
- Harvesting (144 SMs built, 132 shipped; 6 HBM sites, 5 used) turns a ~45% perfect-die yield into ~65–80% sellable dies at ~$350 each, about 10% of the H100's cost.
- Wafer start to a GPU on a board is ~6 months and to a burned-in rack ~7 or more; the 52-week lead time of 2023 was queueing for CoWoS and HBM allocation, not processing.
- The SXM module is a 1,250 A power-delivery and 1 kW cooling problem; the HGX baseboard is a 20–30-layer low-loss PCB carrying 144 NVLink ports to the switch chips.
- NVL72 puts 72 GPUs in one 130 TB/s NVLink domain over ~5,000 passive copper cables because copper only reaches ~1.5 m at 200 Gb/s; that confines the domain to one 120–140 kW rack that can only be cooled with water (~160 L/min per rack).
- Optics begin at the NIC: scale-out is InfiniBand or Spectrum-X Ethernet, and a 100,000-GPU cluster consumes hundreds of thousands of 800G/1.6T transceivers.
- HBM is ~40–50% of an H100's cost, CoWoS and substrate ~20–25%, the die ~10%; NVIDIA's ~75% gross margin and TSMC's ~19% revenue share from NVIDIA show where the value goes, and reported CoWoS and HBM shortages illustrate why downstream capacity matters alongside qualified front-end supply.
- Every input is one to three companies deep; TSMC (wafers and CoWoS), the three HBM makers as a group, Ajinomoto (ABF) and ASML have no substitute on a two-year horizon.

## Common Misconceptions

- **"NVIDIA makes chips."** → NVIDIA designs chips and systems and owns the architecture, RTL, software, and reference designs; every physical object, from the wafer to the rack, is made by TSMC, the HBM vendors, the OSATs, the substrate makers, and the ODMs. NVIDIA's only "factory" is its emulation and compute farm.
- **"The 80-billion-transistor design is hand-built."** → It is a few million lines of parameterized RTL describing one SM, one L2 slice, one PHY, instantiated by the tools hundreds of times and then placed and routed hierarchically by Synopsys and Cadence software. What is hand-tuned is the datapath, the custom cells, and the clock and power grids.
- **"Yield on an 814 mm² die must be terrible, so the die is the expensive part."** → Perfect-die yield is indeed under 50%, but 12 spare SMs and a spare HBM controller make ~65–80% of dies sellable; the die ends up at ~$350, about 10% of a ~$3,300 BOM, with HBM at ~40–50%.
- **"A 52-week lead time means 52 weeks of manufacturing."** → Physical cycle time from wafer start to a GPU on a board is ~6 months. The rest of a 2023 lead time was queueing for CoWoS and HBM allocation and the ~12-month capacity commitments the chain requires.
- **"NVL72 uses optics because it is so fast."** → It is the opposite: the 130 TB/s NVLink domain is all passive copper (~5,000 cables) precisely to avoid the ~20 kW and cost of thousands of transceivers, and the ~1.5 m reach of 200 Gb/s copper is what confines the domain to one rack. Optics begin at the scale-out NIC.
- **"Liquid cooling is a choice for efficiency."** → At 120–140 kW per rack (and 600 kW on the Kyber roadmap) there is no air-cooling design that works; direct-to-chip cold plates and CDUs are a requirement, not an optimization.
- **"4N is a 4 nm node."** → It is NVIDIA's customized flow on TSMC's N5 family, with its own rule deck and library characterization; the transistor is TSMC's, and the name is as much a statement about committed capacity as about geometry.
- **"Emulation replaces testing."** → Emulation validates the design once, before silicon; wafer sort, final test and system-level test validate every individual die and package for manufacturing defects that no emulator can see (Modules 14, 18).

## Where This Fits in the Supply Chain

This module consumes everything the previous eighteen produced: the polished wafers of Module 03 patterned through Modules 05–12 with the masks of Module 04, sorted and harvested in Modules 13–14, joined to the HBM stacks of Module 15 on the CoWoS interposers and ABF substrates of Modules 16–17, and tested in Module 18. It adds the two things no fab supplies: the design (RTL through GDS, verified on emulators and closed with EDA tools against TSMC's PDK) and the system (module, baseboard, tray, rack, cooling, and network), built by ODMs from a supplier tree of several hundred companies. Its output is the finished product: an HGX server or an NVL72 rack shipped to a hyperscaler, a neocloud (a cloud provider built specifically to rent out GPUs), or an enterprise, at $300k to $4M per unit. Module 20 steps back to ask what this chain costs, where it is concentrated, who can and cannot replicate it, and what a single disruption at TSMC, at the HBM makers, or in the Taiwan Strait would do to the numbers in this module.
